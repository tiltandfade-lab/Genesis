# Lane 6 — Belief and festival

**Nothing here is locked. Adam rules; this file evidences.**

## 6.0 The one-sentence result

Religion in a pre-industrial town was **a claim on the streets, funded by the crafts, and
enforced by the corporation** — the procession is the same machine as the punishment
procession and the marching watch (a routed public walk that declares who ranks where) — and
the interesting failure mode, richly documented, is that **towns kept having to compel their
own citizens to pay for the festivals historians assume they loved.**

---

## 6.1 The parish church is not only a church

Green's inventory of what a fifteenth-century town kept in its parish church makes the point
that the building is a civic organ (Green, *Town Life in the Fifteenth Century* I, PG 50392):

> "All the multitudinous activities and accidents of this common life were summed up for the
> people in **the parish church that stood in their market-place, close to the Common House or
> Guild Hall. This was the fortress of the borough against its enemies — its place of safety
> where the treasure of the commons was stored in dangerous times, the arms in the steeple,
> the wealth of corn or wool or precious goods in the church itself, guarded by a sentence of
> excommunication against all who should violate so sacred a protection.**"

So the church in the market-place is: treasury · armoury (in the steeple) · warehouse for corn
and wool · fire station (lane 5 §5.6) · bell-tower and civic clock (lane 1 §1.1) · sanctuary
(lane 4 §4.6) · and the town's seating chart of rank (lane 4 §4.5). Green adds that "a small
fee was sometimes paid to the parson when the church was used as storehouse for grain or wool,
as in case of Southampton."

**The townspeople ran it.** "They were frequently the lay rectors; they appointed the wardens
and churchwardens; they had control of the funds, and the administration of lands left for
maintaining its services and fabric; sometimes they laid claim to the fees paid for masses. The
popular interest might even extend to the criticism and discipline of the rector" (Green I, PG
50392).

**And they traded in it.** At fair time the traders "expected to be allowed to overflow from
the High Street into the cathedral precincts, and were 'ever wont and used… to lay open, buy and
sell divers merchandises **in the said church and cemetery** and special in the king's highway
there as at Wells, Salisbury and other places more, as dishes, bowls, and other things like, and
in the said church ornaments for the same and other jewels convenient thereto.'" A 1427 royal
order forbade fairs and markets in sanctuaries "for the honour of Holy Church," and Green's dry
comment is the finding: "such an order was little in harmony with the habits and customs of the
age; and if there was an occasional stirring of conscience in the matter, **it was not till the
time of Laud that the public attained to a conviction… that the church was desecrated by the
transaction in it of common business**" (Green I, PG 50392). And St Bartholomew's fair booths
stood "within the churchyard of this priory, closed in with walls, and gates locked every night,
and watched" (Stow, PG 42959; lane 1 §1.7).

**Design translation (proposal only):** for site 10, *the market and the temple are not
adjacent uses — they interpenetrate*. Stalls in the churchyard, goods stored in the nave, the
fair's gate locked at night on consecrated ground. That is a stronger and better-evidenced
relationship than "temple beside plaza."

## 6.2 Church ales — the town raises money by closing the taverns

Plymouth's method of paying for a new aisle at St Andrew's is a complete, costed, stageable
civic event (Green I, PG 50392):

> "the town authorities decided that the money should be collected by means of a yearly
> '**church-ale**.' **Taverns were closed by order of the council on a certain day**, and
> **every ward of the town made for itself a 'hale' or booth in the cemetery of the parish
> church**. All inhabitants of the wards were commanded to come with as many friends and
> acquaintances as possible '**for the increasing of the said ale**,' and to bring with them
> 'except bread and drink such victual as they like best'; **but they must buy at the 'hale'
> 'bread and ale as it cometh thereto for their dinners and suppers the same day.'** After ten
> years of these picnics in the churchyard the new aisle of St. Andrews was finished at **a
> cost of £44 14s. 6d.**"

Every clause is a mechanic: **the competition is shut down by order** · **each ward builds its
own booth in the graveyard** · attendance is commanded and recruitment is expected · you may
bring your own food but **must buy the drink** · and it takes **ten annual iterations** to fund
one aisle. A town that does this has a named annual day when the taverns are dark and everyone
is in the cemetery.

## 6.3 Guilds, pageants, and the play as a compulsory tax

**The Corpus Christi guild is a political body.** Green: "the Corpus Christi Guild… evidently
played a political part in the life of every great town. In York it is said to have had in the
sixteenth century **nearly fifteen thousand members**, and at its great pageant, the Mayor and
Town Council 'and other worshipful persons' joined in a common feast, and sent wine and fruits at
the public expense to great nobles and ladies in the city, **till perhaps supplies ran out and
the town was 'drunken dry'**" (Green I, PG 50392).

**The great fraternity's turnout**, at Norwich: the Guild of St George, "whose Alderman in
scarlet robe followed by **the four hundred members with their distinguishing red hoods**,
marched after **the sword of wood with a Dragon's head for the handle** which had been presented
to them by Henry the Fifth" (Green I, PG 50392). Four hundred men in red hoods behind a wooden
sword whose handle is a dragon's head, a royal gift. **A faction with a uniform and a relic.**

**The production values are documented in shillings and pence.** The Canterbury accounts (Green
I, PG 50392) list: "knights' armour, the hire of a sword, the painting of S. Thomas' head, **an
angel which cost 22d., and flapped his wings as he turned every way on a hidden wynch with wheels
oiled with soap**. When all was over the properties of the pageant were put away **in the barn at
S. Sepulchre's Nunnery, and kept safely till the next year at a charge of 16d.**" And for the
*Three Kings of Cologne*, performed at the Town Hall: the kings and their henchmen "appeared
decorated with strips of silver and gold paper and wearing monks' frocks. **The three 'beasts'
for the Magi were made out of twelve ells of canvas distended with hoops and laths, and 'painted
after nature'**; and there was a castle of painted canvas which cost 3s. 4d. The artist and his
helpers worked for six days and nights at these preparations and charged three shillings for
their labour, food, fire and candle."

**A mechanical angel on a soaped winch; three canvas-and-hoop beasts painted after nature; a
canvas castle; a props barn rented for 16d a year.** That is a whole prop department, with a
storage location and an annual rent — and it is a *set of objects that exists in the town all
year round, in a barn at a nunnery*, which is a better hook than the pageant itself.

**And the crafts increasingly resented paying for it.** This is Green's most useful corrective,
and she states it against the received view:

> "It has been commonly supposed that the English people had in the later middle ages a passion
> for pageantry and display… **But towards the end of the fifteenth century at least it becomes
> less and less clear that the freewill of the craftsmen had much to say to the maintenance of
> these public gaieties, or that they felt any enthusiasm for amusements which yearly grew more
> expensive and burdensome.**"

The Canterbury complaint of 1490 is the evidence: the Corpus Christi Play, the City Watch on St
Thomas's Eve, and the Pageant of St Thomas had "fallen into decay." Some mayors "in their year
have full honourably kept the said watch," others had neglected it, "**and 'all manner of harness
within the city is decayed and rusted for lack of the yearly watch.'**" Remedy: every mayor must
henceforth keep the watch, and poor crafts must incorporate or be merged (lane 2 §2.7). Plymouth's
tailors on incorporation in 1496 "had to bind themselves to provide a pageant every year on
Corpus Christi Day"; York's hostellers contracted in 1483 "to bring forth yearly for the next
eight years a pageant of their own, *The Coronation of Our Lady*" (Green I, PG 50392 and note 282).

**The armour rusts for want of the yearly watch.** That sentence alone justifies the whole
finding: the festival was the *maintenance regime* for the town's military kit. Skip the parade
and the town's harness corrodes.

**Some towns went the other way and legislated against entertainment.** "the Mayor and Sheriffs
of Norwich petitioned the Lords and Commons to pass an Act or Order to prevent Players of
Interludes from coming into the city, as **they took so large a share of the earnings of the poor
operatives as to cause great want to their families**, and a heavy charge to the city," and
Bridgnorth obtained an order from Elizabeth "that the town might no longer pay players or
bear-wards; whoever wanted to see such things must see them '**upon their own costs and
charges**'" (Green I, PG 50392).

**Who lobbied to keep the festivals alive is the twist.** Green: "the town authorities were
probably acting under pressure of the publicans, and licensed victuallers. **Cooks and brewers
and hostellers were naturally deeply interested in the preservation of the good old customs**,
and it was in some cases certainly this class, the most powerful in a mediæval borough, who
raised the protest against the indifference and neglect of the townspeople for public processions
and merry-making, because '**thereby the victuallers lose their money**.'" **The victuallers'
lobby demands the festival because the festival is their trade.** That is a faction motive with
no fantasy in it at all.

Green's conclusion is worth keeping as a tone note: "It was not before the fanaticism of religion,
but **before the coming in of new forms of poverty and of bondage** that the old games and
pageants lost their lustre and faded out of existence, save where a mockery of life was preserved
to them by compulsion of the town authorities."

## 6.4 The procession claims the street

**Corpus Christi** falls "on the Thursday after Trinity Sunday"; the consecrated host is carried
in a monstrance "surrounded by a Corpus Christi wreath of flowers"; the route has **"stations"**
where "the Blessed Sacrament is put on an altar table" for readings and hymns; houses are
"decorated with little birch trees and green boughs" with "candles being lit in the windows," and
wreaths "attached to flags and banners, to houses, and to the arches of green boughs that span the
streets." Mystery plays attached to the feast: York's cycle "were performed on Corpus Christi Day
for some 200 years" (Wikipedia, *Corpus Christi (feast)*).

**A procession has a route, stations, and street furniture built for it — arches of green boughs
spanning the street, altars set up at named points, lights in every window.** That is temporary
architecture, and it is exactly the sort of thing a dressing pass can roll on.

**Rushes on the route.** "The strewing of rushes in the way where processions were to pass is
attributed by our poets to all times and countries. Thus at the coronation of Henry V, when the
procession is coming, the grooms cry — '**More rushes, more rushes**'" (Hindley, PG 37114; lane 2
§2.5).

**Bruges' Procession of the Holy Blood** is the fullest single festival-day arc in the corpus, and
it is a *two-act* structure worth reproducing (Omond, *Bruges and West Flanders*, PG 18670):

*Act one, the morning.* The countryside empties into the town — "For some days before and after
the sacred festival the villagers are in the habit of coming into Bruges — whole families of them,
father and mother, sons and daughters, all in their best finery. They walk through the streets,
**following the route by which the Holy Blood is carried**, telling their beads… **crossing
themselves, and kneeling at any image of Christ, or Madonna, or saint, which they may notice at
the street corners.**" The bishop "walks with fingers raised, scattering benedictions from side to
side." At the climax, in the Bourg, "**The relic is taken out of the chasse, and a priest, standing
on the steps of the altar high above the crowd, holds it up to be worshipped. Everyone bows low,
and then, in dead silence, the mysterious object is carried into the chapel.**"

*Act two, the night.* "**The curtain has fallen upon the drama of the day. The flags are furled and
put aside. The vestments are in the sacristy. Shrines, canopies, censers, all the objects carried
in the procession, have disappeared into the churches. The church doors are locked, and the images
are left to stand all night without so much as one solitary worshipper kneeling before them. The
Bourg is empty and dark.**" Meanwhile, across the canal by the fish-market, "**Every second house,
if not every house, is a cafe, 'herberg,' or 'estaminet,' with a bar and sanded floor**… and on the
night of the Procession of the Holy Blood they are crowded to the doors." The description that
follows is unsparing — dancing to jingling instruments in a fetid room, "the hot air heavy with the
smell of decayed fish," rosaries still clutched, half-tipsy country lads, crying babies — and then
"soon the lights are out, the shutters are put up, the last customer goes staggering homewards, and
**the Belfry speaks again**."

**The two-act shape is the finding**: the sacred quarter goes dark and empty at exactly the hour
the drinking quarter fills to the doors, and the bell closes both. A single festival day therefore
produces *two* completely different site occupancies four hours apart, in two different districts.
*(Honesty flag: Omond is writing c. 1905 and his account of the night is coloured by evident
distaste; the structure is reliable, the moralising is his.)*

**Street-corner images** as a fixture class — "any image of Christ, or Madonna, or saint, which
they may notice at the street corners" — is worth logging on its own: the town is studded with
small devotional objects at junctions, which people stop at, kneel at, and presumably maintain.

## 6.5 Sport, misrule and the year's set-pieces

**Chester, Shrove Tuesday** (Green I, PG 50392): "the drapers, saddlers, shoemakers and many
others met at the cross on the Roodeye, and there **in the presence of the Mayor the shoemakers
gave to the drapers a football of leather 'to play at from thence to the Common Hall.'** The
saddlers at the same time gave '**every master of them a painted ball of wood with flowers and
arms upon the point of a spear, being goodly arrayed upon horseback accordingly**.' The whole town
joined in the sports, and **everyone married within the year gave some contribution toward their
funds**."

Three mechanics: **one craft ceremonially gives another craft a football**, to be played *from a
named cross to a named hall* (a route, again); the saddlers give painted wooden balls **mounted on
spear points** to be carried on horseback; and **the newly married of the year pay for it**.

**Midsummer**, from Stow, is the biggest set-piece in the study and belongs in this lane as much
as in lane 1 (Stow, PG 42959):

> "In the months of June and July, on the vigils of festival days, and on the same festival days
> in the evenings after the sun setting, there were usually made **bonfires in the streets, every
> man bestowing wood or labour towards them; the wealthier sort also, before their doors near to
> the said bonfires, would set out tables on the vigils, furnished with sweet bread and good
> drink**, and on the festival days with meats and drinks plentifully, whereunto **they would
> invite their neighbours and passengers also to sit and be merry with them in great familiarity**…
> **These were called bonfires as well of good amity amongst neighbours that being before at
> controversy, were there, by the labour of others, reconciled, and made of bitter enemies loving
> friends**; and also for the virtue that a great fire hath to purge the infection of the air.
> **On the vigil of St. John the Baptist, and on St. Peter and Paul the apostles, every man's door
> being shadowed with green birch, long fennel, St. John's wort, orpin, white lilies, and such
> like, garnished upon with garlands of beautiful flowers, had also lamps of glass, with oil
> burning in them all the night; some hung out branches of iron curiously wrought, containing
> hundreds of lamps alight at once, which made a goodly show, namely in New Fish street, Thames
> street, etc.**"

**The bonfire is a reconciliation ritual with a stated purpose** — enemies are brought together
"by the labour of others" at a street fire, and tables of bread and drink are set out in front of
the rich men's doors for neighbours *and passers-by*. And the door-dressing is a precise plant
list: **green birch, long fennel, St John's wort, orpin, white lilies**, garlands, glass oil-lamps
burning all night, and **wrought-iron branches carrying hundreds of lamps** on the best streets.

**The marching watch** that accompanied it is documented down to the logistics:

- Route: from the little conduit by Paul's gate to West Cheap, by the stocks through Cornhill, by
  Leadenhall to Aldgate, back down Fenchurch street, by Gracechurch, about Gracechurch conduit, up
  Gracechurch street into Cornhill, and through into West Cheap again — **"The whole way for this
  marching watch extendeth to three thousand two hundred tailor's yards of assize."**
- Lighting: **"there were appointed seven hundred cressets, five hundred of them being found by
  the companies, the other two hundred by the chamber of London"** — plus one for each of "more
  than two hundred and forty" constables. **"the charge of every cresset was in light two
  shillings and four pence, and every cresset had two men, one to bear or hold it, another to bear
  a bag with light, and to serve it, so that the poor men pertaining to the cressets, taking
  wages, besides that every one had a straw hat, with a badge painted, and his breakfast in the
  mornings, amounted in number to almost two thousand."**
- The watch proper: "about two thousand men, part of them being old soldiers of skill, to be
  captains, lieutenants, serjeants, corporals, etc., wiflers, drummers, and fifes, standard and
  ensign bearers, sword players, trumpeters on horseback, demilances on great horses, gunners with
  hand guns, or half hakes, **archers in coats of white fustian, signed on the breast and back
  with the arms of the city**, their bows bent in their hands, with sheaves of arrows by their
  sides, pike-men in bright corslets… **there were also divers pageants, morris dancers**,
  constables — half on St John's eve, half on St Peter's eve — "in bright harness, some overgilt,
  and every one **a jornet of scarlet** thereupon, and a chain of gold, **his henchman following
  him, his minstrels before him, and his cresset light passing by him**."
- Rank displayed in pageant count: "**where the mayor had besides his giant three pageants, each
  of the sheriffs had besides their giants but two pageants**."

Green preserves a smaller town's version of the same quota system: "The city was to be lighted by
the Mayor finding '**two cressets, or six torches, or more at his pleasure**,' every Alderman
finding two cressets, and each of the Common Council with every constable and town clerk one
cresset" (Green I, PG 50392).

**Two findings there.** First: **light is rank.** How many cressets you must supply is your
position in the hierarchy, published annually in the street. Second: **the parade is an army
inventory** — 2,000 men in harness with an accompanying 2,000 lamp-bearers in straw hats with
painted badges, each given breakfast. Which is why Canterbury's harness rusted when the watch
lapsed, and why Henry VIII **cancelled** the 1539 marching watch after a 15,000-strong muster made
it redundant, after which "being once laid down, [it] was not raised again till the year 1548."

**And Bartholomew-tide wrestling**: "before the Lord Mayor, aldermen, and sheriffs of London,
placed in a large tent near unto Clerkenwell, of old time, were divers days spent in the pastime of
wrestling, where the officers of the city, namely, the sheriffs, sergeants, and yeomen, **the
porters of the king's beam or weigh-house**… and other of the city, were challengers of all men in
the suburbs" (Stow, PG 42959). **The city's officials wrestle challengers from the suburbs** — the
inside/outside boundary staged as sport.

## 6.6 Superstition, charm and omen

**Charms over doorways** — the brief asks for "a decal that MEANS something," and the archaeology
delivers, with a date caveat. Apotropaic marks are "symbols or patterns **scratched on the walls,
beams and thresholds** of buildings to protect them from witchcraft or evil spirits," concentrated
at the vulnerable openings — "**doors, windows or chimneys**." Forms: hexafoils and "flower-like
designs of overlapping circles" (the commonest in Britain); **taper burn marks on thresholds**;
"crisscrossing lines intended to confuse spirits"; and Marian marks — "**intertwined letters V and
M or a double V (for the protector, the Virgin Mary, alias Virgo Virginum)**." Documented sites
include Knole (marks of 1609 beneath floors), Shakespeare's Birthplace, the Tower of London,
Creswell Crags (over 100 marks found in 2019), and Gainsborough Old Hall, where **20 marks are
concentrated in the servants' quarters**. Concealed protective objects are a parallel practice:
"**witch bottles, horse skulls, dried cats, and shoes concealed within walls**" (Wikipedia,
*Apotropaic mark*).

*(Honesty flag: the dated examples cluster in the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries. Treat the
practice as early-modern-attested and medieval-plausible, not as documented medieval.)*

**Marks concentrated in the servants' quarters** is the detail worth keeping: protection is applied
where the *anxious* people sleep, not where the valuables are.

**Amulets against plague** are attested in the same breath as their failure: the plague-frightened
Londoners of 1665 bought charms from "those unperforming creatures who had gulled them of their
money," and Defoe's narrator notes how many "were afterwards carried away in the dead-carts and
thrown into the common graves of every parish **with these hellish charms and trumpery hanging
about their necks**" (Defoe, PG 376). **Corpses in a plague pit still wearing the charms they paid
for.** That is a Strange-band image that is merely historical.

**The pardoner's relic-economy** — the collector for St Anthony promising that "the blessed St.
Anthony may keep watch over your beeves and asses and swine and sheep" in exchange for corn and
oats, and "that small due which is payable once a year" (in Jusserand, PG 58530; lane 4 §4.3) —
supplies the town's ambient, transactional supernatural: **an annual subscription that protects
your livestock.**

**The public omen** has a documented example with a crowd reaction. Stow records that in 1230,
"upon the feast day of the Conversion of St. Paul," while Bishop Roger Niger was at mass in the
cathedral "**a great multitude of people being there present, suddenly the weather waxed**
[dark] … "lighted on the church, which so shook it, that it was like to have fallen, and therewithal
out of a dark cloud proceeded a flash of lightning, that all the church seemed to be on fire,
**whereupon such a stench ensued, that all men thought they should have died; thousands of men and
women ran out of the church, and being astonied, fell upon the ground void of all sense and
understanding; none of all the multitude tarried in the church save the bishop and one deacon,
which stood still before the high altar, awaiting the will of God. When the air was cleansed, the
multitude returned into the church, and the bishop ended the service**" (Stow, PG 42959).

**The whole congregation flees a stinking flash of lightning; the bishop and one deacon stand
still; the crowd comes back and the service finishes.** That is a complete Strange-band scene, in a
chronicle, with a named date.

Two smaller omen-adjacent facts, both structural rather than atmospheric:

- **Sanctuary's graded circles** marked by stone crosses, with the *fridstool* at the centre (lane
  4 §4.6) — the town contains a mapped supernatural gradient.
- **Excommunication as a security system** — the commons' treasure in the church "guarded by a
  sentence of excommunication against all who should violate so sacred a protection" (Green I). A
  lock made of damnation.

## 6.7 THE FIVE-TO-TEN MOST GAME-RELEVANT FINDINGS

1. **"All manner of harness within the city is decayed and rusted for lack of the yearly
   watch."** The festival *is* the maintenance regime for the town's arms. A town that has skipped
   its parade has rusting armour, and that is a documented causal chain, not an invention.
2. **The victuallers' lobby keeps the festival alive.** Cooks, brewers and hostellers agitate for
   processions "because thereby the victuallers lose their money." A pure economic motive behind a
   religious event.
3. **The pageant props live in a barn all year for 16d.** A mechanical angel that flaps its wings
   on a hidden winch with wheels oiled with soap (22d); three canvas beasts on hoops and laths
   "painted after nature"; a canvas castle at 3s 4d. **The props barn is a better location than the
   pageant.**
4. **THE MIDSUMMER LIGHT QUOTA: LIGHT IS RANK.** Mayor finds two cressets or six torches; every
   alderman two; every councilman, constable and town clerk one. 700 cressets in London, each with
   two men — one holding, one carrying the bag of light — in straw hats with painted badges, given
   breakfast. Nearly two thousand lamp-bearers.
5. **Doors dressed with green birch, long fennel, St John's wort, orpin and white lilies, with
   glass oil-lamps burning all night and wrought-iron branches carrying hundreds of lamps** on the
   best streets. The single most specific dressing recipe in the study.
6. **The bonfire is a reconciliation ritual** — enemies reconciled "by the labour of others" at a
   street fire, with tables of bread and drink set out before the rich men's doors for neighbours
   *and passers-by*.
7. **THE TWO-ACT FESTIVAL DAY.** Procession and relic-elevation by day; then the sacred quarter
   locked, dark and empty while the drinking quarter by the fish-market is crowded to the doors —
   and the belfry closes both. One day, two site occupancies, two districts.
8. **Church ales: the council closes the taverns, each ward builds a booth in the cemetery, and
   you may bring food but must buy the drink.** Ten annual iterations funded one church aisle for
   £44 14s 6d.
9. **The market and the temple interpenetrate** — stalls in the churchyard, goods stored in the
   nave, fair gates locked nightly on consecrated ground, and only Laud's generation thought it
   improper.
10. **Apotropaic marks cluster at doors, windows and chimneys — and in the servants' quarters.**
    Hexafoils, taper burns, VV/Marian marks; concealed shoes, witch bottles, horse skulls and dried
    cats in the walls.
11. **The plague dead were buried still wearing the charms they had been sold.**

## 6.8 DECLARED GAPS

- **The mystery-play cycles were not read.** York, Chester, Wakefield and N-Town all survive in
  print and none was obtained. What I have is the *economics and politics* of the pageants (which
  is arguably the more useful half) and almost nothing of their content, staging on wagons, or
  station routes. The York ordo — which craft played which pageant — is the obvious missing
  artefact and would map guilds to biblical episodes in one table.
- **Corpus Christi processional precedence disputes are asserted in the literature but not
  evidenced here.** I fetched an encyclopedic account that explicitly did not document them. Given
  that precedence quarrels are the single most game-usable feature of a ranked procession, this is
  a real hole.
- **No parish or churchwardens' account book was read.** These are the standard source for the
  economics of parish religion (lights, wax, bells, ales, the rood loft) and would have given exact
  sums for exactly the objects a dressing census wants.
- **The apotropaic-mark evidence is early modern**, and I have flagged it in place; the medieval
  door-charm is *plausible*, not proven, from what I read.
- **Almost nothing on the friars** as an urban presence, despite their being explicitly urban
  orders sited in the poor quarters. They appear in Winchester's belt of ecclesiastical property
  and in Nuremberg's topography and nowhere else in my reading.
- **Non-Christian urban religious life is treated only in lane 7**, and thinly. The mosque as the
  organising centre of a souk is stated there; the synagogue, the temple complex, and the shrine
  as urban anchors are not developed.
- **Nothing on the *sound* of urban worship** beyond bells — no processional music, no waits
  (though Stow names "the waits of the city" in the marching watch), no singing school except
  Nuremberg's Meistersinger.
