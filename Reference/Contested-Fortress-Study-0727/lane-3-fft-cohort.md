## 2. The Bethla Garrison set is the finding

FFT does not have a "fortress map." It has **five maps that are one fortress**: the sluice
approach (64), the granary (65), the south wall (66), the north wall (67), and the garrison
proper (68). One named place, five committed windows, played in sequence.

That is the **materialization-window law demonstrated in a shipped game**. A fortress is too
big to be one board, so it is played as an ordered series of finite slices on one persistent
site, and the slices are chosen by *what is being fought over*, not by geography.

Two consequences fall straight out:

- **Site 9's unit of play is a window, not a fortress.** Asking for one board that "is the
  fortress" is the wrong request, and it is the request the site's name invites.
- **The windows are named after their objective** — a sluice, a granary, a wall, a council
  chamber. Each is a distinct operating circuit of the same host.

### 2.1 The two-sided wall (66 and 67)

This is the pair that earns Site 9 its own portfolio slot.

**66 — South Wall, from inside.** The playfield *is the wall top*: a broad paved terrace
running the width of the plate, parapets on both edges, **two small pitched-roof kiosks
standing on the walk** as cover, stairs dropping to a lower court, blind arcading articulating
the outer face, torch brackets, and a rubble pit at one corner. Two elevation levels, a linear
route, and hard cover objects on it.

**67 — North Wall, from outside.** The same kind of wall, seen from the field: a great square
tower with a **machicolated corbel band** and two ranks of arrow loops, the curtain running
away along a grass slope with a small shrine perched on its top, and — occupying most of the
plate — an **open grassy field scattered with boulders**.

**One wall, two completely different battlefields.** From inside, it is a corridor with cover
and stairs. From outside, it is a blank vertical face across an open field with sparse rock
cover. Neither view is the "real" one, and a fortress that only supports the outside view is
half a site.

### 2.2 The infrastructure is the objective (64)

The sluice map is a dam: timber gates, walkways over them, impounded water on one side and a
dry channel on the other. The fortress's **water-control machinery** is staged as its own
battlefield, and the reason is that operating it decides the larger fight.

Genesis already believes this in principle — Site 5's operating circuits, Site 1's barrier
mechanism. The FFT evidence says it scales: at fortress size, the *infrastructure* (water,
stores, gate machinery, signal) is where the interesting objectives live, because the walls
themselves are too strong to be the interesting objective.

### 2.3 The heart is a room (68)

"Bethla Garrison" is not the castle exterior. It is a **stone council chamber**: a long
table on a raised carpeted dais, banners on the wall, a hearth, a candelabrum, deep window
embrasures, chairs. The fortress's centre of gravity is an interior of ordinary institutional
furniture.

This lines up exactly with the monastery lane's finding that **most of FFT's institutional
repetition is furniture, not architecture**. It also means the fortress's most narratively
loaded window is the cheapest one to build.

## 3. What FFT's fortress gates do

**1 — Igros main gate.** A blocky gate mass, portcullis recessed in an arch, **hanging
banners** and **heraldic shields** on the towers. In front, a **stone causeway across a moat**,
and the causeway is broken into irregular stepped slabs. Water on both sides forces the line.

**6 — Riovanes gate.** A great arched timber double door in a stone frame, reached by **broad
stone steps climbing** through terraced masses, with **two flaming braziers on plinths** at
the approach and a large arched undercroft to one side. Water and rock at the foot; a grass
ramp on the other flank.

Common grammar across both:

- **one gate mass, one committed approach, one water or void obstacle**;
- the approach **changes level** on its way in (broken causeway; climbing steps);
- **cloth and heraldry carry the identity**, not carving — which is a rollable,
  occupancy-swappable dressing exactly as the monastery lane found for institutional gates;
- **motivated fire** at the threshold, owned by the garrison.

**9 — Citadel of Igros** is the interior counterpart: a broad water court inside the works,
a **ruined arched bridge with a real gap in it**, stepping blocks across the water, waterfalls
pouring from arched openings, and a stepped terrace climbing at the back to a tall arcaded
block. The interior of a fortress, in FFT, is a vertical-and-water puzzle, not a parade
ground.

## 4. The fortified-town case, built (35)

**Zaland Fort City** puts the two vernaculars side by side on one plate: a **stone gate arch
and curtain wall run** standing on a rocky bluff at the front, with a **rock and scree slope**
falling below it — and immediately behind, **half-timbered houses with red tile roofs and
chimneys**. The wall is visibly cracked and patched.

This is lane 1's reading 7 solved with almost no geometry: *military masonry in front,
domestic fabric behind, one plate.* It is also the cleanest available answer to the Site 9 /
Site 10 boundary — FFT does not merge them, it **abuts** them.

## 5. Two vernaculars stacked (49)

**Fort Zeakden**, in snow: a stone tower keep flanked by two smaller stone towers, with
**timber platform structures added at mid-height** and a **timber walkway bridging between
them well above the ground**. Snow ground, rock outcrops.

- **Timber additions to a stone core** are how FFT expresses a fortification that has been
  adapted, reinforced or improvised. That is a construction *condition*, and it maps onto the
  makeshift/scavenged vernacular the structure kit already carries.
- **The high timber bridge is a duel deck** — a narrow elevated span between two masses, with
  a fall on both sides. It is the single most FFT-ish tactical object in the cohort.

## 6. The fort is a quarter of the plate (115)

**Banished Fort**: a curtain wall and a square tower with cross loops and a red banner,
clinging to a rocky hillside. **The rest of the plate is hill** — bare rock, grass, a scree
fall, and a low ruined wall run stepping down at one side.

Consistent with the guard-post relational rule that quiet ground is active composition, and
with images A2/A3/D1/E2 in lane 2. FFT's fortress maps are **mostly terrain**, and the
fortification is a strong silhouette occupying a minority of the ground.

## Findings (plain English)

1. **FFT plays a fortress as a set of named windows, not as one map.** Bethla Garrison is
   five maps: sluice, granary, south wall, north wall, council chamber. This is the
   materialization-window law, shipped.
2. **Each window is named for its objective**, and the objectives are the fortress's
   *infrastructure* — water control, stores, a wall segment, the command room.
3. **The wall is two battlefields.** From inside it is a broad walk with kiosks, stairs and
   parapets; from outside it is a blank tower face across an open field. FFT built both.
4. **The wall top is a proper playfield** — wide enough for structures to stand on it.
5. **The fortress's heart is an ordinary institutional interior** (a council chamber), which
   is the cheapest window to build and the most narratively loaded.
6. **Fortress gates are one mass, one committed approach, one water/void obstacle, and a
   level change on the way in.**
7. **Heraldic cloth is the identity buy at gates** — banners and shields, not carving.
8. **Fortress interiors are vertical-and-water puzzles**, with real gaps in ruined bridges
   and stepping stones across water (9).
9. **The fortified town is built by abutting two vernaculars** on one plate: masonry wall in
   front, timber-and-tile houses behind (35).
10. **Timber added to a stone core is FFT's adaptation vocabulary** (49), and it produces the
    high narrow bridge as a signature tactical object.
11. **The fortification occupies a minority of its plate** (115, and every exterior in the
    cohort). Terrain does the rest.
12. **Nothing in the cohort is a full concentric castle.** FFT never builds two closed rings.
    It buys the same tension with one wall and a second level, or with two abutting masses.
    That is a real divergence from the historical lane, and it is a cost warning: if Genesis
    wants the true multi-ward reading, FFT is not evidence that it is affordable.

## Declared gaps

- **Fifteen of about thirty cohort maps read, one view each.** Anything said about a map's
  other rotations or its far side is unverified. Only view 1 was read for every map — no plan
  view (view 0) was read in this lane at all, so **no statement here is a plan reading.**
- **The castle *interiors* (7, 10, 13, 16, 20, 97) were not opened.** Given that lane 4 shows
  the live demand is overwhelmingly for fortification interiors, this is the lane's most
  consequential hole and the first thing a follow-up pass should close.
- **Map 65, the Granary, was converted and not read** — the stores/logistics window is
  therefore un-evidenced, despite being named.
- **No tile counts, no elevations, no measurements.** Every "broad," "wide enough," "well
  above the ground" is an eyeball reading off an upscaled sprite image.
- **No cross-check against `analysis/GUARD-POST-COHORT.md`** in the study archive, which may
  already contain readings of maps 1, 6, 115 and others. This lane worked from the images.
