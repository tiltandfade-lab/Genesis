# Lane 2 — the urban dressing census

## 0. What this lane found, before the roster

Adam's question was "are we accounting for enough materials?" The honest answer after
looking at the evidence is: **no, and the shortfall is not mostly in materials.** It is in
two other places.

**First, the town's real density is on the wall, not on the ground.** Thomson's second-hand
furniture dealer is the clearest single frame in the study: chairs stacked on the pavement,
garments hung up the door jamb, a rug over the opening, a birdcage on the flags, books and
tins on a table. Atget's newspaper kiosk is a timber box that has completely disappeared
under the goods clipped to it. In both cases **the structure is invisible and the goods are
the object.** FFT reaches the same result by a different route (lane 5 §4): fewer than a
dozen distinct props across thirteen town maps, but every wall surface carrying three or
four paint layers.

**Second, the wear layer is a whole bin and the placeholder line does not mention it.** A
pre-industrial street is legible almost entirely through where water, feet, wheels and
smoke have been. Marville's cobbles carry a crowned carriageway with a channel; every wall
base carries a hard grime tide-line; every gutter mouth has stained the render below it.
None of that is a material and none of it is a prop.

So the roster below is deliberately **decal-heavy**. That is the finding, not an accident of
listing.

## 2. The finding under the roster: colour per unit is the town's signature material

Every rendered/plastered street in the evidence — Naples, Chinchón, Quedlinburg, FFT's
Zarghidas and Dorter — reads as *many owners* because **each unit is a different colour of
the same material.** Ochre next to pink next to cream next to bare grey. The masonry is
identical; the limewash is not.

This is cheap and it is exactly the lever the realm-skin axis already uses: one material
family, a rolled colour per building unit, and the frontage stops being one wall. It is
also *causal* rather than decorative — a unit's colour is its owner's last limewashing,
which means colour, condition and occupancy are the same fact. The abandoned unit is the
grey one.

**The second-order version, also evidenced:** where the render has failed, the substrate
shows through in patches with a hard edge (Naples, dramatically). That gives a
render-loss mask a real job — it reveals the masonry material beneath, so both layers have
to exist anyway.

## 3. THE CAUSAL LIGHT LAW COMES TO TOWN — who owns each street light

The law (GOLDEN-SITES-CATALOG, site 7): every light in a scene has a visible physical owner.
The town is the first site with lights that are neither a fire nor a window, so the
ownership question is real. **The historical answer is unusually clean, and it hands the
game a free occupancy mechanic.**

**Street lighting was a duty laid on the householder, not a service supplied by the town.**
In London, "in 1417 it was law … that families hang lanterns during the dark hours of
winter" — the Mayor, Sir Henry Barton, ordaining that all houses hang lanterns outside when
night falls in the winter months. Paris required street-facing windows to show a light at
night, by an order variously dated in the sixteenth century by the sources I found (one
gives 1524). *(Both facts come from secondary web sources, not from the ordinances; see the
gaps.)*

So, per fixture:

| light | physical owner | consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **bracket lantern on a scrolled iron** | **the building unit it is bolted to** | if that unit is dark/shut/abandoned, so is its lamp. A shuttered frontage makes a dark street *causally*. |
| lantern on a wire across the street | **the pair of facing units**, with the pulley on one of them | a lit span means both sides are occupied |
| **lamp on a free-standing post** | **the town** | the civic light — only appears where there is a functioning authority. Its presence is an occupancy statement in itself. |
| arcade lantern on a chain | the arcade's building | lights the covered walk, not the street |
| shrine lamp in a corner niche | the shrine, i.e. the neighbourhood | the one light nobody's occupancy switches off |
| **hearth fire / lit shopfront** | **the room behind it** | interior occupancy leaking into the street |
| torch / brazier carried | a figure | mobile, and the only light that can be taken away |

**The gift.** Because the default urban light is *owned by a specific frontage unit*, the
occupancy axis (functioning · corrupt · faction-seized · abandoned · a front) writes itself
onto the lighting with no new system: count the lit brackets. A street where only the
corrupt watch-house's lamp burns is a scene the engine can produce from facts it already
rolls.

**One fixture, two jobs.** Annan's Glasgow close shows the lamp and the close's name board
mounted on the *same* iron bracket — the light and the sign are one object. Worth knowing
before the lamp and the sign are specced as two props.

## 4. Where the dressing actually sits (the placement grammar)

Stated as relations rather than a list, because placement is what the compiler needs.

1. **Goods occupy the pavement, not the street.** Thomson's chairs, table and birdcage stand
   on the flagged footway right against the shop. The carriageway stays clear — carts use it.
   **The dressing belt is the strip between the wall and the kerb.**
2. **Vertical surfaces carry as much as horizontal ones.** Garments on the door jamb, a rug
   over the opening, prints pinned to a board, bills pasted to a flank wall, goods clipped
   over every face of the kiosk. A town that only dresses its floor will look empty.
3. **The street's air is used.** Laundry lines, hanging signs, bracket lanterns, wires with
   lamps, and jetties all occupy the volume between the frontages above head height. That
   band is free real estate for the fixed camera and it is where the town's silhouette lives.
4. **Wall bases are protected and dirty.** Guard stones along the base, a splash band above
   them, moss in the joints. Every wall meets the ground with three layers.
5. **Wear points at openings and follows the route.** Ruts down the carriageway's line;
   a scuffed fan at each door; a hollowed threshold on the used ones.
6. **Water leaves a vertical trace and a horizontal one.** Stains falling from every gutter
   mouth and downpipe; a wet channel running the length of the street.
7. **The centre stays clear.** Confirmed again from the other side: Chinchón's plaza floor is
   bare, FFT's Lesalia street is bare, Marville's carriageways are bare. Dressing is an edge
   condition.

## 5. What FFT's town does with the same census (cross-check)

Lane 5 §4 counts it: **fewer than a dozen distinct free-standing props across thirteen town
maps, and three or four paint layers on every wall.** So FFT would populate this roster
extremely unevenly — nearly all of §1a and §1b, and perhaps eight or ten of the twenty-four
prop slots.

That is a genuine budget argument, and it points the same way as the historical evidence
rather than against it: **the frames that feel densest — Thomson, Atget, Naples — are dense
in a narrow belt against the building, and empty everywhere else.** The town is not dressed
all over. It is dressed at the wall.

## Findings (plain English)

1. **The gap was never mostly materials.** The placeholder named four wall/roof materials.
   What is actually missing is a **wear-and-message layer** (18 decal slots) and a **prop
   set** (24 slots) — 54 demand slots in total.
2. **Colour per building unit is the town's signature material fact.** One plaster family,
   a rolled colour per unit, and a shared-wall street stops reading as one wall. It is also
   causal: colour is the owner's last limewashing, so colour, condition and occupancy are
   one fact, and the abandoned unit is the grey one.
3. **Goods are the dressing, and the structure disappears under them.** Atget's kiosk is
   invisible beneath clipped-on newspapers; Thomson's shop has its stock on the pavement and
   its clothes hanging up the door jamb. A stall's read comes from what is on it.
4. **Dressing lives in a narrow belt against the wall.** Footway, wall base, and the air
   above head height. The carriageway and the plaza floor stay clear — the centre law and
   the quiet-ground rule survive urbanisation intact.
5. **The town uses its air.** Laundry lines across the street, hanging signs, bracket
   lanterns, wires, jetties. That overhead band is where the street's character sits and it
   costs no floor area.
6. **Every wall meets the ground three times over**: guard stones, a knee-high splash band,
   and moss in the joints. It is the most repeated single observation in the whole lane.
7. **The street light is owned by the house.** London ordered householders to hang lanterns
   in winter from 1417; Paris ordered street-facing windows lit. So the default urban lamp's
   causal owner is a specific frontage unit — which means the occupancy axis writes itself
   onto the lighting for free. Count the lit brackets and you know who is home.
8. **A lamp on a free-standing post is a civic statement.** It only exists where an
   authority functions. That makes the difference between bracket-lit and post-lit a
   rollable occupancy fact rather than a style choice.
9. **The lamp and the sign can be one object.** In Annan's Glasgow close the gas lamp and the
   close's name board hang from the same iron. Worth knowing before specifying two props.
10. **Water writes on the town, vertically and horizontally.** A stain under every gutter
    mouth and downpipe; a wet, algae-edged channel down the carriageway. The downpipe is
    also the only dependable strong vertical on a frontage.
11. **Wheel ruts are the route-region made visible.** The carriageway's worn tracks *are*
    the road corridor the guard-post grammar already insists on — the same fact, drawn in
    wear instead of geometry.
12. **The fountain and the well are not the only water fixtures.** Naples' public wash
    trough — a stepped run of stone basins with running water, worked at by a crowd — is a
    third option, and it is the one that is unambiguously a *working* object rather than a
    monument.
13. **Guard stones are the cheapest period-correct object in the roster.** A row of low
    rounded posts along every wall base, in every French street photograph. One piece,
    repeated, and it dates the town instantly.

## Declared gaps

- **This census is built from eight images I opened plus borrowed evidence from four sibling
  lanes.** It is a census of *what appears in that evidence*, not an exhaustive inventory of
  the pre-industrial street. A larger pull would find more — trade-specific fixtures
  (butcher's hooks and rails, dyer's poles, a smith's shoeing frame), religious street
  furniture beyond the corner niche, market regulation objects (the standard measure, the
  pillory, the tron), and civic apparatus (fire hooks, the parish ladder, the watch box).
  **I know those are missing; I did not evidence them.**
- **The lighting ordinances are secondary sourced.** The London 1417 and Paris sixteenth-
  century facts come from web summaries (a history-of-street-lighting page and a history
  blog), not from the ordinances or a scholarly edition. The Paris date varies between
  sources I saw. If the ownership law becomes load-bearing, the primary references should
  be checked — but note that the *design* consequence (the lamp belongs to the house) does
  not depend on the exact year.
- **No colour data.** The only colour evidence in the lane is one photochrom of Naples, which
  is a hand-tinted process — its ochres and pinks are a printer's choices, not measurements.
  Everything else is monochrome or modern-restored. **The per-unit colour finding is real as
  a relation and unsupported as a palette.**
- **Almost all the photographic evidence is nineteenth-century, not medieval.** Marville
  (1865), Annan (1868–77), Thomson (1877), Atget (1910–11) and the Naples photochrom
  (1890s) document a *pre-industrial fabric photographed late*. Gas lamps, painted fascias
  and kerbed pavements are in that fabric and earlier centuries did not have all of them. I
  have flagged the two clearly later items (post lamp, developed shopfront entablature) but
  the boundary is soft, and someone should decide how late the town's dressing is allowed
  to look.
- **No evidence of a working historic market in use.** Commons searches for market-stall
  photography failed repeatedly. The stall slots are argued from FFT's produce trays, from
  Atget's kiosk, from the market halls' architecture, and from the medieval shutter
  mechanism — not from a photograph of a market day. **The seed's "functional stalls" remain
  the least-evidenced thing in this study.**
- **No quantities.** The roster says which slots exist. It says nothing about how many
  lanterns, carts or stalls a street should carry, and nothing about a density ceiling. FFT
  gives the only number in the study and it is a soft one ("fewer than about a dozen distinct
  props across thirteen maps").
- **No interior dressing census.** Map 96's pub is the only interior I looked at. If site 10
  is enterable — and the institution surely is — the interior roster is a separate job.
- **The bin assignments are proposals, not tests.** Whether a pilaster, a stall board, a
  guard stone or a railing is geometry or paint depends on whether it changes play at the
  grid's resolution, and that is a clay question. I have called each one and said why; none
  of them has been measured.
