# Lane 5 — The senses and the squalor

**Nothing here is locked. Adam rules; this file evidences.**

A note on tone before the evidence. The temptation with this lane is the pantomime version —
"medieval towns smelled bad." The record is more interesting than that, and more useful: it
is a record of **towns fighting their own filth, constantly, expensively, and by name.** The
nuisance presentments are complaints, which means somebody had a standard. That gap — between
the standard and the street — is where the playable material lives.

---

## 5.0 The one-sentence result

Every sensory fact in a pre-industrial town has **an owner, a fine, and a repeating cost**:
the ditch has ditchers on a day-rate, the dung has a raker with a horn, the water has a
bearer who also cries the hour, the pig has a proctor with a bell — so the honest way to
narrate urban squalor is not "it stinks" but **"someone is supposed to be dealing with this,
and here is what happened to them."**

---

## 5.1 The nuisance record — a whole town's filth, itemised

Hythe's presentments, as summarised by Green, are the single densest passage (Green, *Town
Life in the Fifteenth Century* II, PG 51637):

> "[the town's watercourse] till it became a mere swamp, builders blocked it up utterly with
> the framework of their new houses, and traders made their wharves upon it. **Not only the
> most thriving and respectable merchants, such as the Honywodes, but the butcher and
> swine-keeper as well, threw the waste of house and shambles and swine-cote into the open
> street till there was scarcely any passage left for the wayfarer**; or established a
> '**hoggestok**,' '**which smells very badly and is abominable to all men coming to market,
> as well as to all dwelling in the town**,' say the jury. **There was hardly a street or lane
> which was not described as 'almost stinking and a nuisance.'** The 'Cherche Weye' was
> occupied by the pits of a skinner. '**There was no carrying through Brokhellislane.**' The
> street by which the procession went on Holy Thursday, the day of perambulation of the town,
> **could scarcely be traversed**. Everywhere gates and bridges were falling to decay, ditches
> unrepaired, and hedges overgrown; and one offender who had obstructed a road by neglecting
> to repair the ditches found an easy way of escape from his obligations by a courtesy to the
> Bailiff — '**the dyeing of two cloths that the said ditches may not have to be repaired**.'
> Worse still **the Holy Well was choked with refuse**, and so was the well in West Hythe, and
> '**the water in the cart of Geoffrey Waterleader by which the whole community is refreshed**'
> was equally obstructed and spoiled by the refuse of the butchers' shambles."

Everything a dressing census could want is in that paragraph, with the crucial addition of
**agency**: the respectable merchants are named alongside the butcher; the obstruction is
excused by a bribe *paid in dyed cloth*; the holy well and the drinking-water cart are both
fouled by the same shambles. **The bribe-in-kind — two cloths dyed, in exchange for not having
to repair the ditch — is a perfect Grounded-band corruption beat.**

Green's mortality note follows immediately: "It is no wonder that pestilence devastated Hythe
in 1412, as throughout the century it swept over one town after another. But **it has been
calculated that even without the aid of pestilence the ordinary mortality of a borough in the
Middle Ages was almost equal to that of a town during a visitation of cholera to-day**."
*(Honesty flag: that comparison is Green's, writing in 1894, and rests on a calculation she
does not reproduce. Treat as period-scholarship rhetoric, not as a demographic figure.)*

And the darkly funny corollary, also Green's: "**Even the first well-meant efforts of
Corporations to shut pigs out of their streets and banish wandering dogs, by levying fines from
any inhabitant who had an 'irrational animal going about' in the churchyard or the market,
doubtless added to the dangers of pestilence by removing the** [scavengers]." **The pigs were
the street cleaners; removing them made things worse.**

## 5.2 Animals in the street

- **The dawn and dusk herd.** "the common herd went round the streets to collect the swine and
  drive them out on the moor till evening" (Green I, PG 50392; lane 1 §1.2).
- **The impounding rule.** Under the 1665 plague orders: "**no hogs, dogs, or cats, or tame
  pigeons, or ponies, be suffered to be kept within any part of the city, or any swine to be or
  stray in the streets or lanes, but that such swine be impounded by the beadle or any other
  officer, and the owner punished according to Act of Common Council, and that the dogs be
  killed by the dog-killers appointed for that purpose**" (Orders of the Lord Mayor and
  Aldermen, 1665, as reproduced in Daniel Defoe, *A Journal of the Plague Year*, PG 376).
  **"The dog-killers appointed for that purpose" is an occupancy roster entry with an emergency
  trigger.**
- **THE ST ANTHONY'S PIGS — the best animal fact in the study.** Stow (PG 42959):

  > "the officers charged with oversight of the markets in this city did divers times **take
  > from the market people, pigs starved, or otherwise unwholesome for man's sustenance; these
  > they slit in the ear.** One of the proctors for St. Anthonie's **tied a bell about the neck,
  > and let it feed on the dunghills; no man would hurt or take them up, but if any gave to them
  > bread, or other feeding, such would they know, watch for, and daily follow, whining till
  > they had somewhat given them**; whereupon was raised a proverb, '**Such an one will follow
  > such an one, and whine as it were an Anthonie pig**;' but if such a pig grew to be fat, and
  > came to good liking (as oft times they did), **then the proctor would take him up to the use
  > of the hospital.**"

  Read the whole machine: a market inspector condemns a pig → **slits its ear** (a permanent
  visible mark of condemnation) → a hospital's proctor **hangs a bell on it** and turns it loose
  on the dunghills → the bell makes it inviolable, so nobody touches it → the pig learns to beg
  and follows anyone who fed it, whining → **the town gets a proverb out of it** → when it is
  fat, the hospital claims it. **A marked, belled, protected, begging animal that is walking
  charity property.** This is the single strongest meaning-bearing prop in the study: an ear-slit
  and a bell, and every townsperson knows exactly what both mean.

## 5.3 Waste, and who moves it

**The rakers and the horn.** From the 1665 Orders (Defoe, PG 376), and explicitly described as
existing practice, not innovation:

> "**every householder do cause the street to be daily prepared before his door, and so to keep
> it clean swept all the week long**… That the sweeping and filth of houses be daily carried away
> by the rakers, and **that the raker shall give notice of his coming by the blowing of a horn, as
> hitherto hath been done.**"

Two mechanics: (1) **the householder is responsible for the pavement in front of his own door** —
frontage carries a duty, not just a right; (2) **a horn announces the refuse collection**, so the
street has a recurring sound cue that summons people to their doors with their sweepings.

**The laystalls.** Same orders: "**That the laystalls be removed as far as may be out of the
city and common passages, and that no nightman or other be suffered to empty a vault into any
garden near about the city.**" Stow shows what a laystall becomes: beyond Finsbury court the
grounds "have been so **overheightened with lay-stalls of dung, that now three windmills are
thereon set**; the ditches be filled up, and the bridges overwhelmed" (PG 42959). **Three
windmills standing on an artificial hill of accumulated dung** is a genuine, sourced, silhouette-
defining piece of urban-fringe landscape.

**The gong farmers.** Night-soil men, working a legally restricted shift: "no Goungfermour shall
carry any Ordure till after **nine of the Clocke in the night**" (regulation of 1562), working
"between 9:00 p.m. and 5:00 a.m."; charging "**two shillings per ton** of waste removed" in the
late fifteenth century; taking the waste to laystalls or to Thames-side dumps such as **Dung
Wharf**, "then transported by barge to be used as fertiliser on fields or market gardens"; and
"permitted to live only in specified areas" (Wikipedia, *Gong farmer*). *Honesty flag: the
frequently repeated anecdotes attached to this trade — Richard the Raker drowning in a cesspit in
1325, and the offender punished by being sunk in his own pipe of gong up to the neck — are
poorly sourced in that article and should be treated as folklore-grade until checked.*

**The ditch, and its price.** Stow keeps the accounts, and they are a maintenance-economy in
miniature (PG 42959):

- 1354 — the city ditch overflows into the Tower ditch; the king orders it cleansed.
- **1379** — mayor John Philpot has it cleansed, "and every householder to pay **five pence,
  which was for a day's work** towards the charges thereof."
- Richard II, 10th year — a toll on wares by land or water **for ten years** toward repairing the
  wall and cleansing the ditch.
- 1414, 1477 — cleansed again.
- **1519** — "The chief ditcher had by the day **seven pence**, the second ditcher **six pence**,
  the other ditchers **five pence**. And **every vagabond (for so were they termed) one penny the
  day, meat and drink, at charges of the city.** £95 3s. 4d." **A four-tier wage ladder ending in
  conscripted vagrants paid a penny plus food and drink** — and the total cost of the job.
- 1540, 1549 (at the companies' charge), **1569** — cleansing plus "a new sewer, and wharf of
  timber… **£814 15s. 8d.**"

**A public work that has to be redone roughly every generation, at a cost the town records, with
a named wage ladder** is exactly the sort of recurring civic burden that produces factions,
grudges and hooks.

The Walbrook's fate is the same story: a 1589 fifteenth raised a thousand marks to cleanse it and
bring springs from Hampstead Heath, but "much money being therein spent, **the effect failed**, so
that the brook, by means of continual encroachments upon the banks getting over the water, and
casting of soilage into the stream, **is now become worse cloyed and choken than ever it was
before**" (Stow, PG 42959). **An expensive failed public works is a period-normal condition
state.**

## 5.4 Water supply

The corpus supports four distinct supply types, all of which can coexist:

1. **The public conduit** — a piped, cisterned street fountain. Stow's Cornhill conduit is the
   worked example, and note what happened to it: the ancient well was **planked over** and built
   upon (with the cage and pillory on top), then rediscovered when they came to enlarge it and
   "revived and restored to use — **it is since made a pump**" (PG 42959).
2. **The well** — including the "boss": "Bosse alley, so called of **a boss of spring water
   continually running**, which standeth by Billingsgate against this alley, and was sometime made
   by the executors of Richard Whittington" (Stow, PG 42959). **A continuously running spout,
   named after its benefactor.**
3. **The water bearer** — who also cries the hour and carries the holy water (lane 2 §2.6); a
   trade that receives ruined households.
4. **The water cart** — "the water in the cart of Geoffrey Waterleader by which the whole
   community is refreshed" (Hythe; §5.1). **A named man with a cart is the water main.**

And the failure mode is always the same: the source is fouled by an adjacent trade (the shambles),
or built over, or choked by encroachment.

## 5.5 Noise

Assembled from across the corpus, because no source treats it as a topic:

- **Bells** — quarter chimes all day and all night at Bruges; curfew rung twice; the common bell
  as riot summons; the alarm bell for fire; the passing bell.
- **The water bearer crying the hour** (Totnes).
- **The raker's horn.**
- **Street cries** — the whole of lane 2 §2.5. Note the *density*: in *London Lickpenny* the
  countryman is cried at continuously for the length of three streets.
- **Shopkeepers bawling "What do ye lack?"** "like butchers in a market," for centuries.
- **The forge at night** — the spurriers "blow up their fires so vigorously that their forges
  begin all at once to blaze… **to the annoyance of the sick and all their neighbourhood**"
  (Green II, PG 51637). Note *the sick* are named as the injured party.
- **The tavern** — "Pewter pots they clattered on a heap; There was harp, pipe, and minstrelsy."
- **The hue and cry** — "the watch runs, the town wakes up, lights appear, and one after the other
  the more zealous join in the chase" (Jusserand, PG 58530).
- **The market itself.** For the scale a great market reaches, the best witness is not European:
  at Tlatelolco "the bustle and noise occasioned by this multitude of human beings was so great
  that it **could be heard at a distance of more than four miles**" (Bernal Díaz, PG 32474; lane
  7).

## 5.6 FIRE — the urban catastrophe

FitzStephen names it as one of exactly two urban plagues, and the line is worth having verbatim
because it is a whole design brief: "**The only plagues of London are the immoderate quaffing
among the foolish sort, and often casualties by fire**" (in Stow, PG 42959). *Drink and fire.*

**The building law came out of fire.** After the great fires of the late twelfth century, the
1189 assize under Henry FitzAilwin (London's first mayor) required that where two neighbours
agreed to build between them, "each gave **a foot and a half of land**, and they constructed at
their joint cost **a stone wall three feet thick and sixteen feet in height**"; thatched roofs
were to be replaced with tiles, with forfeiture of the structure to the city as a penalty. Stone
was compulsory **in the party walls only** — "the rest of the buildings might be made of
anything, and was usually constructed of wood" (summarised from the several published accounts of
Fitz-Elwyne's Assize of Buildings; see §5.9 gap note).

**That is site 10's signature buy with a legal origin.** The party wall is not an aesthetic
choice; it is a **fire regulation with a stated thickness, a stated height, and a stated land
contribution of eighteen inches from each neighbour**. Everything else may be timber.

**The hazard that survived all regulation** is the jetty: projecting upper storeys narrowing the
street, against which Charles II proclaimed in 1661 and 1665 without effect (Wikipedia, *Great
Fire of London*); and Coulton's night-street hazard list — "projecting staircases and penthouses"
(PG 37277).

**Curfew itself is fire law** (lane 1 §1.4): cover the embers, extinguish the lights.

**The equipment.** Parish churches held the kit: "**long ladders, leather buckets, axes, and
'firehooks'**." A firehook was "**a heavy pole perhaps 30 feet (9 m) long with a strong hook and
ring at one end**," worked with ropes and pulleys to pull a burning or threatened building down
(Wikipedia, *Great Fire of London*). **The parish church is the fire station** — which sits
neatly beside Green's finding that it was also the armoury and the treasury: "This was the
fortress of the borough against its enemies — its place of safety where the treasure of the
commons was stored in dangerous times, **the arms in the steeple**, the wealth of corn or wool or
precious goods in the church itself, guarded by a sentence of excommunication against all who
should violate so sacred a protection" (Green I, PG 50392).

**The technique is demolition, and it needs authorisation.** Pulling down houses to make a
firebreak required the Lord Mayor's approval — the bottleneck that lost London in 1666, when
Bloodworth refused because the premises were rented and the owners absent, and said "**A woman
might piss it out**," then went back to bed (Wikipedia, *Great Fire of London*).

**The watch is the detection layer.** A thousand nightly watchmen or bellmen patrolled; church
bells alerted the citizenry. At Bruges, watchmen stood permanently on the belfry "to give warning
if enemies approached **or fire broke out in any part of the town, a constant source of danger
when most of the houses were built of wood**," and "if there is a fire, the alarum-bell clangs
over the city" (Omond, PG 18670).

**A fully-realised alternative fire regime** exists in the corpus and belongs in this lane even
though its source is Chinese (see lane 7 for context). At Kinsay (Hangzhou) under the Great Khan:
houses keep "**lofty towers of stone in which articles of value are stored for fear of fire; for
most of the houses themselves are of timber**"; each of the bridges has a guard of ten with a
wooden slab and a metal basin and a timekeeper, striking the hours; part of the watch patrols
"to see if any light or fire is burning after the lawful hours; **if they find any they mark the
door**, and in the morning the owner is summoned before the magistrates"; on a fire the watch
beat the slab and one or two thousand converge, moving goods "either by removing them to the
towers above mentioned, or by putting them in boats and transporting them to the islands in the
lake"; and — the sharpest clause — "**no citizen dares leave his house at night, or to come near
the fire; only those who own the property, and those watchmen who flock to help.**" A hilltop
tower carries a hanging wooden slab beaten with a mallet as the citywide alarm (Marco Polo,
*Travels*, vol. II, PG 12410).

**Four transferable fire mechanics** fall out of that: the fireproof stone strongroom in a timber
house · the **marked door** for an illegal light · goods evacuated by boat to an island · and a
law that keeps everyone *but* the owner and the watch away from a burning building.

## 5.7 PLAGUE — a town under emergency government

The 1665 London Orders (reproduced in Defoe, PG 376) are the most complete emergency-ordinance
text in the study, and they read as a ready-made alternate ruleset for an urban site. The offices
and rules, condensed but quoting the operative language:

**Offices created:**
- **Examiners** — one or more per parish, appointed by the alderman and common council, serving
  at least two months; **"if any fit person so appointed shall refuse to undertake the same, the
  said parties so refusing to be committed to prison until they shall conform themselves."**
  *(Refusing the office is imprisonable.)*
- **Watchmen** — "**to every infected house there be appointed two watchmen, one for every day,
  and the other for the night**"; no one in or out "upon pain of severe punishment"; "if the
  watchman be sent upon any business, **to lock up the house and take the key with him**"; day
  watch "until ten of the clock at night," night watch "until six in the morning."
- **Searchers** — **women**, sworn, "of honest reputation, and of the best sort as can be got in
  this kind," to determine cause of death; "**no searcher during this time of visitation be
  permitted to use any public work or employment, or keep any shop or stall, or be employed as a
  laundress**."
- **Chirurgeons** — the city quartered between them, each with "one quarter for his limit," paid
  "**twelve-pence a body searched by them, to be paid out of the goods of the party searched, if
  he be able, or otherwise by the parish**."
- **Nurse-keepers** — if one leaves an infected house within 28 days of a death, **the house she
  moves to is shut up for 28 days.**

**Rules on the household:**
- Notice of "blotch, or purple, or swelling" to the examiner **within two hours**.
- Sequestration the same night; the house shut up **for a month** even if the sick person lives.
- **"That every house visited be marked with a red cross of a foot long in the middle of the
  door, evident to be seen, and with these usual printed words, that is to say, 'Lord, have mercy
  upon us,' to be set close over the same cross, there to continue until lawful opening of the
  same house."**
- Shutting up "**for the space of four weeks after all be whole.**"
- **"That precise order to be taken that the searchers, chirurgeons, keepers, and buriers are not
  to pass the streets without holding a red rod or wand of three feet in length in their hands,
  open and evident to be seen.**"
- Removals of the sick permitted only **by night**.
- Burials "**at most convenient hours, always either before sun-rising or after sun-setting**," no
  neighbours or friends accompanying, no children near the corpse, "**and that all the graves shall
  be at least six feet deep.**"
- No bedding or apparel out of infected houses; brokers who buy from one within two months have
  **their own house shut up twenty days**.
- Hackney-coaches that carried infected persons must "**stand unemployed by the space of five or
  six days**."

**Rules on the streets and on assembly** — the sweeping/raker/laystall/animal clauses (§5.2–5.3),
plus: no stinking fish, unwholesome flesh, musty corn; brewers and tippling-houses inspected for
"musty and unwholesome casks"; **all plays, bear-baitings, games, singing of ballads,
buckler-play** prohibited; **all public feasting**, especially by the companies, forborne "**and
that the money thereby spared be preserved and employed for the benefit and relief of the poor
visited with the infection**"; and no drinking in any tavern, ale-house or coffee-house "**after
nine of the clock in the evening, according to the ancient law and custom of this city.**"

**The design gift here is enormous:** a single roll ("the town is in visitation") swaps in *a
door marking, a wand-carrying occupational class, a two-shift guard on private houses, a curfew
on drink, a ban on all public entertainment, night-only funerals, and a six-foot grave rule* —
and it does so with period wording that needs no invention.

**The enforcement failed exactly where you would expect.** Defoe's narrator records people who
"got two or three keys made to their locks, or they found ways to unscrew the locks such as were
screwed on… **and while they sent away the watchman to the market, to the bakehouse, or for one
trifle or another, open the door and go out as often as they pleased.** But this being found out,
the officers afterwards had orders to **padlock up the doors on the outside**, and place bolts on
them as they thought fit."

**The dead-cart and the bellman** are the period's most recognisable night sound, and Defoe
reproduces the exchange: the watchman knocks, "and the bellman called out several times, '**Bring
out your dead**'; but nobody answered, till the man that drove the cart, being called to other
houses, would stay no longer, and drove away." A servant-maid had earlier been "brought down to
the door dead, and the buriers or bearers, as they were called, put her into the cart, **wrapt
only in a green rug**, and carried her away."

**The pit** is described with dimensions, which is what makes it usable: the Aldgate pit was
"about **forty feet in length, and about fifteen or sixteen feet broad**, and at the time I first
looked at it, about nine feet deep; but it was said they dug it near **twenty feet deep**
afterwards in one part of it, **till they could go no deeper for the water**." Earlier pits had
taken "fifty or sixty bodies each"; the magistrates' order was "**to leave no bodies within six
feet of the surface**," and the water table stopped digging "at about seventeen or eighteen feet."
The great pit was finished 4 September, first used the 6th, and by the 20th — **two weeks — had
taken 1,114 bodies** and had to be filled. "**The mark of it also was many years to be seen in the
churchyard on the surface.**"

*(Honesty flag, important: Defoe's *Journal* was published in 1722 and is a work of fiction
constructed from records, bills of mortality and testimony; the narrator "H. F." is invented. The
**Orders** it reproduces are the genuine printed civic orders and can be relied on as text; the
**narrative details** — the pit's dimensions, the green rug, the keys unscrewed from locks — are
Defoe's reconstruction and should be treated as *excellent period-informed fiction*, not as
record. They are kept here because they are exactly the register a DM wants, and they are labelled
so nobody mistakes them for evidence.)*

## 5.8 THE FIVE-TO-TEN MOST GAME-RELEVANT FINDINGS

1. **THE ST ANTHONY'S PIG.** Ear slit by the market officer as a condemnation mark; a bell hung
   on it by the hospital's proctor; inviolable because of the bell; feeding on dunghills; learning
   to follow and whine at anyone who fed it; claimed by the hospital when fat; and a proverb about
   it in common speech. One animal carrying four institutions.
2. **The party wall is a fire regulation with numbers** — a foot and a half of land from each
   neighbour, three feet thick, sixteen feet high, tile not thatch, forfeiture for breach; and
   *everything else may be timber*. Site 10's signature buy has a legal reason to exist.
3. **The parish church is the fire station, the armoury and the treasury.** Long ladders, leather
   buckets, axes and a 30-foot firehook; "the arms in the steeple"; the commons' treasure inside,
   guarded by excommunication.
4. **Firefighting is demolition, and it needs a magistrate's authorisation** — a permission
   bottleneck that has already destroyed one real city.
5. **THE PLAGUE ORDERS ARE A DROP-IN ALTERNATE RULESET.** A red cross a foot long with "Lord, have
   mercy upon us" over it; two watchmen per house on day and night shifts who lock the door and
   take the key; sworn women searchers barred from all other work; a **three-foot red wand** that
   every plague official must carry visibly; burials only before sunrise or after sunset; six-foot
   graves; all plays, ballads and feasting forbidden; drink stops at nine.
6. **The raker's horn and the householder's swept frontage.** A recurring street sound that brings
   people to their doors, and a duty attached to owning a frontage.
7. **The ditch is a generational public work with a published wage ladder** — 7d / 6d / 5d a day
   by rank, and "every vagabond (for so were they termed) one penny the day, meat and drink, at
   charges of the city." Conscripted vagrants doing the filthiest civic job for a penny and food.
8. **Three windmills standing on a hill made of accumulated dung.** A real, sourced silhouette for
   the town's fringe.
9. **The bribe paid in dyed cloth** to escape a ditch-repair obligation — corruption in kind, at
   exactly the scale a Grounded hook needs.
10. **The pigs were the street cleaners, and banning them made the town filthier.** A civic
    improvement with a documented backfire.
11. **Kinsay's four transferable fire mechanics** — the stone strongroom inside a timber house;
    the **marked door** for a light burning after the lawful hour; goods evacuated by boat to
    islands; and the law that nobody but the owner and the watch may approach a fire.

## 5.9 DECLARED GAPS

- **Fitz-Elwyne's Assize of Buildings (1189) was not read in a scholarly edition.** The
  three-feet-thick / sixteen-feet-high / foot-and-a-half-each figures come from a web search that
  returned several consistent retellings of the same text (including a transcription site and a
  general history site), not from the London Record Society edition or from *British History
  Online*'s text, both of which appeared in the results and were not fetched. **These numbers are
  the most load-bearing thing in this lane for site 10's frontage, so they should be verified
  before use.**
- **Defoe is fiction.** Flagged in place, repeated here because it matters: the Orders are real,
  the narrative is not.
- **No medieval sanitation ordinance was read in the original.** Ernest Sabine's work on London
  latrines and cesspools, and Riley's *Memorials*, are the obvious sources and neither was
  obtained. Everything about privies, cesspits and their emptying comes from a single encyclopedia
  article of mixed sourcing.
- **Smell is almost entirely inferred.** The sources give me *what was in the street* (shambles
  waste, swine-cotes, skinners' pits, dunghills, a choked ditch) and the jury's word for it
  ("almost stinking and a nuisance," "smells very badly and is abominable to all men coming to
  market"), but no period text I read attempts to describe an urban smell directly. Any richer
  olfactory narration is extrapolation.
- **Nothing on urban death outside plague.** Ordinary burial practice, churchyard management,
  charnel houses, the passing bell, the gravedigger's trade, burial fees, and where the poor went
  are all missing. I greped for it and the corpus returned monuments and epitaphs, not process.
  This is a real hole given that "death and burial in town" is named in the brief.
- **Fire *frequency* is unquantified.** FitzStephen calls fire one of London's two plagues and
  Stow records individual fires by date, but I built no rate, no seasonality, and no typical
  extent.
- **Nothing on urban medicine or hospitals as institutions** beyond St Anthony's claiming its pigs
  and condemned meat being sent to leper houses.
