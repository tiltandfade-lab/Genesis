# Lane 1 — market halls and plaza typology

## 1. The market hall is four different buildings, and only two of them are the seed

Opening nine buildings settled something the phrase "market hall" hides: the type splits
into four families that fight very differently.

### A. The open post hall — posts, a roof, and nothing else
*Evidence: Ledbury Market House (1617), Questembert halle, Dunster Yarn Market.*

A roof carried on a forest of timber posts standing on stone plinths. **No walls at all at
ground level.** Ledbury is "supported by 16 oak posts" with "seven bays facing onto the High
Street" (Wikipedia) — which is a long, narrow object, roughly seven bays by one, not a
square pavilion. Questembert is the same idea at ten-plus bays, with the posts on tall
granite pedestals and a **low dry-stone dwarf wall running between the pedestals** — about
waist height, closing the bottom of each bay while leaving the top open.

At gameplay scale this family is remarkable: it is a **covered lattice**. You can see
through it, shoot through it, and move through it; the posts are one-cell cover at regular
spacing; the roof denies anything above. It is the only building type in the twelve golden
sites that is simultaneously interior and open.

### B. The arcaded stone hall — piers and arches
*Evidence: Chipping Campden Market Hall (1627, interior read), Shrewsbury Old Market Hall
(1596).*

The same idea in masonry: square piers on chamfered plinths carrying arches, five wide
arches on each side at Campden. The floor is cobbled; the roof is open timber trusses
overhead; timber benches sit against the piers. Shrewsbury is the two-storey version — one
big arched passage through the ground floor with an enclosed hall above, a clock and
heraldic panels in the gable end facing the square.

Piers read heavier than posts, so the arcaded hall is closer to real cover and blocks more
sightline. **A market hall is either a screen or a lattice depending on which of these two
you build, and that is a tactical choice, not a style choice.**

### C. The enclosed barn hall — walls, one huge roof
*Evidence: Halle de Saint-Pierre-sur-Dives.*

Stone walls, dormers, and an enormous single roof plane with a big timber door in the gable
end. This is a covered market that is genuinely a building — you go inside it, and from
outside it is a mass, not a lattice. It is a completely different tactical object from A
and B, and it is the one that would read as "institution" rather than "canopy."

### D. The free-standing arcaded block *in* the square
*Evidence: Kraków's Sukiennice (Cloth Hall), arcade read.*

A long two-storey building standing free in the middle of the market square, with a vaulted
public arcade running the whole length of each long side — columns on the outer side, shop
doors on the inner. **The hall does not sit at the edge of the square; it divides the square
into two streets and gives each of them its own frontage.** Big lanterns hang on chains down
the middle of the vault.

## 2. What the upper chamber actually is, and how you get up

Consistently across the evidence:

- The upper room's **first job is storage, not ceremony.** Ledbury's first floor "was
  initially designed as a storeroom for grain" and was only converted for civic use in the
  early 19th century. Wikipedia's market-house overview says the type is "sometimes combined
  with spaces for public or civic functions on the upper floors and often with a jail or
  lockup in the cellar or basement floor."
- **Access is an external stair.** Ledbury's first floor is "accessed by a wooden staircase
  at the north end of the building" — visible in the photograph as a timber flight climbing
  the outside of the frame. Shrewsbury's is internal but reached from the passage.
- **The upper floor jetties out over the pavement** at Ledbury — so the chamber is *wider*
  than the post grid below it, and the overhang shelters the market edge.
- Not every hall has one. Chipping Campden is single-storey with an open roof.

**Three free gameplay facts fall out of this without inventing anything:** the hall has a
grain loft (a room worth entering), an outside stair (an honest deck route), and — where
the lockup exists — a cellar (a room worth being locked in). Occupancy states could be read
straight off which of the three is in use.

## 3. Plaza-edge grammar: where the stalls actually go

The evidence is consistent and it is *not* "stalls fill the square."

- **Stalls hug the edge and the shelter.** At the open post halls the trading happens
  *under* the hall and immediately around its skirt; Dunster's low rubble plinth wall
  between the posts is itself a counter. At Questembert the dwarf wall does the same job.
- **The arcade is the permanent stall.** At Chinchón and Kraków the ground-floor arcade
  running round the plaza edge is where the shops are. The stall did not need to be built;
  the building's ground floor is the stall.
- **The centre is left open.** Chinchón's Plaza Mayor is a bare packed-earth field with
  nothing in it — the tables, awnings and goods are all pressed back under the galleries.
  Perugia's great piazza is a wide empty paved field with people crossing it.
- **The step is the seating.** Perugia's cathedral presents a broad flight of steps as one
  whole side of the square, and people sit on it in rows. A monumental stair facing a plaza
  is furniture as much as circulation.

## 4. How the centre object actually behaves — and this argues with the seed

The banked seed puts a market hall in the scene *and* a fountain at the plaza's centre. The
evidence says pick one, or place them so that only one claims the void.

- **Perugia's Fontana Maggiore does not stand at the geometric centre.** It stands close in
  against the cathedral steps, at the hinge between the two great buildings, and the rest of
  the piazza is empty. Physically it is a **two-tier polygonal basin on a low circular
  stepped plinth of about three steps** — call it 1h of plinth plus the basin mass. It is
  fenced today; historically it was not.
- **Chinchón has no centre object at all.** The void is the object.
- **Kraków's centre object is the hall itself** — the Sukiennice occupies the middle of the
  square and there is no fountain competing with it.
- **Bern puts the fountain in the roadway, not the square.** Kramgasse's Zähringer/
  Kreuzgass fountains stand in the middle of a wide arcaded *street*, one after another
  down its length: a tall polychrome column on a round stone basin at knee-to-waist height,
  planted with flowers, with the traffic passing on both sides.

**That last case is the useful one for site 10.** Genesis's town is a street slice, not a
square. Bern proves the fountain is a legitimate *street* object — it claims the middle of
a carriageway, forces movement around it, and gives the slice a centre without needing a
plaza to be its centre. It also comes with a free tactical fact: a waist-high stone basin
is `climb-cost` cover standing in the open, exactly where a street otherwise has nothing.

## 5. Plaza proportion — what I can and cannot say

I could not find a numeric rule attributable to Camillo Sitte himself. The proportional
rule the search surfaced is **Alberti's**, reported second-hand: a proper height for
buildings about a square is about one third of the breadth of the open area, or one sixth
at least.

Taking that at face value and converting: a plaza faced by three-storey buildings (about
30 ft, 12h) implies a breadth of roughly 90–180 ft, i.e. **18–36 cells across**. That is
larger than most trays will be. **Stated plainly: a historically-proportioned civic square
does not fit in a tactical tray.** The site is a slice, so what it can hold is a plaza
*edge*, a plaza *corner*, or a widening of the street — not a whole square. Chinchón and
Perugia are both far bigger than a battle map; Ledbury's market house sits in what is really
a wide street junction, and that scale *does* fit.

## 6. The relational grammar, stated for a composition compiler

Candidates only, in the guard-post idiom. Each is backed by at least two of the buildings
read.

| candidate | what it is | evidence |
|---|---|---|
| `COVERED_LATTICE` | a roofed volume whose ground floor is posts/piers only — passable, see-through, one-cell cover at regular spacing | Ledbury, Questembert, Campden, Dunster |
| `SKIRT_WALL` | a waist-high wall or plinth between the posts: counter, seat and low cover in one piece | Questembert, Dunster |
| `LOFT_OVER_LATTICE` | one enclosed room above the lattice, entered from outside | Ledbury, Shrewsbury |
| `EXTERNAL_FLIGHT` | a stair on the outside of a mass, the honest route to the loft/deck | Ledbury |
| `ISLAND_IN_THE_ROUTE` | the hall stands free with route on all sides, splitting one space into two | Ledbury, Shrewsbury, Sukiennice |
| `EDGE_ARCADE` | the plaza's own ground-floor arcade — permanent stalls without building stalls | Chinchón, Sukiennice |
| `GALLERY_TIER` | continuous open timber galleries stacked above the arcade, overlooking the void | Chinchón (two tiers) |
| `RESERVED_FIELD` | the plaza floor left empty, with all activity pressed to the edges | Chinchón, Perugia |
| `GRANDSTAND_STEP` | a broad monumental flight forming a whole edge, used as seating | Perugia |
| `ROADWAY_FOUNTAIN` | a basin standing in the carriageway, claiming the street's middle | Bern |
| `STEPPED_BASIN` | fountain as a low stepped plinth (~1h) plus a basin mass — climbable, not a wall | Perugia, Bern |

## Findings (plain English)

1. **"Market hall" is four different buildings.** Posts-and-roof (see-through), piers-and-
   arches (heavier, blocks sight), walls-and-one-big-roof (a real interior), and a
   free-standing arcaded block that splits the square in two. They fight completely
   differently and the choice is tactical, not decorative.
2. **The open post hall is the most interesting object in the whole site.** It is covered
   and open at the same time — a lattice of posts you can see, shoot and move through, with
   a roof denying everything above. Nothing else in the twelve golden sites does that.
3. **The hall is long and narrow, not square.** Ledbury is 16 posts in seven bays facing the
   street — roughly seven bays by one. It reads as a covered length of street, which is
   exactly what it is.
4. **The waist-high wall between the posts is the market's real furniture.** Questembert and
   Dunster both have it: it is counter, seat and low cover in one piece, and it costs one
   run of geometry.
5. **The upper room was a grain store before it was a council chamber**, it is reached by an
   outside stair, and the type often carries a lockup below. That is three rooms' worth of
   free story on one building — and an honest deck route for nothing.
6. **Stalls hug shelter and edges; they do not fill the square.** In every case the trading
   presses back under the hall's skirt or into the plaza's own arcade, and the middle stays
   open.
7. **The plaza's ground-floor arcade IS the stall row.** Chinchón and Kraków never built
   market stalls — the buildings' ground floors are the shops. That is a much cheaper way to
   get "market" than N stall props.
8. **Chinchón stacks two tiers of continuous timber gallery above that arcade** — the
   monastery study's "tiered galleries" chassis member, arriving at town scale, and it is
   the balcony deck as one continuous run rather than N separate balconies.
9. **The centre law survives, but the fountain is often not in the centre.** Perugia's great
   fountain stands against the cathedral steps with the piazza empty around it; Chinchón has
   no central object at all; Kraków's central object is the hall. The consistent rule is
   *one thing claims the void*, not *the thing sits at the middle*.
10. **Bern proves the fountain belongs in the street.** A basin standing in the middle of a
    wide arcaded carriageway, repeated down its length — which fits site 10's street-slice
    premise far better than a plaza fountain, and gives the open street a waist-high
    climbable object it otherwise lacks.
11. **A historically-proportioned civic square will not fit a tactical tray.** On Alberti's
    reported one-third rule, a square faced by three-storey buildings wants 18–36 cells
    across. Site 10 can hold a plaza *edge*, a *corner*, or a street widening — not a square.
12. **A broad flight of steps facing an open space is furniture.** Perugia's cathedral steps
    are the piazza's seating and its grandstand. If the institution stands above the plaza,
    its stair is the plaza's best piece of terrain.

## Declared gaps

- **No measured plan or section of any market hall.** Everything about proportion here is
  read off photographs plus the two facts Wikipedia states for Ledbury (16 posts, seven
  bays). I have **no bay width, no post spacing, no eaves height, and no floor area** for
  any hall in the set. Every grid conversion offered above is therefore a ratio, not a
  dimension.
- **The plaza proportion rule is second-hand and misattributed in my source.** It is
  Alberti's, reached via a blog summarising Sitte; I did not read Alberti or Sitte
  directly. Treat the 18–36 cell figure as an order of magnitude, not a number.
- **Chipping Campden was read from an interior view only**, and Kraków's Sukiennice from an
  arcade interior only. I did not see either building's relationship to its square with my
  own eyes; the Sukiennice-in-the-middle-of-the-square fact is from the file description and
  general knowledge, not from an image I opened.
- **No stall arrangement evidence from a working historic market.** Commons searches for
  historic market-stall photography returned nothing usable, so §3 argues from where the
  *architecture* puts the trading, not from photographs of stalls in use. This is the
  weakest link in the lane and it matters, because the seed's "functional stalls" are a
  named invention.
- **No French/Italian loggia dei mercanti, no Belgian cloth hall, no Ypres or Bruges.** The
  monumental civic-hall end of the type is unresearched.
- **Every photograph is modern** (except the Marville/Atget material, which belongs to lane
  2). Cars, tarmac, planters and modern shopfronts are in most frames. I read past them, but
  a modern conservation restoration can flatter a building's original clarity.
