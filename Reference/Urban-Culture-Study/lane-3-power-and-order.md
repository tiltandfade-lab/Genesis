# Lane 3 — Power and order

**Nothing here is locked. Adam rules; this file evidences.**

---

## 3.0 The one-sentence result

Urban authority in the period was **plural, overlapping and physically marked**: a town
typically contained several jurisdictions that did not agree, and the argument between them
was conducted in objects — a bell, a gate, a mace, a set of weights, a pillory — so **the
plaza's centre object is not decoration, it is the town's constitutional statement**, and
smashing it is a documented political act.

---

## 3.1 Nobody runs the town alone

The clean model of "the mayor and council govern the borough" is the exception. Green's
Winchester is the anatomy lesson, and every clause of it is a faction (Green, *Town Life in
the Fifteenth Century* I, PG 50392):

- The **Queens** held Winchester as their "morning gift," lived tax-free in the Queen's House,
  and "took rent and tolls from the row of Queen's stalls on the High Street." *A row of
  stalls on the main street belonging to a person outside the town's law.*
- At the east gate, "another belt of ecclesiastical property — the settlements of the
  Franciscans and Dominicans, — and next to them a group of poor houses depending on S.
  Swithun's."
- **In the middle of the town, opposite the Guild Hall**, the liberty of "Godbeate," "where
  the writ of the King or the authority of the city had no power; and whose church formed a
  sanctuary always open for ill-doers flying from municipal justice."
- **The gates were not the town's.** "The bishop had charge of one; and two were in the hands
  of the convent, which in times of civil war could freely admit within the city walls the
  armies of the side opposed to the townsfolk."
- The **bishop** took the river tolls, and owned the fair (lane 1 §1.7).
- The **King's chamberlains and the King's clerk of the market** "regulated business in their
  master's interest, and collected the dues of the market and tolls on every load carried by
  man or horse into the town."
- The **mayor** "almost alone among the mayors of English towns in the fifteenth century had
  to go to London to take his oath of office from the King's judges," and "might be imprisoned
  by the King for one offence, and punished by the bishop for another."

**That is six or seven competing authorities inside one wall, each with a physical footprint
you could put on a tray.** Green's own summary of the outcome is the useful design line:
"With the King or with a neighbouring lord the boroughs might make terms of peace, or impose
conditions as conquerors, but **their most imposing demonstrations were inevitably routed
before the power of the Church.**"

**Bridport** shows the small-borough version: burgesses assisted by "twelve jurors chosen
every Michaelmas, who yearly perambulated the town to watch over its boundaries, and who had
charge of the 'parish cheste' or coffin and the parish bier, **and of the pillory, whipping
post, and cucking stool**" (Green I, PG 50392). One jury owns the boundary, the coffin, the
bier, and the three punishment engines. **The instruments of shame are parish property with
named custodians.**

**Fordwich** shows the seigneurial version and its cruelty: the mayor could give sentence of
death and "order the prosecutor if he won his suit to carry the condemned criminal to the
'**Thefeswell**' and himself throw him into it with hands and feet tied, '**knebent**' as it
was called." The town prison was "a filthy hole of nine feet square which still exists"
(Green I, PG 50392). **A named drowning-pit; the winning plaintiff performs the execution
personally.** Meanwhile the Abbot owned the soil, "his bailiff lived within its walls and
presided over the Hundred Court which he summoned by his officer '**Cachepol**'; he had his
own prison."

**Nuremberg** shows the oligarchic version, and its committee structure is a ready-made
faction chart (Headlam, *The Story of Nuremberg*, PG 46401, note 7):

> "The new Council was to consist of **eight artisans and thirty-four patricians**. The
> artisans, however, only attended on special occasions. They had no real power. **The Guilds
> were suppressed**… Members of the Council had to be fathers of families. Of the thirty-four
> patrician members twenty-six were eligible for the office of Burgomeister. **Two by two, one
> old and the other young, they held office by turn for four weeks. One of the duties of the
> younger was to be present at the function of torturing prisoners.** The elder had to perform
> the office of asking the opinion of the house. From the thirteen older men **seven** were
> chosen as a secret committee. From this committee **three** were chosen, and from this
> triumvirate **two** were chosen, the chief of whom was the first person in the town."

Read it as a pyramid: 42 → 34 → 26 → 13 → 7 (secret) → 3 → 2 → 1. **A named, nested,
partly-secret ruling structure with a rotating two-week-ish magistracy and a torture duty
attached to the junior office.** Headlam also notes the patriciate was not a closed
blood-caste: "in the fourteenth century and later, even up to the beginning of the sixteenth
century, it happened that **foreign families settling here were at once accepted as eligible
to the Council**" — the door is open at the top and shut in the middle.

## 3.2 Livery, and the crime of belonging to somebody else

The town's central political anxiety in Green's account is not taxation; it is **clientage**.
Burghers who took a lord's livery were taking a second master.

- A borough decreed "that any burgher who put on the livery of a lord, or accepted his
  maintenance and protection, should be **blotted out of the book of burgesses**, and driven
  from their market-place and assembly hall, and 'that he come not among them in their
  congregations'" (Green I, PG 50392).
- **Exeter** supplies the theatre. The mayor, having ruled against the earl in a fish-market
  dispute, was summoned; "Followed by a tumultuous procession of 'his brethren and honest
  commons of the said city,' the mayor went from the Guild Hall to the earl's house, entered
  his lord's 'lodging chamber,' and **there took off his 'livery' coat and gave it back to the
  earl once for all**, the commons meanwhile beating at the door and loudly demanding their
  mayor, till the terrified earl entreated him to quiet their clamour. The town forthwith
  passed a law that no citizen should ever again wear 'foreigner's livery'" (Green I, PG
  50392).

**A coat handed back in a private chamber while a crowd hammers the door** is a complete
scene, and the mechanic underneath it — *wearing another faction's colours inside this town
is a punishable act* — is directly rollable as an arrival condition for a PC party.

The reciprocal indignity: at Exeter the Earls of Devonshire "forced the mayor to lay aside
his mace as he approached the suburb" (Green I). **A mace that must be lowered at a boundary
line** marks jurisdiction on the ground better than any signpost.

## 3.3 Disfranchisement — the town's own excommunication

The list of offences that cost a burgher his freedom, from Carlisle, is worth keeping whole
because it is a list of *what a town considers treason against itself*: "a refusal to help the
Mayor in keeping the peace, clamour and undue disturbance at the election of town officers,
**revealing the counsels of the Common Assembly**, resistance in word or deed to the
municipal officers, contempt of the Mayor's authority, or any offence for which the punishment
of the pillory or the tumbrill was adjudged." At Preston those "who betrayed the municipal
confidence **or exposed the poverty of the town**" lost the franchise, "and their toll was
taken every day as of forsworn and unworthy persons who could not be trusted beyond the
passing hour" (Green I, PG 50392, and notes 333–335).

Publication of the shame was itself the punishment: forfeiture "was declared by **open
proclamation of the common crier, or by sound of the town bell, or by having his name written
up on a Disfranchised Table in the Guild Hall**, so that all the town should know his shame."
And it was redeemable — at Hereford, a man pilloried or tumbrelled "by that means let him lose
his freedom; but afterwards **by the special favour of his bailiff and the commonalty he may
be redeemed**," a perjurer only "by the redemption of his goods and chattels **at least for
twice as much as he gave before**."

**Three findings in one paragraph:** (a) *exposing the poverty of the town* is an offence;
(b) shame is published by crier, bell, or a **named board in the guild hall**; (c)
disgrace has a **posted price** and can be bought back.

## 3.4 The punishment furniture — the plaza's centre objects

The engines are consistently named as a set: **pillory · tumbrel (tumbrill) · thew · stocks ·
cucking/ducking stool · whipping post · cage · gallows**. Their placement is documented.

**Stow's Cornhill conduit** is the best-attested single stack of civic furniture in the corpus
(Stow, *A Survey of London*, PG 42959):

> "Then was the well planked over, and **a strong prison made of timber called a cage, with a
> pair of stocks therein set upon it, and this was for night walkers. On the top of which cage
> was placed a pillory**, for the punishment of **bakers offending in the assize of bread, for
> millers stealing of corn at the mill, for bawds, scolds, and other offenders.**"

A well → planked over → a timber cage with stocks inside → a pillory on top of the cage. **A
three-storey punishment object standing on a water source, in a market street, next to the
old-clothes trade.** Stow then records that when the conduit was later enlarged, "the pillory
and cage being removed, they found the ground planked, and the well aforesaid worn out of
memory, which well they revived and restored to use — it is since made a pump; **they set the
pillory somewhat west from the well**." The furniture moves; the well is forgotten and
rediscovered under it.

**The pillory's clientele** is specified above: bakers who broke the assize, millers who stole
corn at the mill, bawds, scolds. Stow adds jurors: in 1468 "divers persons being common
jurors, such as at assizes were forsworn for rewards, or favour of parties, were judged to
ride from Newgate to the pillory in Cornhill, **with mitres of paper on their heads**, there to
stand, and from thence again to Newgate." In 1509 "Darby, Smith, and Simson, ringleaders of
false inquests in London, rode about the city **with their faces to the horse tails, and papers
on their heads**, and were set on the pillory in Cornhill, and after brought again to Newgate,
**where they died for very shame**."

**Punishment was legible — the sentence spelled out the crime in props:**

| offender | staging | source |
|---|---|---|
| **vintner selling corrupt wine** (John Penrose, 1364) | "sent to the pillory… **was made to drink publicly there his own stuff, had what he could not drink poured over his head**, and was besides **sentenced to renounce his trade for ever**" | Jusserand PG 58530, citing Riley, *Memorials of London*, p. 318 |
| **quack physician** (London) | "carried to the pillory **through the middle of the city, with trumpets and pipes**, on a horse without a saddle, **his parchment and a whetstone round his neck**, unseemly pottery hanging round his neck and down his back, **in token that he had lied**" | Jusserand PG 58530 |
| **quack doctor/astrologer** (St Alban's chronicler) | "set on a horse [barebacked] with **the beast's tail in his hand for a bridle**, and **two pots which in the vulgar tongue we call *Jordans* bound round his neck, with a whetstone in sign that he earned all this by his lies**; and thus he was led round the whole city" | Coulton PG 37277 |
| **fraudulent tradesmen** generally | "in the pillory, **with their putrid wares burning under their noses**" | Coulton PG 37277 |
| **baker breaking the assize** (Edward I, *Liber Albus*) | "**let him be drawn upon a hurdle from the Guildhall to his own house through the great street where there be most people assembled, and through the great streets which are most dirty, with the faulty loaf hanging from his neck**" | *Liber Albus*, as reported in secondary accounts of the Assize of Bread — see gap note §3.9 |
| **scolding wives** | "the somewhat milder '**thewe**'" | Coulton PG 37277 |
| **a gong farmer who dumped illegally** | "**put in one of his own pipes filled up to his neck with gong**, before being publicly displayed in Golden Lane" | Wikipedia, *Gong farmer* — weak sourcing, see §3.9 |

**The routing rule is the finding.** The baker is dragged *through the busiest street and then
through the dirtiest streets*. The quack is led *round the whole city* with trumpets and pipes.
Punishment in this world is a **procession with a specified route**, chosen to maximise
witnesses and humiliation — the exact structural twin of the festival procession in lane 6.
Coulton says as much: "**The scenic penances of the pillory and cucking-stool were among the
most popular spectacles in every town.**"

Whetstone and chamber pots hung round a liar's neck; a whetstone in particular "in sign that
he earned all this by his lies" — **a portable, legible symbol of the specific offence** — is
the meaning-bearing dressing prototype for the whole study.

## 3.5 The measure, the assize, and the economics of fines

**Weights and measures are the sovereignty object.** The bishop's fair-time right at
Winchester was "authority to take all weights and measures and bear them to the Pavilion and
there make assay" (lane 1 §1.7). At Leadenhall the argument was over where "the common beam"
should live and who should farm it (lane 2 §2.3). Marco Polo's Kinsay and the Islamic
*muhtasib* (lane 7) do the same thing with the same object. **Whoever holds the standard
weights holds the market.**

**The assize of bread and ale** tied the loaf's weight to the price of grain: "when the price
of grain increased, the weight of bread was reduced accordingly… when the quarter of wheat was
sold at twelve pence, the farthing loaf of the best white bread should weigh six pounds
sixteen shillings" (Wikipedia, *Assize of Bread and Ale*; the weight is expressed in the old
troy-derived usage where shillings are a unit of weight). Enforcement in the countryside was
by manorial lords "who held tri-weekly court sessions"; the law survived "until the beginning
of the 19th century." The same article gives the folk-etymology of the **baker's dozen**:
bakers "would include an extra loaf to ensure that they did not violate the law."

Where the assize was in a lord's hands, the whole apparatus went with it: "the assize of bread
was given to the lord's bailiff sitting with the provost of the town; **all suspected bread and
weights were to be seized by him**, the offenders to be fined in the lord's court, **all
punishments by tumbril or pillory inflicted by his orders, and all proceeds of fines given over
to him**" (Green I, PG 50392). **Fine revenue follows jurisdiction — so jurisdiction is worth
fighting for.**

**And now the honest part, which is the most useful finding in the lane.** The theoretical
ladder (fine → pillory → loss of freedom) was mostly *not* what happened. Coulton, summarising
Hudson's work on the Norwich records:

> "whereas the breach of certain civic regulations should nominally have been punished by a
> fine for the first offence, pillory for the second, and expulsion for the third, **yet in
> fact there was no pretence, in an ordinary way, of taking the law literally.** 'The price of
> ale was fixed according to the price of wheat. Almost every housewife of the leading
> families brewed ale and sold it to her neighbours, and **invariably charged more than the
> fixed price. The authorities evidently expected and wished this course to be taken, for
> these ladies were regularly presented and amerced every year for the same offence, paid
> their amercements and went away to go through the same process in the future as in the
> past.** Much the same course was pursued by other trades and occupations. Fishmongers,
> tanners, poulterers, cooks, etc., are fined wholesale year after year for breaking every
> by-law that [exists]'"
> (Coulton, *Chaucer and His England*, PG 37277, quoting Hudson's *Records of the City of
> Norwich*.)

**The fine is a licence fee. The "offence" is the tax.** And the corollary, with a number:
Coulton records that between 1347 and 1375 "there are only **23 cases of pillory** in all" in
London (PG 37277, note 142) — in a city he elsewhere sizes at 40,000 people. **Spectacular
punishment is rare and therefore memorable; routine punishment is a revenue line.** For a
game this is exactly the right calibration: the pillory is an *event*, not the default.

The other side of selective enforcement, from Green: "the actual result of the trade laws was
mainly to **give the rich wholesale merchant an additional advantage over the poor trader.**
Forestalling and regrating became the fashionable privilege of town councillors and magnates"
(Green II, PG 51637). **The law against cornering the market is enforced against the people
who cannot afford to corner it.**

Where the pillory *was* used in earnest was food safety: "Here we do indeed find the
theoretical horrors of the pillory inflicted in all their rigour, but not once a year among
the 40,000 people of London" (Coulton, PG 37277). The Norwich leet jurors' presentment is the
flavour: "**All the men of Sprowston sell sausages and puddings and knowingly buy measly pigs;
and they sell in Norwich market the aforesaid sausages and pigs, unfit for human**
[consumption]." And the disposal rule is remarkable — a Scottish Act of 1386: "**Gif ony man
brings to the market corrupt swine or salmond to be sauld, they sall be taken by the bailie,
and incontinent, without ony question, sall be sent to the leper folke; and, gif there be na
lepper folke, they sall be destroyed all utterlie**" (quoted in Coulton, PG 37277, note 143,
from Wheatley). Oxford had a parallel rule sending putrid meat and fish to St John's Hospital.
**Condemned food is redirected to the leper house or the hospital, and only destroyed if there
is no leper house.**

## 3.6 Tolls at the gate — the guard post's urban cousin

The brief asks for this explicitly, and the answer is that the gate toll was usually
**earmarked**: a temporary tax with a named building project attached.

- **Murage** — "a medieval tax levied primarily in England, Wales, and Ireland to fund
  construction and maintenance of defensive town walls," granted by the king "through letters
  patent for a limited term," collected "at town gates on goods entering the settlement for
  sale." Grants "were initially short-term, usually lasting a few years"; because "the walls
  were frequently not completed within the term, **the grant was periodically renewed**."
  Earliest on record: **Shrewsbury, 1220**, charging "4d. for a boatload of goods coming into
  the town, and then smaller derivatives for horse-loads, cows, pigs, goats, and sheep."
  Estimated burden: "under 1% of goods' value." Coventry's was **revoked in 1370 by Edward III
  due to economic harm.** Its siblings: **pavage** (street paving) and **pontage** (bridges).
  (Wikipedia, *Murage*.)
- Stow's London confirms the pattern with dates: Richard II "in the 10th of his reign, granted
  a toll to be taken of wares sold by water or by land, **for ten years, towards repairing of
  the wall and cleansing of the ditch**" (PG 42959).

**Design translation (proposal only):** a gate toll should carry *a purpose and an expiry*.
"The toll doubled this week" is a Grounded hook; "the toll is still being collected for a wall
that was finished forty years ago" is a Textured one; "the toll is collected for a wall that
was never begun" is Strange. All three are inside the historical envelope.

Tolls also discriminated. The Preston disfranchised paid toll "every day as of forsworn and
unworthy persons"; foreigners at Leadenhall paid more for storage than freemen; Green records
petitions praying "that the former laws may be put in force, ordering **strangers only to
dwell in the houses of Englishmen, to sell only in gross and not by retail, and to remain only
a month in any town after their first coming**" (Green II, PG 51637, note 205). **A one-month
residence cap on strangers, no retail, and compulsory lodging with a citizen** is an arrival
ruleset in three clauses.

## 3.7 Riot, and the objects that get destroyed

When a town fought its lord, the *targets* were the instruments of jurisdiction. Gloucester,
1331: "the mayor and burgesses gathered at the ringing of the common bell for an assault on a
Lord Thomas of Berkeley, **destroyed his tumbrill and pillory**, carried his bailiff to the
Guild Hall, and forced him to swear that he would never again execute any judgements in the
courts" (Green I, PG 50392). The dispute was then settled by "an opportune payment to the King
of £40."

**Smashing the pillory is the medieval equivalent of tearing down a flag.** Which makes the
plaza's centre object a *destructible political statement* — and its condition (new, worn,
recently rebuilt, conspicuously absent) a legible history.

The Flemish version is bloodier and shows the same instinct: at Ypres the rioters killed the
Baillie, imprisoned the magistrates in the belfry, and beheaded the Burgomaster and two
Sheriffs "in front of the Halle in the presence of their colleagues" — Omond's comment is the
keeper: "**It was by such stern deeds that the fierce democracy of the Flemish communes
preserved their rights**" (PG 18670). And at Bruges, Peter De Coninck, **Dean of the Guild of
Weavers**, "standing in front of the Belfry, with the leaders of five-and-twenty guilds around
him, he declaimed on liberty, and attacked the magistrates," was arrested and jailed in the
Bourg, "**But in a few hours the mob forced an entrance and released them**" (Omond, PG 18670).
He was later joined by John Breidel, **Dean of the Guild of Butchers**.

**A weaver and a butcher lead the rising.** That is the Flemish urban politics in one line and
it is a better faction pairing than anything invented.

The submission ritual is equally stageable. After a Southampton quarrel, the sentence was that
the prisoners "should be sent home, and at their coming to Hampton **sit in the open stocks
under the pillory, till the mayor and his brethren and the king's lieutenant walked down the
street, when the penitents were to plead for mercy and forgiveness and confess their guilt.**"
Which was done, and the mayor "of his great mercy, accepted the apology… 'And thereupponne [he]
commaunded them owt of the stokkes, and hadd them to the audite hous, and bound them by
obligacon to be good aberying ageynst the kinges grace and the mayor and his brethryn
hereafter, and so delyveryd them'" (Green II, PG 51637). **Public stocks → a scripted walk-past
by the magistrates → a rehearsed plea → release into a bond.** A whole submission ceremony, on
a plaza, with a timed entrance.

## 3.8 THE FIVE-TO-TEN MOST GAME-RELEVANT FINDINGS

1. **A town is normally several jurisdictions, not one.** Winchester had a bishop's gate, a
   convent's two gates, a queen's tax-free stall row on the high street, a sanctuary liberty
   opposite the guild hall, and a king's clerk of the market — all inside the wall. Site 10's
   occupancy axis can be *whose law runs here*, and the answer can be "more than one, and they
   disagree."
2. **THE PLAZA'S CENTRE OBJECT IS A CONSTITUTIONAL STATEMENT, AND IT GETS SMASHED.**
   Gloucester's burghers rang the common bell and destroyed the lord's tumbrel and pillory.
   Condition of the centre object (intact / new / rebuilt / conspicuously missing) is a legible
   political history.
3. **Punishment is a routed procession with props that spell out the crime.** The baker dragged
   on a hurdle "through the great street where there be most people assembled, and through the
   great streets which are most dirty," loaf round his neck; the vintner made to drink his own
   corrupt wine and have the rest poured over his head, then forbidden his trade for life; the
   quack with a whetstone and two chamber pots round his neck, led round the whole city with
   trumpets and pipes. Legible, portable, routed.
4. **The fine is a licence fee; the pillory is an event.** Norwich brewsters were amerced every
   single year and paid; London saw **23 pillory cases in 28 years**. Calibrate accordingly:
   routine graft is the background, spectacular punishment is the scene.
5. **Whoever holds the standard weights holds the market** — and at fair time they can be
   physically carried off to somebody else's court for assay.
6. **Gate tolls are earmarked and time-limited.** Murage grants ran for a few years, were
   renewed because the wall was never finished, and could be revoked for economic harm
   (Coventry 1370). "The toll is still funding a finished wall" is a hook that is merely true.
7. **Wearing another lord's livery is a punishable act of citizenship failure** — expulsion
   from the market-place and assembly hall, name struck from the book. And a mace must be
   lowered when crossing into another's liberty.
8. **Shame has a public board and a posted redemption price.** The Disfranchised Table in the
   guild hall; redemption at "twice as much as he gave before."
9. **Nuremberg's nested oligarchy** — 42 → 34 → 26 → 13 → **7 secret** → 3 → 2 → 1, with the
   junior burgomaster obliged to attend the torture of prisoners, and guilds abolished
   outright. A complete alternative to the guild-run town.
10. **Condemned food goes to the leper house, and is only destroyed if there is no leper
    house.** (Scotland, 1386; Oxford, 15th c.) A rule with a whole moral economy folded into it.

## 3.9 DECLARED GAPS

- **The *Liber Albus* baker's-hurdle passage was not read in the original.** It reached me
  through a web search returning several secondary retellings, all quoting the same wording.
  The phrasing is consistent across them and matches the period's style, but **it is
  quotation-of-quotation and should be verified in Riley's edition of the *Liber Albus* before
  it becomes load-bearing.**
- **The gong-farmer punishment story ("put in one of his own pipes filled up to his neck with
  gong") is weakly sourced** — it comes from an encyclopedia entry without a visible primary
  citation. Kept because it is vivid and plausible; flagged because it may be folklore. The
  same applies to the frequently-repeated tale of Richard the Raker drowning in a cesspit in
  1325.
- **No coroner's roll, leet roll, or court book was read directly.** Everything about
  presentments, amercements and juries here is filtered through Green, Coulton and Jusserand.
  The single highest-value follow-up for this lane is Riley's *Memorials of London and London
  Life* and Hudson's *Leet Jurisdiction in the City of Norwich*.
- **No exact fine schedule survives in this lane.** I have the *shape* of penalties
  (fine → pillory → disfranchisement) and a handful of sums (£40 to the King; 20s. for refusing
  incorporation; 4d. per boatload murage; 2s. per ton for gong-farming; 12d. a body for plague
  chirurgeons) but nothing like a tariff. "The exact fine for a false measure," which the brief
  names as a target, **was not recovered.**
- **The watch's establishment is undocumented here.** How many watchmen, on what rota, paid
  what, with what equipment, in an ordinary borough — unknown from these sources. The Midsummer
  *marching* watch is richly documented (lane 6), which is the ceremonial version, not the
  working one. This is a real hole for an occupancy roster.
- **Courts are named but not described.** Portmote, leet, piepowder, Hundred, Views of
  Frankpledge, the mayor's court — all appear as words; none was reconstructed as a scene
  (who sits where, who speaks, what the room looks like).
- **Continental and Islamic legal comparison is confined to lane 7.** The *muhtasib*'s summary
  jurisdiction is a much closer analogue to the European clerk of the market than this lane
  had space to develop.
