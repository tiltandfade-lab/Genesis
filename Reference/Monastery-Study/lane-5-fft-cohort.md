## 2. Orbonne Monastery (56) — what FFT actually built, and how it differs from the seed

This is the map the brief points at, and it is not what the banked seed describes.

**What is there.** A small green bench of ground with water wrapping its low edges and dark
tree canopy closing the top and bottom of frame. One **dominant vertical mass**: a tall stone
church block with a steep teal spire roof, standing at the **high** end. Running away from
its door, diagonally down the bench, a **broad raised stone causeway of large flagged slabs**
— a processional terrace with **arched openings in its flank** (an undercroft arcade beneath
the walk). At the **low** end, a **timber bridge over the water**, and timber fencing/palisade
sections at the edges. The plan view (56_0) reads as a big quiet grass field with one stone
band running through it.

**What that means, stated as relations:**

- **The composition is a single climbing route from a low water crossing to a high door.**
  Gate low, sanctuary high — the seed's core reading is *confirmed* at gameplay scale.
- **But there is no enclosed courtyard.** FFT's monastery exterior is a *causeway and a
  façade*, not a wrapped yard. The site's identity comes from **one raised path, one tall
  mass, and a lot of reserved quiet ground**.
- **The arcade appears under the walk, not around a yard.** The arched openings are in the
  causeway's retaining flank — which is precisely what lane 2 found the terraced sanctuary
  does (arcade on the terrace *face*, seen from below).
- **Ornament is concentrated on the portal**, exactly as the caravanserai finding predicts.
- **Water is the composed edge.** The stream/moat closes the plate at the low corners and
  supplies the reason the bridge exists.

**The transfer risk:** the seed asks for a walled compound with a wrapped arcade *and* a
stepped yard. FFT bought the same feeling with a fraction of the geometry — one causeway,
one façade, one bridge. If cost ever becomes the question, map 56 is the proof that the
monastery's *feeling* does not require the compound.

## 3. The raised platform is FFT's universal institutional move

Four different institutions, one piece:

- **62 (Chapel of Orbonne):** a rectangular raised platform running the length of the room,
  carpeted red, edged with a turned timber balustrade, reached by a **broad flight of stone
  steps** at the low end. Behind it, a polygonal end with a large traceried window. Tall
  lancets in a strict rhythm down the side walls; a banner pole for vertical punctuation.
- **51 (St. Murond Temple interior):** the fullest version — a full-width raised chancel
  across the room's end, reached by a **broad red-carpeted stair**, flanked by two stone
  plinths carrying **braziers**. Checkered tile floor, a long red carpet defining the axis,
  benches on the nave floor.
- **95 (Church):** the small version — two steps up to an altar table with candelabra, a
  central carpet aisle with a diamond medallion, and two banks of pews flanking it.
- **24 (Military Academy's Auditorium):** the school version — a **raised timber stage** with
  heraldic banners and red curtains behind, panelled walls, steps up.
- **63 (Golgorand Execution Site):** the punitive version — a raised timber scaffold
  platform standing in the middle of a grass yard.

**One noun, five dressings.** The deck ruling ("whoever holds the head of the yard commands
it") is doing the same job FFT's chancel does, and FFT proves it reads at tactical scale with
a height of about one tile plus a stair.

## 4. Repeated bays appear in three registers, and only one of them is architecture

- **Structural arch modules (59).** The Third Floor is a **grid of freestanding barrel-vault
  units** — a pair of piers carrying a vault, repeated on a regular lattice across a plank
  floor, some raised on 2–3-step plinths. The aisles *between* the units are the movement
  lattice, and the units are the cover. In plan (59_0) it reads as a checkerboard of solid
  cells with cross-shaped clear aisles.
- **Wall-niche arcading (58 lower level).** Repeated arched recesses in a retaining wall —
  articulation with no opening, the same move as the blind arcading at Easby (lane 1 §4).
- **Repeated furniture (57, 58 upper, 95, 96, 65, 33).** Bookcase bays, pew banks, tavern
  tables, storage racks, bunk frames. **This is where most of FFT's institutional repetition
  actually lives**, and it costs one prop × N rather than one building piece × N.

**The lesson for the repetition ruling:** the arcade bay earns the site's identity *from
outside*; but at the tactical scale where the player is standing, the repetition that
carries the room is furniture. Both matter, and they are different budgets.

## 5. Gate framing, at gameplay scale

- **63 (Golgorand)** is the clearest institutional gate in the corpus: a **single arched
  opening through a high pale stone wall, flanked by two hanging heraldic banners.** The
  banners are the ornament; the arch is plain. Inside, the wall's top is a **walkable
  walkway** reached by stairs from the yard.
- **36 (Church Outside of Town)** frames its door with the tower base and a short flight of
  steps — the ground rises toward the church, so the door is up.
- **3 (Hall of St. Murond)** frames its axis with **flanking white statues** and carpet
  runners rather than with an arch — a threshold made of props.
- **45 (Ruined church)** shows the endgame: the façade's great arch survives as a
  **freestanding screen** when everything behind it is gone. A good cheap ruin rule.

## 6. Roof families, read off the cohort

- **36** carries three at once: a **square tower with a flat-ish dark cap and corner
  pinnacles**, a **gable** over the nave, and **shed/lean-to** aisles down the flanks. That
  is the banked roof assignment (walkway → shed, row → gable, tower → flat deck) present in
  one FFT map, with the hip absent.
- **50** adds the **steep spire/pyramid** over the crossing and **twin west towers**.
- **56** uses a steep **pyramidal/hipped spire** as the single vertical signature.
- **91 (Thieves Fort)** is the flat-roof case: stacked cubic whitewashed blocks with small
  timber shed roofs — the closest FFT gets to the dzong/Himalayan silhouette.

**The hip roof (the banked debut) is not evidenced in this cohort.** FFT's institutional
buildings use gable, shed, flat and spire. That is not an argument against the hip; it is a
declared absence.

## 7. Terrain and quiet ground

- **56** and **36** both reserve **large areas of plain grass** with only a path crossing
  them, and both close the plate with trees and water. Consistent with the guard-post
  relational rule "quiet ground is active composition."
- **100 (Public Cemetery)** puts its repeated units — identical gravestones — across
  **terraced grass** with tall dark cypresses. The terracing is shallow and broad, exactly
  the "few broad masses" habit.
- **27 (Goland Coal City)**, included as a comparison, shows FFT's hillside settlement
  method: broad stepped platforms carrying gabled buildings, with one path climbing through.
  **If the monastery is to be a hillside site, this is the FFT idiom for it** — terraces as
  a few big benches, not a smooth slope.

## Findings (plain English)

1. **FFT's own monastery is not a cloister.** Orbonne's exterior map is a raised stone
   causeway climbing from a timber bridge over water to a tall spired church door, on a
   green bench with quiet grass and trees. One path, one mass, one crossing.
2. **It nevertheless confirms the seed's spine:** arrive low at a water crossing, climb a
   processional terrace, the sanctuary door is at the top. "Gate low, chapel high" is exactly
   what the map does.
3. **Orbonne puts its arcade under the causeway, not around a yard** — arched openings in
   the terrace's retaining flank. That independently matches what real terraced sanctuaries
   do, and it is the cheapest possible way to show an arcade on a hillside site.
4. **The raised platform is FFT's universal institutional piece.** Chapel chancel, temple
   chancel, church altar step, school stage, execution scaffold — one noun, five dressings,
   about one tile of height plus a stair.
5. **Map 59 is the repeated-bay proof:** a regular grid of freestanding barrel-vault modules
   on a plank floor, with the aisles between them as the movement lattice and the modules as
   cover. One piece × N, doing structure, cover and rhythm simultaneously.
6. **Most of FFT's institutional repetition is furniture, not architecture** — bookcases,
   pews, tables, racks, bunks. That is a much cheaper identity budget than an arcade, and it
   is what the player actually stands among.
7. **The institutional gate is a plain arch with heraldry hung beside it** (Golgorand). The
   ornament is cloth, not carving — which is a rollable, occupancy-swappable dressing.
8. **Golgorand also proves the walkable wall top at tactical scale**: a broad walkway on the
   wall, reached by stairs from the yard, overlooking a grass court with one central raised
   object.
9. **The ruin rule is "the façade survives as a screen"** (map 45): the great arch and the
   paired belfry openings stand freely after the building behind them has gone.
10. **FFT's hillside idiom is a few broad benches with one climbing path** (Goland, Public
    Cemetery), not a graded slope. If the monastery goes on a hill, this is the local
    precedent for how much terracing is enough.
11. **The hip roof is absent from the institutional cohort.** FFT uses gable, shed, flat deck
    and steep spire. The banked hip debut has no FFT support either way.
12. **Water and trees close the composed edge on both outdoor institutional maps** (56, 36) —
    the site is bounded by the things that are not the site.

## Declared gaps

- **The classification is name-primed.** All 121 ids were classified from `map.c` names;
  only 25 maps were actually looked at. An institutional interior hiding inside a
  castle-named or city-named id is a real residual risk (for example, the office and hall
  maps of the castle set may contain more chassis evidence).
- **Sampling is uneven.** Only two maps (56, 62) were read in all five views; most were read
  in a single oblique. Anything I say about a map's back side or its second rotation is
  therefore unverified for 23 of the 25.
- **I did not measure tile counts or elevations.** All "about one tile" statements are
  eyeball readings off 280 × 392 px images upscaled 2×, not measurements. If the raised-deck
  height matters numerically, it needs to come from a map inspection tool, not from these
  contact images.
- **No cross-check against the guard-post study's own cohort overview.** That analysis
  exists in the study archive and may already contain relevant institutional readings; this
  lane worked directly from the images instead.
