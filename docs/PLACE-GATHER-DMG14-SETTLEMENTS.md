## Ch.1 — Settlements (pp.15–17)

### Purpose & framing (p.15)

A settlement exists to facilitate the story; its purpose sets the detail budget. Three purpose tiers (p.15–16):

- **Local Color** — rest/resupply stop; needs only a name, a size, and one dash of sensory flavor; deepen only on return visits.
- **Home Base** — the campaign anchor; flesh out via the players' own NPC ties (mentors, family, favorite tavern), then leadership + law enforcement, information sources (sages, priests), merchants, and "a handful of wild cards" (shady dealer, mad prophet, retired mercenary, drunken rake) (p.16).
- **Adventure Site** — the settlement IS the adventure location (secret cult village, wererat-guild town, hobgoblin-conquered city); detail the intended adventure areas and the NPC cast (p.16).

Nine authoring questions on p.15: purpose in game; size/who lives there; look/smell/sound; who governs + other power-holders + larger state; defenses; where goods/services are found; prominent temples/organizations; fantastic distinguishing elements; why the characters care.

### Size categories (pp.16–17)

Most settlements are villages clustered around a larger town or city; villages feed towns/cities, towns/cities seat the governing/defending nobles (p.16).

| Category | Population | Government | Defense | Commerce | Organizations |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Village** (p.16) | Up to ~1,000 | Non-resident noble rules via an appointed resident agent (a reeve) who adjudicates disputes and collects taxes | Reeve may have a small soldier force; otherwise citizen militia | Basic supplies readily available (inn or trading post); other goods from traveling merchants | One or two temples/shrines; few or no other organizations |
| **Town** (p.17) | Up to ~6,000 | Resident noble rules, appoints a lord mayor for administration; elected town council represents the middle class | Noble commands a sizable professional army + personal bodyguards | Basic supplies ready; exotic goods/services harder to find; inns and taverns support travelers | Several temples plus merchant guilds and other organizations |
| **City** (p.17) | Up to ~25,000 | Resident noble presides with several other nobles sharing function; a lord mayor oversees administration; elected city council (middle class) may hold more real power; other power centers too | Army of professional soldiers, guards, town watch; each resident noble keeps small bodyguard force | Almost any goods or services readily available; many inns/taverns | Multitude of temples, guilds, and other organizations, some holding significant power |

Supporting prose (p.17): villages disperse population over farmland with a small clustered center (well, marketplace, small temple or two, gathering place, maybe an inn). Towns arise at road/waterway intersections, major trade-route meetings, strategic defensive spots, or significant mines/resources; town population is more diverse than a village's. Cities thrive amid large fertile arable land at a trade-accessible location, almost always on a navigable waterway; cities almost always have walls, and growth stages show as internal walls dividing wards (each with its own council representative and noble administrator). Cities above 25,000 are extremely rare (metropolis examples: Waterdeep, Sharn, Free City of Greyhawk).

### Atmosphere (p.17)

Settle on a single defining sensory factor that sums up the settlement's personality and extrapolate (the canal-city example). Climate/terrain, origin/inhabitants, government/political position, and commercial importance all bear on atmosphere.

### Government (pp.17–18)

Feudal default: power concentrated in towns/cities; nobles collect taxes in exchange for protection; commoner reeves/sheriffs/bailiffs serve as village agents chosen because they already hold local respect (p.18). In towns/cities, lords share authority with lesser nobles and middle-class representatives; a noble-born lord mayor heads council; wise rulers heed the council because middle-class economic power often outweighs hereditary authority. Even in a village, a popular individual (wise elder, well-liked farmer) can out-influence the reeve; town/city equivalents: a prominent temple, an independent guild, or an individual with magical power (p.18).

Definitions (pp.18–19), condensed to the system content:

- **Autocracy** — one hereditary ruler, absolute power; backed by bureaucracy/military or sole authority in an otherwise anarchic society; could be immortal/undead.
- **Bureaucracy** — departments each own an aspect of rule; heads answer to a figurehead autocrat or council.
- **Confederacy** — each city/town self-governs but contributes to a league/federation for (at least in theory) the common good; attitude toward the center varies by place.
- **Democracy** — citizens or elected representatives set law; a bureaucracy or military does day-to-day work via open-election positions.
- **Dictatorship** — one supreme ruler, absolute but not necessarily dynastic; otherwise autocracy-like.
- **Feudalism** — layers of lords and vassals; vassals provide soldiers or scutage (payment in lieu of military service), lords promise protection.
- **Gerontocracy** — elders preside; sometimes long-lived races (elves, dragons) entrusted with leadership.
- **Hierarchy** — feudal or bureaucratic government where every member except one is subordinate to another.
- **Kleptocracy** — groups/individuals ruling chiefly to enrich themselves at subjects' expense (bandit kingdoms, thieves'-guild-run realms).
- **Magocracy** — spellcaster governing body ruling directly (as oligarchs/feudal lords) or participating in a democracy/bureaucracy.
- **Matriarchy or Patriarchy** — rule by eldest or most important members of one gender (drow theocratic matriarchies as example).
- **Meritocracy** — the most intelligent and educated oversee society, often with a bureaucracy for day-to-day work.
- **Militocracy** — military leaders rule under martial law; possibly an elite soldier order, dragon riders, or sea princes.
- **Monarchy** — single hereditary sovereign whose powers are limited by law; heads a democracy, feudal state, or militocracy.
- **Oligarchy** — small number of absolute rulers share power (districts/provinces or joint rule); an adventuring party seizing a nation might form one.
- **Plutocracy** — rule by the wealthy: ruling council, purchased representation at a figurehead court, or default rule because money is the true power.
- **Republic** — government entrusted to representatives of an established electorate; any democracy with a restricted franchise (landowners/classes) counts.
- **Satrapy** — conquerors/foreign representatives rule the settlement as part of a larger empire; satraps are bureaucrats, military officers, or unusual characters/monsters.
- **Theocracy** — rule falls to a deity's direct agent(s); power centers on sacred sites.

### Commerce (p.19)

Even small villages sell adventuring basics (provisions, tents, backpacks, simple weapons); traveling merchants carry armor, martial weapons, more specialized gear — plus gossip, adventure hooks, and news/reports "that cry out for the attention of adventurers"; they hire guards against bandits/monsters. City-only services: libraries, dedicated sages, exotic-beast trainers, architects. Most villages have traveler inns ("even if the quality leaves much to be desired").

### Currency (pp.19–20) — adjacent section, captured for completeness

Standard gp/sp/cp/ep/pp terms can be reskinned per nation; countries mint their own coinage corresponding to the basic terms; nearly all currencies are accepted worldwide. Forgotten Realms worked example (Sembia's shaped coins, Waterdeep's toal 2 gp local / harbor moon 50 gp local–30 gp elsewhere, Silverymoon's electrum moon 1 gp local–1 ep elsewhere and eclipsed moon 5 ep local–2 ep outside, Cormyr's stamped royal coinage, cursed-feared Thay/Halruaa coinage, prized ancient Cormanthyr coins). **Trade bars** (p.20, mechanical values): 2-lb silver bar = 10 gp (~5×2×0.5 in); 5-lb silver bar = 25 gp (~6×2×1 in); 5-lb gold bar = 250 gp (size of a 2-lb silver bar); Mirabar's 2-lb black iron spindles = 10 gp in-city, far less elsewhere (iron normally 1 sp/lb). **Odd currency:** Gond bells = 10 gp trade / 20 gp to a Gond temple; Shaar ivory rings = 3 gp per slice. Design guidance: keep invented coin values simple ("don't invent a coin worth 1.62 gp") (p.20).

### Mapping a Settlement (p.114)

Concentrate on major features, not every building. Village: sketch roads (trade routes out + farm connectors in), note the village center, mark specific visited places. Town/city: major roads and waterways + surrounding terrain; outline walls; mark known-important features (lord's keep, significant temples). For cities: add internal walls and give each ward a personality; ward names reflect dominant trades (Tannery Square, Temple Row), geography (Hilltop, Riverside), or a dominant site (the Lords' Quarter).

### Urban Encounters (p.114) + Law and Order

Cities/towns can be as dangerous as a dungeon (sewers, alleys, slums, taverns, tenements, marketplaces as battlegrounds); adventurers must behave or draw the authorities.

**Law and Order** (p.114): whether a settlement has a police force depends on size and nature — lawful city = city watch + trained militia; frontier town may rely on adventurers or citizenry. **Trials:** overseen by magistrates or local lords; some argued with advocates presenting precedent and evidence (with or without spells/interrogation); others decided by trial by ordeal or trial by combat; overwhelming evidence lets a magistrate/lord skip to sentencing. **Sentences** (p.114): jails hold the accused awaiting trial, but few settlements have prisons for the convicted; a guilty person is usually fined, condemned to forced labor (several months to years), exiled, or executed, by magnitude of crime.

Entry mechanics/logic (pp.114–116), condensed — each entry is a short prose paragraph; the system-bearing content is:

- **Animals on the Loose** — one or more unexpected animals loose in the street (pack of baboons up to escaped circus bear/tiger/elephant).
- **Announcement** — herald/town crier/mad person proclaims: foreshadowing (e.g. public execution), mass information (new royal decree), or a dire omen/warning.
- **Brawl** — erupts nearby: tavern brawl, rival factions/families/gangs, or guards vs. criminals; PCs may be witnesses, hit by stray fire, or mistaken for one side.
- **Bullies** — **1d4 + 2** bullies harass an out-of-towner (commoner stats for all); a bully flees on taking any damage.
- **Companion** — a friendly local attaches to the party; twist option: a spy gathering information on them.
- **Contest** — party drawn into an impromptu contest, from an intellectual test to a drinking competition, or witnesses a duel.
- **Corpse** — party finds a humanoid corpse.
- **Draft** — a watch member drafts the party's help for an immediate problem; twist: a disguised criminal luring them into ambush (thug stats).
- **Drunk** — a tipsy drunk mistakes a random party member for someone else.
- **Fire** — breaks out; the party has a chance to help contain it before it spreads.
- **Found Trinket** — a random trinket, determined via the PHB Trinkets table.
- **Guard Harassment** — **1d4 + 1** guards eager to throw their weight around; if threatened they call out, attracting other guards or citizens.
- **Pickpocket** — a thief (spy stats) targets a random character; passive Perception ≥ the thief's Dexterity (Sleight of Hand) total catches the theft.
- **Procession** — citizens parading in celebration or forming a funeral procession.
- **Protest** — peaceful protest against a new law or decree; a handful of guards maintain order.
- **Runaway Cart** — horse-drawn wagon races through the streets; PCs must avoid; stopping it earns the pursuing owner's gratitude.
- **Shady Transaction** — witnessed between two cloaked figures.
- **Spectacle** — public entertainment: bard's impersonation of a royal, street circus, puppet show, flashy magic act, royal visit, or a public execution.
- **Urchin** — a street urchin gloms on and follows until frightened off.
