# Lane 6 — Urban touchstones at gameplay scale

**How to read the sourcing.** Every claim is tagged. "**[source]**" means a named published
source says it — the source is named inline. "**[inference]**" means it is my reading, not
anyone's statement. Where I could not reach a source, I say so in Declared gaps rather than
filling from memory.

The single most useful thing this lane found is not in Baldur's Gate 3. It is **Triangle
Strategy's "Wolffort Streets"** map, which is a documented street-and-market battle with
rooftops, a marketplace you can ask a shopkeeper to pack away, and traps set during a
pre-battle walk. That is the closest published thing to what golden site 10 is trying to be.

---

## 1. Baldur's Gate 3 — the Act 3 city

BG3 is the biggest turn-based city in the touchstone set, so it gets the most space. The
useful finding is that **BG3's most legible urban fights are not in the Lower City proper —
they are in Rivington and on Wyrm's Crossing**, the two approach areas, which are exactly
"a slice of town" rather than a whole town.

### 1a. Rivington — a road-bisected slice on rising ground

**[bg3.wiki]** Rivington is "bisected by the main road, dividing Rivington in two uneven
parts." Buildings named along that road include Arfur's Mansion at the southern entry,
Rivington General (a smithy and shop), the Open Hand Temple, and the Circus of the Last Days
adjacent to the temple.

**[bg3.wiki]** The ground rises. The wiki describes "a large open-air forge on a stone-paved
dais, which accommodates the building to the gradually raising level of the ground," and puts
the Gur Camp and the Abandoned Windmill on hills to the north.

**[bg3.wiki]** There is **a well at the centre of Rivington** (the wiki gives it coordinates
X: 10, Y: -16) and it descends to caves below.

**[bg3.wiki]** Documented combat in Rivington happens at the **South Span Checkpoint** (the
gate) and at the **Western Beach** (a smuggling fight), not in the middle of the street.

**[inference]** Rivington is the closest published thing to the banked "terrain-borne street
elevation" expression: one road entering and leaving, buildings stepping to follow rising
ground, a raised working dais as a legible platform, and a centre-of-town well that is also a
way down. It is a slice-of-town shape, not a town.

### 1b. Wyrm's Crossing — a bridge-town with a second street underneath

This is the richest single piece of layout documentation the lane found.

**[bg3.wiki]** Wyrm's Crossing "occupies the wide stone bridge that connects Rivington and the
Wyrm's Rock Fortress" and "resembles an urban area much more than Rivington." Buildings "form
its borders" and "all buildings in Wyrm's Crossing have a purpose."

**[bg3.wiki]** The named frontage is a real mixed street: Fraygo's Flophouse (three storeys,
west side), Sharess' Caress (three storeys, east side), Danthelon's Dancing Axe and The
Velveteen Elixir (two storeys, over "a vast cellar beneath the level of the bridge's
pavement"), Carm's Garms (clothier and dyer), and the South Span Checkpoint's fortified
wooden towers.

**[bg3.wiki, quoted]** "Most buildings have open wooden decks and scaffolds at the backside,
hanging somewhat precariously above the river; they can be used to move along the bridge and
gain access to most establishments through back doors or open parts of walls."

**[bg3.wiki]** The checkpoint gate can be bypassed "by a series of jumps or using most any
means of spatial movement" from a rocky cliff at a stated coordinate.

**[inference]** This is the anti-corridor move stated outright by a shipping game: **the
street has a parallel back lane at a different height, and every frontage building opens onto
both.** The bridge deck is the public street; the scaffold decks are the private street; the
cellar is below the pavement. Three levels on a bridge two buildings wide.

### 1c. Sharess' Caress — a frontage building that documents all three deck expressions

**[bg3.wiki]** It is "a three-storied building on the stone bridge" with "several lodgings
atop the archway connecting the two sides of the street." Entry points documented: the main
door on the east side; **"wooden balconies on upper stories" that make "approaching them even
easier from the street"**; and **a wooden ladder from the Elminster's Library balcony that
"leads to the roof."**

**[inference]** One building carries the banked town-deck ladder in full: balconies as the
frontage's own ledges, a ladder from a balcony to a roof, and lodgings bridging *over* the
street on an archway. That archway is worth stealing — it is how a roofline chain crosses from
one side of the street to the other without a plank.

### 1d. The Circus of the Last Days — a plaza fight that is documented as a shape

**[bg3.wiki, quoted]** The fairground is "a rough U-formed alley, with a row of traders and
performers lined along the one side and beasts' cages and the stage on the other. In the
farmost corner is the ringmaster's tent."

**[bg3.wiki]** Named contents: a traders' alley of merchant stalls and games (disguise kits, a
wheel game, a love test, a crystal shop, merchandise) on one side; a performers' alley with a
tent of dancing skeletons, animal cages holding a displacer beast and a dilophosaurus, and the
clown's comedy stage on the other.

**[bg3.wiki]** Entry is multiple: the main iron fence gate, gaps in the fencing, lockpickable
gates from the Open Hand Temple side, and cliff-access routes near the graveyard.

**[bg3.wiki]** The fight: on approaching the stage performance, the clown, his pet and the two
nearby beast tamers reveal themselves as doppelgangers, and they attack the party **and the
civilian patrons**. "On their first turn, Ryland and Keren set the caged beasts loose, and both
animals side with the attackers." Gamerant's guide adds that the two tamers are positioned to
the left of the stage and that the cutscene fires when the party approaches the audience.

**[bg3.wiki / Gamerant]** Both sources document that the cages are a **pre-battle lever**: you
can break the lock on the dinosaur's cage or feed and tame it beforehand to keep it out of the
fight, gated on Animal Handling and Investigation checks (Gamerant gives DC 15 for each).

**[inference]** This is the best documented plaza-fight grammar in the set. The plaza is a U:
two facing rows of dressed frontage and a closed end holding the boss's tent. The fight starts
at the focal object (the stage) with the crowd still in it. And the plaza's dressing is
*armed* — the cages are enemy reinforcements you can disarm before initiative by talking to
the town.

### 1e. Open Hand Temple — the institution as a courtyard block

**[bg3.wiki]** The Open Hand Temple sits in Rivington south of the South Span Checkpoint. It
"consists of a large main nave, a back annex for the infirmary, dormitory and kitchen, and an
inner courtyard."

**[bg3.wiki / TheGamer]** The documented fight is not in the nave. A secret hatch in the
kitchen leads to tunnels and a crypt below, and approaching the altar room down there starts an
unavoidable fight with shapeshifters.

**[inference]** Nave + service annex + inner courtyard + a way down from the service room is a
usable institutional chassis for golden site 10: the public room, the working room, the
enclosed yard, and the basement that carries the secret.

### 1f. Steel Watch Foundry — four documented ways in, one of them the roof

**[bg3.wiki]** Four entrances: the main gate across the fence; **an open skylight on the roof**;
two south-side passages (one unlocked but requiring jumps, one locked at DC 20); and a
ventilation shaft restricted to Small and Tiny characters that comes out in the antechamber
behind the main entrance.

**[bg3.wiki]** The perimeter is an iron fence with two gates, patrolled by three Steel Watchers
— one on the fence, one at the berth, **one on the roof**. Interior is three levels (main
floor, lab, control centre). Gamer Guides adds that you can climb the guard shed and jump the
fence.

**[inference]** The industrial institution's answer to the arrival hook is a *perimeter with
four holes in it*, and the roof is one of them. Note that the roof is both an entrance and a
guard post — the same surface is the defender's high ground and the attacker's back door.

### 1g. The Guildhall — the faction-seized institution as a scaffold stack

**[bg3.wiki]** The Guildhall is the Guild's underground headquarters, entered two ways: up
from the Lower City Sewers (manhole north of Basilisk Gate Barracks, then a DC 15 Perception
wall break and a DC 20 lockpick), or down from an above-ground entrance in the Brampton
district behind the Barren's Coop, gated on faction standing, a companion, dialogue checks, or
a Guild Ring bought in nearby shops.

**[bg3.wiki]** The main area is "a large cavernous space with multi-leveled scaffolding systems
connected by staircases, ladders, and rope nettings," with a bar on the north side, the
guildmaster's office and bedroom, and a secret vault behind a bookcase.

**[bg3.wiki]** The documented fight is a **coup**: the Zhentarim move on the Guild, and the
page frames it as "two sides, each itching to draw blades against the other," with the player
choosing a side.

**[inference]** This is the banked occupancy axis in a shipped game. The same building is the
Guild's home *and* the site of the faction seizure, and the fight is a two-faction standoff
inside it that the player tips. It also documents the honest-access law in an interior: the
verticality is scaffolding, stairs, ladders and rope netting — visible construction, not a
teleport.

### 1h. Civilians and crime — what BG3 actually does

**[bg3.wiki Crime page]** BG3 tracks crimes by severity (moving an owned object, vandalism,
trespass, theft, corpse desecration, pickpocketing, assault, murder) and by individual
character: "Crimes are committed individually by characters and punishments are usually meted
out similarly." Reactions vary by NPC — "Some NPC's are annoyed if they witness crime, reducing
their attitude to the offender, but take no action. Two NPC's might have different responses to
the same crime." And the search behaviour is documented: "if a civilian witnesses a crime, they
can report the crime to one or more guards, who will run over to search for the offending
character. If the culprit is nowhere to be found, they will investigate the area for a number of
turns before dispersing." Arrest, bribery, and skill-check escapes are documented outcomes.

**[Dexerto, Switchblade Gaming, SmoothFPS]** BG3 ships a graphics option called **Crowd
Density** which controls how many non-essential citizens populate city areas, and Act 3's
performance problems are widely attributed to the CPU cost of hundreds of scripted NPCs in the
Lower City; lowering Crowd Density is reported as the single highest-impact fix. Larian's
Hotfix #34 notes reducing framerate spikes in busy areas such as the Lower City.

**[inference]** Two lessons, and they point opposite ways. The good one: BG3 treats witnessing
as a *system* — a witness, a report, a search radius, and a timer — which is a mechanic Genesis
could carry without any crowd simulation at all. The cautionary one: **a dense civilian crowd
is the most expensive thing in a city scene**, expensive enough that the shipping game exposed
it as a slider and still shipped with a performance problem in that act.

### 1i. The Lower City proper — a documented absence

**[bg3.wiki]** The Lower City page lists 60-plus named sub-locations and five waypoints but
contains **no prose describing districts, streets, market squares, plazas, fountains, rooftops
or ladders**, and no open-street combat encounters. TheGamer separately reports a player
stacking 76 crates to see the whole district, describing homes and shops stretching "in all
directions until they're snug against the giant, towering city walls."

**[inference]** I take this as evidence about the shape of the problem, not about BG3's
quality: the Lower City is documented as a *place you are in*, not as a set of fightable
spaces. Its legible fights are named interiors. **The whole-town city did not produce
documented street battles; the slice-of-town approaches did.** That is a direct argument for
golden site 10's premise.

## 2. Divinity: Original Sin 2 — mostly a gap

**[Fextralife DOS2 wiki]** The Driftwood page functions as an index. It documents sub-areas
(the Black Bull Tavern) and NPC/merchant/quest lists, but it contains **no description of the
town square, market, docks, gates, elevation or bridges, and no documented combat encounter
inside the town itself**, nor any account of civilian or guard reaction to fighting. I am
recording this as an absence, not inferring a layout.

**[Fextralife DOS2 wiki, Combat page]** What is documented is the height system Larian carried
into BG3: units on higher ground get greater range and visibility and sometimes bonus damage,
and the UI shows the extra range as an additional green circle.

**[Fextralife / community guides]** Around Driftwood, documented encounters use vines to climb
to high ground and explosive barrels as environmental hazards; the Driftwood Arena has a high
ground position off to one side.

**[Steam community discussion — weak source, flagged]** For Act 4's city of Arx, players report
that paladins and townsfolk are separate factions, so attacking paladins does not turn the
civilians hostile. I could not confirm this from a wiki or a developer statement, so it is
**recorded but not relied on**.

**[inference]** DOS2's contribution to this lane is the height rule and the barrel, not town
design. The same-studio-earlier-game angle the brief hoped for is **not evidenced** in reachable
documentation.

## 3. Triangle Strategy — "Wolffort Streets" is the lane's best find

Triangle Strategy is the touchstone that actually shipped a documented street-and-market
battle. Chapter VII, Part II: "Not a Word, My Friend."

**[Game8]** The map's name is **"Wolffort Streets."** Victory condition: "Decimate the enemy
forces" — eliminate all enemies, not hold a point.

**[Game8]** Game8's guide names rooftops as key tactical positions "that players should
control to avoid enemy archer dominance," and names totem poles that "serve as choke points for
holding back reinforcements."

**[Neoseeker, via search result]** "A lot of the fighting in this map takes place in the narrow
streets between houses. One of the keys to winning this battle is to not let enemies control
the rooftops."

**[TheGamer]** The map is tiered — the guide refers to "the middle level" as a distinct area,
and places the three fire traps as: one near the stone stairs by the gate, one on the middle
level outside a house by the castle wall, one on the middle level next to a named unit. It also
documents a roof that ground units cannot reach: "set Hughette up on the roof of a house that
normal characters cannot reach" (Hughette is the flying hawk-rider).

**[Neoseeker via search / TheGamer]** Enemies arrive in **three reinforcement waves**; the
player deploys up to 10 units against 10 starting enemies.

### The market, and the civilian who packs it away

**[Fandom wiki summary via search, corroborated by Game8]** This is the finding. During the
**pre-battle exploration phase** you can walk the town and talk to people. Talking to the
scout Hughette establishes that another trap can be placed "should the market place be
cleared." Talking to a townsperson at the marketplace lets Serenoa **"convince him to pack up
their stalls to prevent the loss of their business."** Going back to Hughette then places a
fourth trap in the middle of the map. The wiki notes the fourth trap "covers a smaller area
relative to the others, [but] it is also fairly easy to bait enemy units into."

**[TheGamer / Game8]** Firing any of the traps has a story cost: activating even one Hawk
Statuette locks the player out of the Golden Route ending.

**[Neoseeker via search]** A unit named Jens can **build ladders** on the battlefield; guides
recommend him specifically on maps with cliffs and high ground, where "Jens can create
shortcuts that lead to higher ground," and pair the ladders with his nets and spring traps.

**[inference]** Wolffort Streets is, feature for feature, an argument for the banked town-deck
ladder:

1. **Narrow streets are the *lower* lane, and the roofline is the contested lane.** The guides
   do not say "hold the street"; they say do not let the enemy have the roofs. A street fight
   in this game is a fight about the surface above the street.
2. **Some roofs are honest-access, some are flier-only.** Stone stairs by the gate get you to
   the middle level; one roof is explicitly out of reach for walkers. That is a deliberate
   two-tier roofline — a walkable chain plus a few perches only the flier owns.
3. **A unit can manufacture the access.** Jens's ladder is the "honest route" law turned into
   a character ability. Genesis's version could be a cart, a crate stack or a shed the player
   *positions*.
4. **The stalls are a switch, not cover.** In this map the market is an obstruction that a
   civilian removes on request, converting the plaza's centre from clutter into a killing
   ground. The mechanic is not "stalls give +2 AC" — it is "the market is in the way of the
   trap until you ask the trader to move."
5. **The civilian is an interlocutor before the fight, not a body during it.** The
   pre-battle walk is where the town participates. That is a much cheaper and more interesting
   civilian model than crowd AI.

## 4. XCOM 2 — the street as an assembled cover set

The published GDC deck is "Plot and Parcel: Procedural Level Design in XCOM 2" by Firaxis lead
level designer Brian Hess (GDC 2018).

**[Hess GDC deck, via the DocsLib text mirror]** A **Plot** is the "Master layout map." A
**Parcel** is a streamed-in building component that fills designated spaces in the plot.
Parcels come in three sizes — "Small parcels - 12x12", "Medium parcels - 12x24", "Large
parcels - 24x24" — and plots contain designated spaces for each size; parcels can subdivide and
take random facing. XCOM 2 shipped with "80 plots" supported by "Over 200 Parcels and 450
PCPs." War of the Chosen added plot types including Abandoned Cities, Tunnels, Chosen
Strongholds and a Xenoform biome.

**[Hess GDC deck, via DocsLib]** **Plot Cover Parcels (PCP):** "Road networks converted into
PCP sets," where "each piece in a set had multiple variants" and "variants were randomly
selected on load."

**[Hess GDC deck, via DocsLib]** Documented failure modes during development: "Parcel sizes
aren't big enough, Subdivision isn't working, Parcels needed orientation guidelines," plus
"issues with variation" and problems from random starts. Later iterations added "Parcel
Facings, Driveways, Zones."

**[XCOM Wiki]** Cover is graded and destructible: low cover +20 Defense, high cover +40;
direction matters, so an enemy on an unprotected flank gets a clean shot; cover can be
destroyed by bullets or explosions and a unit without cover is Exposed. Some cover is sturdy
enough to survive weak explosives. Grenadier Demolition targets cover specifically; certain
heavy units destroy environment objects simply by walking through them.

**[XCOM Wiki]** In ADVENT Retaliation missions, 13 civilians are scattered across the map and
XCOM must rescue at least 6 while killing all hostiles; the mission does not start concealed
and has no turn limit; undiscovered enemy pods **collectively kill one civilian per turn**;
rescue is done by moving a soldier adjacent to a civilian.

**[inference]** Three things Genesis should take seriously here.

- **The single most transferable idea in the lane is "roads are a cover set."** XCOM did not
  author streets as empty pavement between authored buildings; it authored the *road* as a
  parcel type whose job is to carry cover, with multiple variants per piece. A Genesis street
  that is a bare 2-cell ribbon between frontages is the corridor problem; a street authored as
  a cover kit — carts, crates, water troughs, market debris, a stalled dray — is a fight.
- **XCOM's answer to "market stalls: cover, destructible, loot or obstacle?" is cover *and*
  destructible, and that pairing is what makes it interesting** — the stall is a promise that
  degrades. A stall that cannot be destroyed is just a wall.
- **XCOM's civilian model is a timer, not a simulation.** Civilians are stationary objects that
  die on a schedule and are rescued by touch. No pathing, no crowd. Cheap, legible, and it makes
  the clock the enemy.

## 5. Fire Emblem — the village as a structure with a fuse

**[Fire Emblem Wiki]** A village is "a type of building present on the map which is threatened
by enemy forces," and a side goal is to visit it with a unit to warn it and protect it from
pillage. **During the enemy phase, if a brigand ends its turn on a village entrance, the village
is destroyed** and its reward is lost. Visiting first closes the village's gates and safeguards
it. Rewards are an item, gold, or sometimes a new recruit. Brigands often appear as
reinforcements near villages specifically to pressure the player to get there first.

**[inference]** This is the oldest and cleanest civilian mechanic in tactics games and it does
not model a single civilian. A village is a **door tile with a timer and a prize.** Applied to
golden site 10: a market stall, a shrine, a strongbox in the hall, or the fountain itself could
each be a "visit before they get there" object, and the enemy's interest in destroying it is
what makes the plaza worth standing in.

## 6. Into the Breach — the city is the objective

**[PC Gamer, Game Developer, Subset Games]** The stated origin of the design is the observation
that superhero films destroy cities and nobody cares; Subset wanted a game where **protecting
the city is the task**. Civilian buildings power the player's mechs and must be defended;
every building that falls costs a point of Power Grid, and at zero the run ends. The grid is
8x8. Subset has said publicly that the "closest thing to our eureka moment" was cutting about
60% of the planned systems (city building, tech tree, persistent damage).

**[inference]** Into the Breach is the strongest available answer to "what do civilians do."
They are not units and they are not scenery — **they are the scoreboard.** The buildings sit on
grid cells, take up space, block movement, and losing them loses the game. If Genesis wants an
urban scene to feel different from a wilderness scene, this is the difference: in town, the
terrain can be *harmed*, and harming it costs the player something.

## 7. Hillside town — the one thing this lane could not find

The brief asked for a well-documented hillside or terraced town battle scene. **I did not find
one outside Baldur's Gate 3, and I am declaring that rather than papering over it.**

- **Rivington (BG3)** is the only terraced-town evidence with a real source: **[bg3.wiki]** the
  ground rises gradually, the forge sits on a stone-paved dais built to accommodate the rise,
  and the north end climbs to hills carrying the Gur Camp and the windmill.
- **Wyrm's Crossing (BG3)** is the bridge-town variant: the level change is the bridge deck
  against the river below, with scaffold decks and a sub-pavement cellar occupying that gap.
- **Kirkwall (Dragon Age II)** is a genuinely terraced city — Hightown above Lowtown, with
  stairs as the barrier between them — but the only sources I could reach for it were **fan
  blog posts and a forum thread**, discussing both the vertical separation and the game's
  heavily reused interior maps. **This is weak sourcing and I am not building anything on it.**
- **Tactics Ogre** town maps: the Tactics Ogre wiki map list refused automated fetch (HTTP 402),
  and the walkthroughs I could reach describe objectives and enemy composition but **no terrain,
  elevation or layout**. No evidence gathered.
- A general search for terraced/stepped town battle-map design analysis returned nothing
  citable.

**[inference]** The banked "San Francisco pattern" — a winding descending lane flanked by level
streets that overlook it — has **no touchstone backing it in this lane.** Triangle Strategy's
tiered Wolffort map is adjacent (a middle level reached by stone stairs, roofs above) but no
source describes a descending lane overlooked from above. If that expression is going to be
ruled, it should be ruled on its own merits or on architectural evidence from another lane, not
on a claimed precedent.

## 8. What this lane says to the market-hall + fountain seed

**Supports the seed:**

1. **A U-shaped plaza with dressed frontage on two sides and a focal structure at the closed
   end is a documented, shipped shape** — that is exactly what bg3.wiki describes the Circus as,
   and the fight starts at its focal object with the crowd still present. A market hall standing
   at the head of a plaza addressed by two rows of frontage is the same composition.
2. **An institution as nave + service annex + inner courtyard is documented** (Open Hand
   Temple, bg3.wiki). A market hall is that with the nave open-sided: the hall floor is the
   public room, the back annex is the weigh-house/counting room, the yard is the loading court.
3. **A single object at the centre of a town is documented at Rivington** — a well, at the
   centre, that also descends to caves. That is the centre law *and* a way down out of the
   centre object. A fountain reads the same way and can carry the same secret.
4. **Stalls have a documented job, and it is a switch** (Triangle Strategy: talk to the trader,
   the stalls pack away, the cleared ground becomes trap-ready). An open-sided market hall gives
   Genesis a legible place for those stalls to live and a legible thing that happens when they
   are struck or cleared.
5. **The frontage carries the deck honestly** (Sharess' Caress: balconies reachable from the
   street, a ladder from a balcony to the roof, lodgings bridging over the street on an
   archway). A market hall's own low roof is the natural first step of a roofline chain.

**Argues with the seed, or warns it:**

6. **An open-sided hall risks being a gazebo — a roof with no fight under it.** Nothing in the
   documentation shows an open-sided hall working as a combat space. What the documentation
   shows working is a plaza with *two facing rows of stuff* and a focal object. **[inference]**
   If the hall is open on all four sides it may read as a canopy rather than a building, and the
   fight will happen around it rather than in it. The Circus's U — closed at one end — is the
   safer plan: hall at the head, frontage down two sides, street open at the fourth.
7. **A fountain in the centre and a hall at the head is two centres.** The centre law says one
   object in a reserved centre. **[inference]** Either the hall is the head of the U and the
   fountain owns the centre, or the hall stands in the centre and the fountain moves to the
   frontage. Both are defensible; having both in the middle is not.
8. **Do not put a crowd in it.** BG3's Lower City is the shipped warning: hundreds of scripted
   citizens became a shipped graphics slider and a widely reported performance problem in that
   act. Triangle Strategy, Fire Emblem, XCOM and Into the Breach each solved civilians *without*
   simulating a crowd — as a pre-battle interlocutor, a door tile with a timer, a rescue token,
   and a scoreboard respectively.

## Findings (plain English)

1. **A street stops being a corridor when it has a second lane at a different height, and the
   documentation says so twice.** Wyrm's Crossing gives every frontage building a back deck over
   the river (bg3.wiki's words: decks and scaffolds "can be used to move along the bridge"), and
   Triangle Strategy's Wolffort map is won or lost on who holds the roofs above the narrow
   streets. Both put a walkable route *parallel to* the street, not just at its ends.

2. **The roofline is the contested lane, not a bonus perch.** The Triangle Strategy guides do
   not advise holding the street; they advise not letting the enemy hold the rooftops. That is
   the strongest available support for the banked roofline expression — but it also means the
   roofline has to be big enough and connected enough to be worth contesting, or the advice is
   meaningless.

3. **Roof access should be layered: honest routes for walkers, a couple of perches for fliers,
   and a way for a player to manufacture access.** Triangle Strategy documents all three — stone
   stairs to a middle level, a roof "normal characters cannot reach," and a unit whose ability is
   literally building a ladder. Sharess' Caress documents the CRPG version: balcony from the
   street, ladder from the balcony to the roof.

4. **Market stalls are documented as a switch and as destructible cover — not as loot.** The
   Triangle Strategy market is packed away on request by the shopkeeper, converting the plaza
   centre into trap-ready ground. XCOM's cover is graded (low/high) and destroyable by fire and
   explosion, with a whole ability line devoted to breaking it. Nothing in the documentation
   treats stalls primarily as containers.

5. **Nobody solves civilians by simulating a crowd, and BG3 is the cautionary case.** BG3 ships
   a Crowd Density setting and Act 3's Lower City is widely reported as CPU-bound on scripted
   NPCs. The four games that handle civilians *well* handle them as: someone you talk to before
   the fight (Triangle Strategy), a door tile with a fuse and a prize (Fire Emblem villages), a
   stationary rescue token on a per-turn death clock (XCOM Retaliation), or the scoreboard
   itself (Into the Breach).

6. **Every documented urban site in BG3 has three to four ways in, and one of them is usually
   the roof or the cellar.** Steel Watch Foundry: gate, roof skylight, two south passages, a
   vent. The Circus: main gate, fence gaps, temple-side gates, cliff route. The Guildhall: up
   from the sewers or down from the street. South Span Checkpoint: bribe, or five documented
   bypasses. **Two entrances is below the documented norm.**

7. **The plaza's centre object is real, and in the best examples it is also a door.** Rivington
   has a well at its centre that descends to caves. The Circus's focal object is the stage, and
   the fight is triggered by approaching it. **[inference]** A fountain that is only a fountain
   is scenery; a fountain that is a cistern lid, a body dump or the way into the undercroft earns
   its reserved cell.

8. **The plaza's dressing should be armed and disarmable before initiative.** The Circus's
   animal cages are enemy reinforcements you can neutralise beforehand with skill checks; the
   Wolffort traps are player reinforcements you unlock by talking to the town. Both make the
   pre-fight walk the real decision layer. That is the most reusable single pattern in this lane.

9. **Author the street itself as a cover kit.** XCOM's road networks were converted into "Plot
   Cover Parcels" with multiple randomised variants per piece — the street is a manufactured
   cover set, not the gap between buildings. For a procedural Genesis, that is the direct
   instruction: the street tile type carries its own furniture table.

10. **The whole-town city did not produce documented street battles; the slices did.** BG3's
    Lower City page has no layout prose and no documented open-street fights at all, while
    Rivington, Wyrm's Crossing and the Circus — each a slice with one road in and one road out —
    are documented in detail. That is independent support for golden site 10's core premise.

## The six questions, in one line each

- **(a) What makes a street fight good rather than a corridor?** A parallel lane at a different
  height (back decks or roofline), a street authored as a cover kit rather than bare pavement,
  and more than two ends. All three are documented — Wyrm's Crossing, XCOM's road PCPs, and the
  Circus's four entrances respectively.
- **(b) Do the touchstones use rooftops, and how do players get up?** Yes, and by stairs,
  balconies, ladders from balconies, back scaffolds, skylights, a jump from a guard shed, a
  buildable ladder, and flight — every documented route is a visible piece of construction.
- **(c) What do market stalls do mechanically?** Documented as a removable obstruction that a
  civilian clears on request (Triangle Strategy) and, by the general cover rules of the genre, as
  graded destructible cover (XCOM). Not documented as loot.
- **(d) What role do civilians play, and does anyone solve them well?** BG3 makes them witnesses
  in a crime system and ambient population with a performance cost; Triangle Strategy makes them
  pre-battle interlocutors who change the map; Fire Emblem makes them a door tile with a fuse;
  XCOM makes them a rescue token on a death clock; Into the Breach makes them the scoreboard.
  **Triangle Strategy and Into the Breach solve them best, and neither simulates a crowd.**
- **(e) How many ways in and out do documented town fights have?** Three to four is the norm in
  BG3's Act 3 sites, with a documented bypass for every gate; two would be below the norm.
- **(f) Is a central object used, and how?** Yes — Rivington's centre well (which is also a
  descent), the Circus's stage (which is the fight's trigger), and Wolffort's totem poles and
  Hawk Statuettes (which are chokepoints and traps). In every case the centre object *does
  something*; none of them is pure ornament.
