# Urban Study — synthesis

> **Authority note (2026-07-25 audit):** this is the research record and possibility
> census, not visual approval or an MVP shopping list. Current rule classes, evidence
> gaps, and Proof/MVP/Ideal scope live in `docs/GOLDEN-SITES-CATALOG.md`.

## 0. The one-paragraph answer

Adam's question was whether the urban tray was accounting for enough materials, and the
honest answer is **no — but the shortfall is not mostly materials.** The census found 54
demand slots, and only twelve of them are materials; eighteen are wear-and-message decals
(ruts, splash bands, gutter stains, worn thresholds, painted signs) and twenty-four are
props. The single biggest miss was a whole *bin*: a pre-industrial street is legible almost
entirely through where water, feet, wheels and smoke have been, and none of that is a
material. Three other things came out of the evidence that were not in the brief. **The
medieval shopfront is one hinged piece with two poses** — shut it is a blank panel, open it
folds down into a counter and up into an awning — which hands the site a day/night state, a
cover object and a market for the price of one asset. **The street lamp historically
belonged to the householder, not the town**, which means the causal light law arrives in
town already wired to the occupancy axis: count the lit brackets and you know who is home.
And **the third deck expression, the terrain-borne street, turned out to be the
best-evidenced of the three, not the most speculative** — four FFT trade cities build it,
three separate real traditions build it, and Edinburgh builds it by making one range's flat
roof the upper street's pavement. The one place the evidence argues hard with a banked
ruling is the golden seed: **a market hall and a central fountain are two centres**, and
every plaza in the study puts exactly one thing in its void.

**The three findings that shape the roster more than any single slot:**

1. **Density sits in a narrow belt against the wall** — footway, wall base, and the air above
   head height. Carriageway and plaza floor stay clear. The centre law and the quiet-ground
   rule survive urbanisation untouched.
2. **Vertical surfaces carry as much as horizontal ones** — goods hung on door jambs, rugs
   over openings, bills pasted on flank walls, laundry strung across the street. A town
   dressed only on its floor will read empty.
3. **Colour per building unit is the town's signature material fact.** One plaster family, a
   rolled colour per unit, and a shared-wall street stops reading as one wall — and because
   colour is the owner's last limewashing, colour, condition and occupancy become one fact.

## 2. The frontage-bay grammar

**The bay is 3 to 6 cells wide.** Burgage plots run 16–40 ft to the street (Alnwick's are
"roughly 28 feet wide, or an obvious fraction or multiple of 28 feet"), and 100–200 ft deep
— 20 to 40 cells, deeper than any tray. So the slice only ever shows the front few cells of
each plot, which honestly justifies never modelling the backs.

**The bay has two states, and they are the same geometry.** Two independent sources describe
the medieval shopfront as two horizontal shutters, "the top one folding up to double as an
awning or 'pet roof', and the lower one folding down to form the stall board."

| state | the bay is | the street gets |
|---|---|---|
| shut | one blank timber panel in a wide opening | a flat wall, night, a shut town |
| open | opening + counter board + tilted awning above | waist-high cover, an overhead plane, a place goods sit, market day |

**The geometry/paint split, per §5:**

| geometry | paint |
|---|---|
| the wide ground opening · stall board · awning plane · shutters (shut state) · jetty oversail · carriage arch · gallery deck + balustrade · external and gallery stairs · kerb run · chimney stack | fascia lettering · timber-frame pattern · cornice mouldings, console brackets, glazing bars · painted motif bands · shutter colour · every wear and stain decal |

**And four variation rules, each attested in both the historical and the FFT evidence:**
the frontage line wobbles a foot or two per unit · heights vary by about one storey, never
three · roof direction alternates gable-to-street and eaves-to-street within one run · the
ground storey is a different material from the storeys above, because the base takes the
wear and gets repaired in whatever was to hand.

## 3. THE HILLSIDE VERDICT

> **The ruled third deck expression holds, and holds better than its phrasing suggests.
> "A level street overlooking a descending one" is not a novelty — it is a whole class with
> at least three building traditions behind it (the stair street, the two-level street, and
> the street bridged over by another street), it is inventoried municipal infrastructure in
> the modern world (New York's DDC counts 64 step streets in the Bronx alone), and it is the
> deck FFT actually builds: four separate trade cities put a level paved street above a
> lower lane, held by masonry and joined by a stair. Lombard Street is the novelty. Its
> class is not.**

**What it costs.** Cheapest of the three decks in *new geometry* — it needs no new piece
family — and dearest in *plan area*, because slope eats cells and breaks the repeated-bay
trick. It also never needs a climb roll: a 30° walkable slope is a 58% grade, and San
Francisco's steepest legal street is 31.5%.

**The cheapest boundary is a building.** Six solutions were found, cheapest first: blind
retaining face → rubble parapet → kerbed planting bed → **a building whose flat roof IS the
upper pavement** (Edinburgh's Victoria Terrace, railed) → open iron railing (the low-poly
tax) → an arch carrying the upper street over the lower. **Site 10 is already buying
multi-storey street walls with flat-deck roofs.** Making one side's roof deck be the upper
street would deliver the ruled relation almost for free — and it would collapse decks 1 and
3 into one piece of construction.

**Two further results worth carrying.** Buildings **step, never ramp** — the wall runs
continuously, the doors jump, each on its own landing with a one-to-three-step stoop, which
is exactly Genesis's existing n×h rule. And the best junction between a stepped street and a
level one is **an arch cut under a building** (Matera's *Gradoni Sant'Antonio*): it uses a
piece the kit already owns, hides the descent until you are in it, and turns a level change
into a threshold you can hold.

**The camera caveat, stated plainly.** An overlook reads when you can see roofs in the near
field, an edge line with a beat of nothing behind it, the lower surface as a band, and a
visible connector — with the upper edge's shadow on the lower ground as the strongest cue.
The failure mode is real: a parapet at the bottom of frame hides the lower street and the
whole deck reads as a plain wall. Lane 3's overlook analysis comes from photographs taken by
people standing at overlooks, **not from a fixed diorama camera**, and should be re-tested
against FFT's own hillside maps before it is relied on.

## 4. Plaza and market-hall relational grammar

**The market hall is four different buildings.** Diagram: `market-hall-families.svg`.

| family | construction | how it fights |
|---|---|---|
| **A — open post hall** (Ledbury, Questembert, Dunster) | roof on a forest of timber posts on stone plinths; often a waist-high dwarf wall between them; no walls | a **covered lattice**: see through it, shoot through it, move through it; posts are one-cell cover at regular spacing; roof denies everything above. Nothing else in the twelve sites does this. |
| **B — arcaded stone hall** (Campden, Shrewsbury) | square piers carrying arches; five wide arches a side at Campden | a **screen**: heavier, blocks more sightline, closer to real cover |
| **C — enclosed barn hall** (St-Pierre-sur-Dives) | stone walls, dormers, one enormous roof | a **mass** — a real interior you enter, reads as institution not canopy |
| **D — free-standing arcaded block** (Kraków's Sukiennice) | a long two-storey building in the middle of the square with a public arcade down each long side | **splits one space into two streets** and gives each its own frontage |

**The upper chamber, consistently:** its first job was **storage** (Ledbury's first floor was
"initially designed as a storeroom for grain"), it is reached by an **external stair**, it
**jetties out over the pavement**, and the type often carries a **lockup in the cellar**.
Three rooms of free story on one building, plus an honest deck route, for nothing.

**Where the trading goes.** Stalls hug shelter and edges — under the hall's skirt, or in the
plaza's own ground-floor arcade. **In Chinchón and Kraków nobody built market stalls at all:
the buildings' ground floors are the shops.** The middle stays open in every case.

**And the plaza will not fit.** On the reported Albertian rule (buildings about a third of
the square's breadth), a square faced by three-storey buildings wants **18–36 cells across**.
Site 10 can hold a plaza *edge*, a *corner*, or a street widening. Not a square. Ledbury's
market house standing in a wide street junction is the scale that does fit — and it is a
better fit for "the site is a slice" anyway.

| **Occupancy states ride a standard axis** | The lighting finding makes this free: the default street lamp belongs to a specific frontage unit, so a shut unit is a dark lamp. Lane 6 adds that the best documented town fights arm and disarm their dressing *before* initiative. |
| **The frontage grammar is the module test's predicted missing part** | Lane 4's piece list produces the carriage arch again — the camp study's "gate arch with the control relation deleted" — now as an ordinary street bay. |

### Where the evidence pushes back

**(i) The golden seed has two centres.** The seed puts a market hall in the scene *and* a
fountain at the plaza's centre. Every plaza in the study puts exactly one thing in its void:
Perugia's great fountain stands hard against the cathedral steps with the piazza empty
around it; Chinchón has no central object at all; Kraków's central object *is* the hall.
Lane 6 reaches the same conclusion from the games side and calls it out explicitly. The
consistent rule is **one thing claims the void**, not *the thing sits at the middle*.
There is a clean resolution available and it is better for a street slice than a plaza
anyway: **Bern puts its fountains in the roadway**, one after another down a wide arcaded
street — a basin claiming the middle of a carriageway, forcing movement around it, and
giving the open street a waist-high climbable object it otherwise lacks.

**(ii) FFT has no market hall and no fountain.** Not one, anywhere in the corpus. Its markets
are open timber produce trays standing against walls and its one water object is a plain
well. That is not an argument against the seed — it means the seed is **inventing rather
than inheriting**, and it has to be argued from history and taste, not from the FFT
constitution.

**(iii) "Functional stalls" is the least-evidenced thing in the whole study.** Commons
searches for historic market-stall photography failed repeatedly. The stall is argued from
FFT's produce trays, from Atget's kiosk, from the halls' architecture, and from the shutter
mechanism — never from a photograph of a market day.

**(iv) The deck order may be backwards.** The ruling lists the roofline first, balconies
second, terrain third. The evidence ranks them **terrain (four FFT cities, three real
traditions, and a nearly-free construction) → roofline (built as a connected chain in three
FFT maps, and flat decks exist, but nothing in these images proves FFT fights on them) →
balconies (one clear FFT gallery; and three separate traditions build a *continuous railed
gallery* rather than separate balconies)**.

**(v) Deck 2 probably is not balconies.** Chester's Rows, Chinchón's stacked timber tiers and
FFT's Goland gallery all build **one continuous run serving every unit**, entered repeatedly
along its length by short flights. Separate balconies are the more expensive and less
evidenced way to the same deck.

**(vi) A fully enclosed street corridor is the shape FFT most avoids.** Its town maps usually
have **one** frontage and one open flank — the open side doing the quiet-ground work. Two
closed sides is a corridor, and lane 6 independently identifies the corridor as the failure
mode a street fight has to escape.

**(vii) Two ways in may be too few.** Every documented town fight in lane 6 has three to
four, and one is usually the roof or the cellar.

## 6. THE FOUNDER PACKET

Ten questions, plain English. Each is argued by evidence above; none is answered here.

**Q1 — Which market hall is the seed?** They are four different buildings and they fight
differently. **(A)** posts and a roof, no walls — a covered lattice you can see, shoot and
move through, with regularly spaced one-cell cover and a roof denying everything above;
nothing else in the twelve sites does that. **(B)** stone piers and arches — heavier, blocks
more sightline, closer to real cover. **(C)** walls and one huge roof — a real interior you
go inside, which reads as institution rather than canopy. **(D)** a free-standing arcaded
block standing in the square and splitting it into two streets. One warning from the games
side: an open-sided hall risks reading as a canopy with the fight happening *around* it
rather than in it. Which one, and is the choice fixed or rolled?

**Q2 — Where does the fountain go, now that the hall is also a centre?** The seed has a
market hall and a fountain in the plaza's middle, and that is two things claiming one void
— every real plaza in the study puts exactly one thing there. Three ways out: the hall *is*
the centre and the fountain moves to a corner or against the institution's steps (Perugia's
own answer); the fountain is the centre and the hall stands at the plaza's edge; or —
the option that fits a street slice best — **the fountain stands in the roadway**, the way
Bern's do, claiming the middle of the street rather than the middle of a square, forcing
movement around it and giving the open street a waist-high climbable object.

**Q3 — Which stall, out of five?** This is the least-evidenced thing in the study and it is a
named invention, so it is worth choosing deliberately. **(a)** The plaza's own ground-floor
**arcade** — Chinchón and Kraków never built stalls, the buildings' ground floors are the
shops, and this costs nothing. **(b)** The **produce tray** — an open timber box at waist
height filled with goods as coloured mass; FFT's exact object, and it reads even in plan.
**(c)** The **shutter-stall** — free with the frontage, if Q4 goes that way. **(d)** A
**trestle** with a cloth. **(e)** A **lean-to box** projecting from a wall. Which are in, and
does the market hall's skirt wall count as a sixth?

**Q4 — Adopt the two-state shutter as the frontage's signature mechanism?** Two independent
sources say the medieval shop's opening was closed by two horizontal shutters: the upper
hooks up to make an awning, the lower folds down to make the counter. One hinge, two poses.
Shut, the bay is a blank panel — night, a shut town, no interaction. Open, it is a
waist-high counter with an overhead plane and goods on it. That is a day/night state, a
cover object, a market and an occupancy tell for the price of one asset, and it is
historically exact rather than invented. Adopt it as the bay's core mechanism?

**Q5 — Is the frontage bay 3 to 6 cells, and do the four variation rules become law?** Real
burgage plots run 16–40 ft to the street, with 28 ft common — 3 to 6 cells, which lands on
the grid without fudging. The four variation rules the evidence agrees on: the frontage line
wobbles a foot or two per unit; heights vary by about one storey and never three; roof
direction alternates gable-to-street and eaves-to-street inside one run; and the ground
storey is a different material from the storeys above. Those four are what stop a street wall
reading as one wall. Confirm the module and promote the rules, or hold them as defaults?

**Q6 — Is deck 2 a continuous gallery rather than balconies?** Chester's Rows, Chinchón's two
stacked timber tiers, and FFT's own Goland gallery all build **one continuous railed walkway
serving every unit**, entered by short flights at intervals along its length — not N separate
ledges. It is cheaper, better evidenced, and it gives the deck a real route instead of a set
of perches. Does the balcony become a gallery run, with individual balconies as the rarer
variant?

**Q7 — Does one side's roof deck double as the upper street?** This is the biggest cost
saving in the study. At Edinburgh's Victoria Terrace the level upper street is literally the
flat roof of the shop range below it, edged with a railing. Site 10 is already buying
multi-storey street walls, and the kit already builds flat decks with parapets. Making one
range's roof *be* the upper pavement would deliver the ruled terrain-street relation almost
for free — and it would collapse decks 1 and 3 into a single piece of construction. Take it,
or keep the roofline and the terrain street as separate things?

**Q8 — Does the street lamp belong to the house?** Historically it did: London ordered
householders to hang lanterns on winter nights from 1417, and Paris ordered street-facing
windows to show a light. That makes the causal light law arrive in town already wired to the
occupancy axis — the default urban lamp is bolted to a specific frontage unit, so a shut,
abandoned or seized unit is a dark lamp, and a street where only the corrupt watch-house
burns is something the engine can produce from facts it already rolls. It also gives a second
free reading: **a lamp on a free-standing post is a civic light, so it only exists where an
authority functions.** Adopt both, or just the first?

**Q9 — Rolled limewash colour per building unit?** Every rendered street in the evidence
reads as many owners because each unit is a different colour of the same material — ochre
next to pink next to cream next to bare grey. It is one material family with a rolled colour
per unit, it costs almost nothing, and it is causal rather than decorative: a unit's colour
is its owner's last limewashing, so colour, condition and occupancy become the same fact and
the abandoned unit is the grey one. One caution: the only colour evidence in the study is a
hand-tinted photochrom, so the *relation* is well evidenced and the *palette* is not.

**Q10 — How many ways in and out?** The capsule says the street enters one side and leaves
the other, which is two. Every documented town fight in the touchstone lane has three or
four, and one of them is usually the roof or the cellar — and the market hall's own type
comes with a grain loft up an outside stair and often a lockup below. Does the slice carry
two honest street ends plus one vertical way (a roof, a gallery, a cellar), or does two
stay the rule?

## 7. What this study could not do

- **It could not photograph its own central finding.** Lane 4's two-state shutter — the best
  single mechanism in the study — rests on two text sources and no image. Three surviving
  reconstructions are named by one of those sources (the Weald & Downland 15th-century shops,
  Gladstone's Land, the Tewkesbury Merchant's House) and **none of them was looked at.**
- **No measured drawing, plan or section, anywhere, for anything.** No bay width, no post
  spacing, no eaves height, no tread and riser, no market-hall floor area, no Chester Rows
  section. Every dimension offered is either a cited textual figure or an explicit eyeball
  read, and each lane says which.
- **It found no photograph of a working historic market.** Commons searches failed
  repeatedly. The stall slots are argued from architecture, from FFT, and from the shutter
  mechanism, never from a market day. This is the weakest link and it sits under a named
  invention.
- **The census is a census of the evidence gathered, not of the pre-industrial street.**
  Trade-specific fixtures, market regulation objects (the standard measure, the pillory, the
  tron), civic apparatus (fire hooks, the watch box) and most religious street furniture are
  known to be missing and were not evidenced.
- **Almost all the photographic evidence is nineteenth-century.** Marville (1865), Annan
  (1868–77), Thomson (1877), the Naples photochrom (1890s) and Atget (1910–11) document a
  pre-industrial fabric photographed late. Someone has to decide how late the town's dressing
  is allowed to look.
- **No colour data.** One hand-tinted photochrom is the entire colour evidence base.
- **The FFT classification is name-primed for 108 of 121 ids**, 21 images were read across 13
  ids, and **nothing in the corpus tells you what is walkable** — the renders carry no unit
  tokens and no height data. Every FFT statement about rooftops and galleries is about what is
  *built*, not what can be stood on.
- **Lane 6 observed nothing in-game and lost five fetches** (two HTTP 402, two HTTP 403, one
  over the size limit). Its BG3 readings are the wiki's account, not observation. It found
  **no hillside-town battle scene documented anywhere** outside one BG3 location, and **no
  developer postmortem on town or market combat design at all**.
- **Lane 3's overlook analysis is from photographs taken by people standing at overlooks**,
  not from a fixed diorama camera. It should be re-tested against FFT's hillside maps before
  anything depends on it. Its Italian/Anglo-Scottish sample is also probably not
  representative — Guanajuato, Lisbon, the Greek islands and Perugia all went uncovered.
- **Party-wall construction is unresolved.** Whether two adjacent timber frames share one
  wall or stand back to back is a real construction question for a kit, and this study
  answers it from street elevations only.
- **Balcony construction is unresearched**, and no interior dressing census was done beyond
  one FFT pub.
- **The bin assignments in the roster are proposals, not tests.** Whether a pilaster, a stall
  board, a guard stone or a railing is geometry or paint depends on whether it changes play
  at the grid's resolution — and that is a clay question, not a research one.
