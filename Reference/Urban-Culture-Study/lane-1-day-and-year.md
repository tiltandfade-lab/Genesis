# Lane 1 — The day and the year

**Nothing here is locked. Adam rules; this file evidences.** This is a reading lane: it keeps
the exact hour, the exact bell, the exact fine, because the whole point of the study is that
Genesis can mine specifics for years. Where a claim is thin or the source is late/derivative,
the text says so in place rather than in a footnote.

---

## 1.0 The one-sentence result

A pre-industrial town did not run on a clock; it ran on a **sequence of sounds owned by
somebody**, and almost every social fact you would want at a site — who is present, what is
legal right now, whether the gate is open, whether the stranger is a guest or a suspect —
is downstream of *which signal has last been given and who had the right to give it*.

That sentence has a design consequence worth stating up front: **time of day in an urban
site is not a lighting parameter. It is a rules parameter.** The same plaza at the market
bell and at curfew is not the same place with different shadows; it is a different legal
regime with a different population, different permitted actions, and different consequences
for being there.

---

## 1.1 The bell is the clock, and the bell has an owner

The town's time was kept by a bell, and the bell's ownership was a political fact, not a
municipal convenience.

In the French communes the point was explicit and constitutional: **"the right to have a
belfry and a town hall were given by charter when the commune was established, and were
taken away when it was suppressed"**, and "the bell-tower often formed the town prison."
In England the arrangement was quieter but not neutral — "the town clock and the assembly
and curfew bells in almost all cases were set in the tower of the parish church, and the
ringers paid by the corporation" (Alice Stopford Green, *Town Life in the Fifteenth
Century*, vol. I, PG 50392, note 286).

The consequence when ownership was contested is a ready-made site premise. At Winchester,
the liberty of "Godbeate" belonging to St Swithun's sat **"right in the middle of the town,
opposite the Guild Hall in the High Street"**, a patch where "the writ of the King or the
authority of the city had no power," with a church that "formed a sanctuary always open for
ill-doers flying from municipal justice." Green's summary sentence is the whole idea in one
line: **"The very curfew-bell which hung in its tower rang out from land that defied the
mayor's authority."** (Green I, PG 50392.)

Ypres shows the other end of the same fact. When the town rose, the crowd "rushed into the
council chamber, seized the other magistrates, and locked them up in the belfry, where they
remained prisoners for some days," and then beheaded the Burgomaster and two Sheriffs "in
front of the Halle in the presence of their colleagues" (George W. T. Omond, *Bruges and
West Flanders*, PG 18670). The belfry is simultaneously the clock, the treasury of the
charter, the watchtower, and the gaol — a single object doing four jobs.

At Bruges the belfry's working function is described directly: **"In olden times watchmen
were always on duty on the Belfry to give warning if enemies approached or fire broke out
in any part of the town, a constant source of danger when most of the houses were built of
wood."** And, as observed c. 1905 and presented as continuous custom: "All day, from year's
end to year's end, the chimes ring every quarter of an hour; and all night, too, during the
wildest storms of winter" (Omond, PG 18670).

**Bell inventory, from the evidence:** curfew bell · assembly/common bell (summons to the
Common Assembly) · market bell · alarm/fire bell · the quarter chimes · the passing bell for
a death · the church's own office bells. Green records a Gloucester incident where "the
mayor and burgesses gathered at the ringing of the common bell for an assault on a Lord
Thomas of Berkeley, destroyed his tumbrill and pillory, carried his bailiff to the Guild
Hall, and forced him to swear that he would never again execute any judgements in the
courts" (Green I, PG 50392). **The common bell is a riot trigger with a legal face.**

A poverty detail worth keeping, because it shows how expensive the sound was: Totnes in 1449
was so poor "there were only three people in the town who paid as much as twentypence" of
the half-tenths and fifteenths, and yet, having four new bells "anointed and consecrated in
1442," it resolved to replace the wooden belfry with a stone tower — appointing a master
mason in 1448 and sending "supervisors" **"to visit the bell towers of all the country round
and to make that at Totnes 'according to the best model.'"** Parishioners were called out to
dig stone from the quarry, everyone with a horse to help carry, and "those who have no
horses of their own are to work with the horses of other persons, but at their own cost";
finally the mayor, vicar and proctors went round each parishioner to record what he would
give, **"and those who contributed nothing were to have their names entered on a roll and
sent to the Archdeacon's Court"** (Green I, PG 50392). A defaulters' roll is a hook.

## 1.2 Dawn — the gate, the herd, the first cries

**The gate.** The gate's schedule was formal and the enforcement was slack, which is exactly
the interesting shape. Coulton states it plainly: **"Nominally, the great gate was shut at
the hour of sunset, and only the wicket-gate left open till curfew; but regulations of this
kind were generally interpreted with a good deal of laxity"** (G. G. Coulton, *Chaucer and
His England*, PG 37277, note 117). Two doors, two hours, and an understood tolerance — the
wicket in the great gate is a design object and a social object at once.

Winchester again gives the ownership variant: "Winchester had not even control of its own
gates. The bishop had charge of one; and two were in the hands of the convent, which in
times of civil war could freely admit within the city walls the armies of the side opposed
to the townsfolk" (Green I, PG 50392). **Who holds which gate is a faction fact.**

**The herd.** The single best dawn image in the corpus is agricultural and domestic at once:
the freeman "opened the door of his yard in the early morning when the common herd went
round the streets to collect the swine and drive them out on the moor till evening" (Green
I, PG 50392). A town's morning therefore begins with a *municipal animal round*: a hired
herd walking a fixed route, doors opening ahead of him, pigs joining the column, and the
whole thing reversing at dusk. This is an occupancy roster and a sound cue and a reason for
the street to be briefly impassable, all in one custom.

**The first traders.** The pre-dawn buyer is a criminal category. Coulton quotes the
complaint that a forestaller was "out at cockcrow to buy privately when the citizens were at
Mass, so that by six o'clock, there was nothing left in the market for the good folk of the
town" (Coulton, PG 37277, quoting Green, *Town Life* II). **Cockcrow-to-six is the illegal
trading window**; the market's own opening signal is what makes it illegal.

**The labourers' corner.** Stow records a standing hiring pitch: "labourers every work-day
are to be found in Cheape, about Soper's lane end" (John Stow, *A Survey of London*, PG
42959, "Of Orders and Customs"). A named corner where day-labour stands and waits is a
permanent morning occupancy fixture, distinct from the market itself.

## 1.3 The market's hour, and the market as a temporary legal regime

FitzStephen's twelfth-century description of London, which Stow reprints, gives the
governing sentence for the working day: **"Men of all trades, sellers of all sorts of wares,
labourers in every work, every morning are in their distinct and several places"** (Stow, PG
42959, "Of Orders and Customs"). The morning *sorts* the town: each trade to its own ground.

Two things follow that matter for a market-hall site.

**(a) The market is bounded in time as well as space.** Buying before the signal is
forestalling; buying to resell inside the market is regrating. Green records the standard
ladder of penalty: "the forestaller was to be heavily fined for the first offence; for the
second to lose his merchandise and be put in the pillory; and for the third to be deprived
of the freedom of the city" (Green, *Town Life*, vol. II, PG 51637). She immediately adds
the honest caveat — "the law was simply ignored, or some trifling fine was inflicted" — and
draws the conclusion that "forestalling and regrating became the fashionable privilege of
town councillors and magnates." **The rule is real; the enforcement is selective; the
selectivity is the story.**

**(b) There is at least one class of shop that never closes.** FitzStephen's cook-row on the
riverbank is the clearest pre-industrial statement of a 24-hour urban service:

> "in London, upon the river side, between the wine in ships and the wine to be sold in
> taverns, is a common cookery, or cooks' row; there daily, for the season of the year, men
> might have meat, roast, sod, or fried; fish, flesh, fowls, fit for rich and poor. If any
> come suddenly to any citizen from afar, weary, and not willing to tarry till the meat be
> bought and dressed, while the servant bringeth water for his master's hands, and fetcheth
> bread, he shall have immediately from the river's side all viands whatsoever he desireth:
> what multitude soever, either of soldiers or strangers, do come to the city, **whatsoever
> hour, day or night**, according to their pleasures may refresh themselves."
> (FitzStephen, in Stow, PG 42959.)

That is a night-time occupancy anchor with a stated *reason to exist*: the arriving stranger
at an unsociable hour. It also gives the cook-row its correct location — between the ships'
wine and the taverns' wine, i.e. on the working waterfront, not in the civic plaza.

**Noon** has a name in the sources — "high prime" — and a matching image: at Westminster
Gate, "When the sun was at high prime: Cooks to me, they took good intent, and proffered me
bread, with ale and wine, Ribs of beef, both fat and full fine; A fair cloth they gan for to
spread" (John Lydgate [attrib.], *London Lickpenny*, as printed in Charles Hindley, *A
History of the Cries of London*, PG 37114). Outdoor tables with a cloth spread at midday
outside the courts: a lunchtime fixture attached to an institution.

## 1.4 Evening — curfew, and what curfew actually meant

The word is literal. Curfew is *couvre-feu*, "cover fire" — and it named both the rule and
the object: "a 'curfew' also refers to a device used to cover the embers of a fire at night,
allowing it to be re-ignited more easily in the morning" (Wikipedia, *Curfew*). The
regulation is conventionally attributed to William the Conqueror, requiring "that all lights
and fires should be covered to extinction at the ringing of an eight o'clock bell," and "until
1100 it was against the law to burn any lights after the ringing of the curfew bell" (same).
*Honesty flag: the William-the-Conqueror attribution and the 8 p.m. hour are the standard
encyclopedic account and should be treated as tradition-grade, not charter-grade, evidence.
The eight-o'clock hour in particular varied by town and season.*

Coulton's set-piece of the London evening is worth quoting at length because it is a
composition brief:

> "The sun goes down, a common greyness silvers everything… From beneath their very windows
> the long road stretches far into the fading landscape; men and cattle begin to straggle
> citywards, first slowly, and then with such haste as their weariness will permit, for the
> curfew begins to ring out from Bow steeple." … "The curfew is ringing again from Bow
> Steeple; the throng of citizens grows thicker as they near the gates; inside, the street
> echoes still with the laughter of apprentices and maids, while sounds of still more
> uproarious revelry come from the wide tavern doors. Soon, however, **in half an hour or
> so, the streets will be empty**; the drinkers will huddle with closed doors round the
> embers in the hall."
> (Coulton, *Chaucer and His England*, PG 37277.)

Note the shape: **the curfew bell rings twice**, with a movement of people between the two
ringings, and the street empties on a roughly half-hour lag after the second.

**Keeping open house after curfew was itself an offence**, and the stated reason is a
gift to a rumour system: taverns were forbidden to stay open "because such offenders as
aforesaid, going about by night, do commonly resort and have their meetings and hold their
evil talk in taverns more than elsewhere, and there do seek for shelter, lying in wait and
watching their time to do mischief" (royal prohibition quoted in J. J. Jusserand, *English
Wayfaring Life in the Middle Ages*, PG 58530, citing Riley's *Memorials of London*). **"Evil
talk in taverns" is the medieval authorities' own name for the information economy.**

## 1.5 Night — the watch, the hue and cry, and being out of doors

Jusserand's reconstruction of a night arrest is the single most usable night-scene in the
corpus, and it is built out of ordinance language ("hue and cry shall be made from town to
town until that they be taken and delivered to the sheriff"):

> "A singular picture: night wraps in its shadows the crooked lanes of the unlit city; the
> stranger is perhaps a robber, perhaps an honest man, who has lost his way, not knowing the
> place; **his fault is not to be within doors by curfew**; he gropes his way as best he can;
> the watch perceives and challenges him; fearing the result he takes to his heels, and
> behold! the hue and cry begins, the watch runs, the town wakes up, lights appear, and one
> after the other the more zealous join in the chase. **If the town is fortified, the postern
> gates have long been closed, and he will be surely taken.** Scarcely can he hope to cast
> himself into some unshut doorway at a turning of the street, behind which he will cower…"
> (Jusserand, PG 58530.)

Four mechanics are embedded there and all four are runnable: (1) being outdoors after curfew
is itself the offence; (2) flight converts suspicion into pursuit; (3) the hue and cry
recruits the neighbourhood — lights appear in windows, private citizens join; (4) **the
closed posterns make the walled town a container**, so a night chase in a walled town has a
bounded solution space.

Coulton adds the reason the streets themselves were dangerous — and it is an architecture
note: "in those unlighted streets, much that was most picturesque by day was most dangerous
at night, **from the projecting staircases and penthouses** down to doorways unlawfully
opened after curfew, wherein 'aspyers' might lurk, 'waiting men for to beaten or to
slayen.'" He concludes: "night-walking was treated in medieval towns as an offence
presumptively no less criminal than, in our days, the illegal possession of dynamite," and
gives the calibration fact: **"The 15th-century statutes of Oxford condemn the nocturnal
wanderer to a fine double that which he would have incurred by shooting at a proctor and his
attendants with intent to injure"** (Coulton, PG 37277). That comparison is the single best
one-line proof of how the period weighted night-walking.

The suspicion vocabulary is also on record. At the Views of Frankpledge the sheriffs and
bailiffs asked juries to say on oath what they knew "of such as continually haunt taverns,
and no man knoweth whence they come; **of such as sleep by day and watch by night, eat well
and drink well, and possess** [nothing]" (Jusserand, PG 58530). *Sleeps by day, wakes by
night, eats well, owns nothing* is a period-authentic NPC descriptor and a period-authentic
accusation.

## 1.6 The week

- **Market days** are the town's economic pulse and are named in charter grants; the fair is
  their annual amplification (see §1.7).
- **The Saturday half-holiday from noon "seems to have been universal"** (Green II, PG 51637,
  note; citing Rogers).
- **Sunday and vigil trading was generally forbidden** in the mid-fifteenth century "save in
  harvest time, and unless 'great high need may excuse'" — and the stated reason is
  wonderfully mundane: Sunday closing "was ordered as a matter of public convenience, because
  apprentices 'could not be trusted to carry on work in the absence of their masters at
  church'" (Green II, PG 51637). **The masters go to church; the shop closes because the
  apprentices cannot be left alone with it.**
- **Legal hours of work** (country labourers, statute 11 Henry VII c. 22, as summarised by
  Green II): "from March to September… from 5 A.M. till between 7 and 8 P.M., with half an
  hour for breakfast, an hour and a half for dinner; from September to March, from the
  springing of the day till the night of the same day. They were not to sleep in day-time
  save after dinner from May to August." **A statutory siesta window, May to August only.**
- **Night work was fought over trade by trade,** and the arguments are quotable. Against
  night work generally: "sight is not so profitable by night, or so certain, as by day — *to
  the profit, that is, of the community*." Against the spurriers specifically (a London
  ordinance Green quotes at length): they "wandered about all day idle, and 'then when they
  have become drunk and frantic they take to their work to the annoyance of the sick and all
  their neighbourhood… and then they blow up their fires so vigorously that their forges
  begin all at once to blaze… and all the neighbours are much in dread of the sparks which so
  vigorously issue forth in all directions from the mouths of the chimneys in their forges'"
  (Green II, PG 51637). **Sparks out of the chimney mouths at night is both a dressing note
  and a fire-risk note.**

## 1.7 The year — and the fair as a suspension of the ordinary law

This is the lane's most important structural finding for site 10, and it is not a
decoration: **at fair time, the town's own government could be switched off and replaced.**

Winchester's St Giles' fair is the documented case (Green I, PG 50392):

> "once a year when the great fair of S. Giles' took place he [the bishop] assumed supreme
> command in Winchester; **for the time all civic government was altogether suspended**; the
> bishop closed all shops in and round the town; traders coming with their cloth and woollen
> goods, their wines, their pottery, their brass-work, or their eastern spices, were subject
> to his jurisdiction, and handed over to him the biggest share of the profits, which he
> divided with the various religious establishments in the city."

Green's note gives the bishop's fair-time rights in enforceable detail: "tronage, authority
to take all weights and measures and bear them to the Pavilion and there make assay, to
demand that **the people of the city should come to the Pavilion to present cry raised and
bloodshed**, and other things touching the peace of our Lord the King." At Southampton
during the same fair, the bishop's bailiff saw "that only food was weighed or sold in the
town, that no merchant whether resident or not ventured to sell anything except food, that
there was no weighing or measuring, and that merchants who came with their goods swore they
did not bring them to sell at this time" (Green I, PG 50392, note 601).

So: during the fair, **the town's own weights are physically confiscated and carried to the
fair's court**; the town's shops are shut; the town's residents must bring their bloodshed
cases to the fair's tribunal; and a neighbouring port's merchants must swear an oath that
they did not come to trade. Winchester tried to break this in 1451, "raised a debate as to
the franchises and customs of the fair, and interfered with the bishop's privileges; but
their usual ill luck pursued them and they were obliged to submit and give a promise that he
should never again be disturbed" (Green I).

**The fair's own court** has a name and a speed: the **court of piepowder** (*pieds
poudrés*, dusty feet), sitting "daily during the fair… for debts and contracts" (Stow, PG
42959, on Bartholomew Fair). Its abuse is also on record: at Norwich a complaint alleged
that a lord "had made an evil use of the Pye-powder Court, using its summary and autocratic
procedure to imprison many men wrongly and tyrannically," and in 1477 a statute confined the
court to "contracts or bargains made during the fair" (Green II, PG 51637, note 789).

**The fair's physical form** is given by Stow for St Bartholomew's, and it is directly
buildable:

> "King Henry II. granted the privilege of fair, to be kept yearly at Bartholomew tide for
> three days, to wit, the eve, the day, and next morrow, to the which the clothiers of all
> England, and drapers of London, repaired, and had their **booths and standings within the
> churchyard of this priory, closed in with walls, and gates locked every night, and
> watched, for safety of men's goods and wares**; a court of pie powders was daily during the
> fair holden for debts and contracts."
> (Stow, PG 42959.)

And its decay, which is a ready-made site-condition: the booths "(only let out in the
fair-time, and closed up all the year after)" were replaced by "many large houses built, and
the north wall towards Long lane taken down, a number of tenements are there erected for such
as will give great rents." **A fairground eaten by permanent tenancies** is a condition state
for a plaza.

There was also a running quarrel about **where** a fair could be held: traders "expected to
be allowed to overflow from the High Street into the cathedral precincts, and were 'ever wont
and used… to lay open, buy and sell divers merchandises in the said church and cemetery and
special in the king's highway there'"; a 1427 royal order forbade fairs or markets in
sanctuaries "for the honour of Holy Church," and Green notes drily that "such an order was
little in harmony with the habits and customs of the age" (Green I, PG 50392).

**Seasonal rhythms** are visible in the entertainers' calendar: "Minstrels and harpers and
pipers and singers and play-actors… stayed at home through the dark winter days **'from the
feast of all Saints to the feast of the Purification,'** to make music and diversion for
their fellow citizens, started off on their travels when the [spring came]" (Green I, PG
50392). **1 November to 2 February = the town's resident-entertainer season; the rest of the
year they are on the road.** That is a directly rollable occupancy rule.

**The perambulation** (beating the bounds) is the year's territorial ritual, and Green
records a version — reported in the *Pall Mall Gazette*, 16 September 1892, and therefore a
late witness to an old custom — that is unforgettable:

> "the whole of the freemen of the borough have assembled in the morning in the
> Market-place. **The skull of an ox borne on two poles was placed at the head of a
> procession**, and then came the freemen and their sons, a certain number of them bearing
> spades and others sticks… The procession then moved along the boundary line of the borough,
> **the skull being dragged along the line as if it were a plough. The boundary holes were
> dug afresh, and a boy thrown into each hole and struck with a spade.** At a particular
> point, called Blacktone Leys, refreshments were provided, and the boys competed for prizes.
> The skull was then again raised aloft, and the procession returned to the Market-place."
> (Green I, PG 50392, note 242.)

*Honesty flag: this is a nineteenth-century newspaper report of a surviving custom, cited by
Green; it evidences the survival and the shape, not the medieval detail.* The wider custom is
well attested elsewhere: at Hythe "Holy Thursday was the day of perambulation," and Green
records that the Hythe procession route "could scarcely be traversed" for filth (Green II, PG
51637) — the ritual walks the same streets the nuisance jury complains about.

## 1.9 THE FIVE-TO-TEN MOST GAME-RELEVANT FINDINGS

1. **The bell has an owner, and the owner can be the site's antagonist.** Winchester's
   curfew bell rang "from land that defied the mayor's authority"; French communes received
   and lost the right to a belfry by charter. A rolled urban site can carry "who rings the
   hours here" as a faction axis, independent of who owns the market.
2. **THE FAIR SUSPENDS THE TOWN'S LAW.** At St Giles', "for the time all civic government
   was altogether suspended," the town's weights were carried off to the bishop's Pavilion
   for assay, all shops were shut, and bloodshed cases were presented to *his* court. This is
   the single strongest time-based occupancy/faction switch found in the lane, and it is a
   complete alternate ruleset for the same plaza.
3. **The gate is two doors on two schedules, laxly enforced.** Great gate at sunset, wicket
   until curfew, "interpreted with a good deal of laxity." That gives arrival hooks at three
   states (open · wicket-only · shut) plus a bribery/tolerance state, without inventing
   anything.
4. **Night-walking is the offence, not the crime you were going to commit.** Oxford fined the
   nocturnal wanderer *double* the fine for shooting at a proctor. A PC out after curfew is
   already in violation; the watch does not need a pretext.
5. **The hue and cry turns the whole neighbourhood into the encounter.** "the town wakes up,
   lights appear, and one after the other the more zealous join in the chase" — and the
   closed posterns bound the chase. This is a walk-native urban pursuit with an honest,
   spatially bounded solution.
6. **The cook-row is the 24-hour anchor and it lives on the waterfront, not the plaza.**
   "whatsoever hour, day or night" — explicitly justified by strangers arriving late. It is
   the natural night-time occupancy fixture for an urban tray.
7. **The morning belongs to the animals.** The common herd walking the streets at dawn to
   collect swine, and returning them at evening, is a two-beat daily event that occupies the
   street, blocks it, and smells.
8. **Entertainers are resident 1 Nov – 2 Feb and travelling the rest of the year.** A rollable
   seasonal switch on who is in the plaza.
9. **Illegal trading has a *time window*, not just a method.** Forestalling at cockcrow while
   the citizens are at Mass. The crime is defined by the bell.
10. **Bell-tower funding is a civic pressure story with a defaulters' roll.** Totnes named the
    non-contributors and sent the roll to the Archdeacon's Court.

## 1.10 DECLARED GAPS — what this lane could not establish

- **No town-by-town table of curfew hours.** The eight-o'clock figure is encyclopedic
  tradition attached to William I. The corpus gives *sequence* (sunset → wicket → curfew →
  empty streets) reliably and *clock hours* unreliably. Any Genesis number here is invented,
  not evidenced.
- **No primary text of a market-opening ordinance was read.** The market bell is inferred
  from the forestalling offence ("by six o'clock there was nothing left") and from the
  Chinese case (lane 7), which *is* explicit about drum strokes. The European market-opening
  signal is attested in outline only in what I read.
- **Riley's *Memorials of London and London Life* was not read directly.** It is the obvious
  keystone source for exact London ordinances and I reached it only at second hand, through
  Jusserand, Coulton and Green, who each cite it by page. Every London ordinance quoted in
  this lane is therefore a **quotation of a quotation**, and should be re-checked against
  Riley before anything load-bearing is built on the exact wording.
- **Continental daily rhythm is thin.** Bruges and Ypres supplied the belfry; Nuremberg
  supplied governance (lane 3) but not hours; Paris was not mined for the *guet* (the royal
  night watch) at all. The French and German night watch is an unfilled hole.
- **Nothing on the liturgical hours as *lay* timekeeping.** Matins/prime/vespers appear only
  as words. Whether ordinary townsfolk actually reckoned by the office bells, or only by the
  civic bells, is unresolved here.
- **The seasonal trade calendar is asserted, not tabulated.** Fairs are dated by saint's day
  (St Giles, St Bartholomew) but I did not build a wool/cloth/harvest/slaughter calendar,
  which is what a "when you arrive, the town is doing X" system would actually want.
- **The perambulation description is a Victorian newspaper report**, not a medieval record.
  Flagged in place.
