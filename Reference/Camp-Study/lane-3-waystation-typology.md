# Lane 3 — Waystation typology and the service-yard grammar

**The question.** Site 2's rung 2 is the waystation: outward **frontage** topology addressing the
road, and an **apron-without-gate** — threshold grammar inherited from the guard post WITHOUT the
control relation. This lane tests that ruling against the real typology and extracts the
service-yard grammar the waystation would use.

---

## 1. The typology, coldest to warmest

The four reference types form a clean ladder of *how much the site asks of you* — which is exactly
the "courtesy of the road" reading the brief wants.

| type | what it offers | what it asks | control over the route | built mass |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **well / spring stop** | water | nothing | none | none (a rim, a trough, a tree) |
| **way shelter** (bothy · refuge hut · trail lean-to) | roof, floor, sometimes fire | nothing; courtesy only | none | one small building, unlocked |
| **coaching inn** | food, bed, stabling, fresh horses | money | none — it profits from the route | a building wrapped around a yard |
| **caravanserai** | all of the above, plus safety | money (or charity); *the gate closes at night* | **none by day, total by night** | a fortified block around a court |

**The finding that matters most:** the ladder's control column stays empty until the very top, and
even there it is *self*-protection, not *route* control. The caravanserai's gate does not stop
anyone travelling — it stops people entering the building. **The brief's "offers, never commands"
survives the whole typology intact.**

---

## 2. Caravanserai — the grand cousin, and a real bridge to courtyard grammar

*Evidence: `l3-caravanserai-plan.jpg` (Choiseul-Gouffier, 1809, measured plan of a khan);
`l3-sultanhani-01.jpg` (Sultanhanı, the courtyard and the covered-hall portal);
`l3-sultanhani-02.jpg` (the covered hall interior).*

- **The plan is one gesture:** a rectangle of continuous cellular ranges around one **large empty
  court**, with a **fountain at the centre** and exactly **one entrance**, marked by an oversized
  portal on one side. The 1809 plan labels the ground floor as ranges of small identical rooms and
  the upper floor as more of the same; one side is a **covered hall** (*comble*) — a deep vaulted
  space for winter and for animals.
- Seljuk practice puts these **every ~30 km — one day's travel.** They are windowless, high-walled,
  single-entrance, and the ornament is concentrated at exactly two places: **the outer portal and
  the portal into the covered hall.** Larger examples carry a small mosque raised on piers in the
  middle of the court, or built over the entrance.
- The photographs confirm the composition at eye level: a **blank stone wall with one huge pointed
  arch**, a **flight of stairs running up the inside face of the wall** to the roof, small doors
  either side of the portal, and a **flat paved court** with almost nothing in it.
- **What this gives Genesis.** Four transferable relations:
  1. **One entrance, oversized.** Ornament goes on the threshold, not on the walls.
  2. **The court is empty on purpose,** with a single central object (fountain/well/mosque). It is a
     reserved quiet field with a landmark — the same grammar as the camp's fire-at-the-centre, at a
     larger permanence rung.
  3. **Cells are repeated and identical.** One piece × N.
  4. **Stairs up the inside of the wall to a usable roof** — a ready-made deck that is architecture
     rather than terrain, and therefore a *rung-2* deck. Worth flagging against the camp's "terrain
     owns the high ground" ruling (synthesis Q6).

## 3. Coaching-inn yard — frontage without control, in its purest form

*Evidence: `l3-coaching-yard-print.jpg` (an 18c trade card: a coach standing in the galleried yard
of the Ram Inn, Cirencester, seen through the carriage arch); `l3-coaching-inn-george.jpg` (The
George, Huntingdon — the surviving galleried range and its carriage arch).*

- **The whole type is one move: a building with a hole punched through it.** The carriage arch is
  cut through the street range so the road can flow *into* a yard; everything else — galleries,
  stables, chambers — hangs off the yard.
- The trade card shows the yard doing four jobs at once: coach standing, horses being handled, a
  porter carrying baggage, and people watching from the galleries above. **The yard is the stage.**
- The George photograph shows the modern residue: the arch, the gallery on timber posts, an
  external stair, and the yard now full of tables.
- **The Genesis reading.** This is the **apron-without-gate** the brief already ruled, drawn
  precisely: the arch is a threshold *shape* with no threshold *function* — nothing bars it,
  nothing checks you, and its whole purpose is to invite the road in. Compare the guard post, where
  the identical geometry (gate arch straddling the route) carries a control relation. **Same piece,
  opposite relation — which is the strongest possible argument that the waystation can be
  assembled from inherited pieces rather than needing its own module.**

## 4. Way shelters — the courtesy-of-the-road reading

*Evidence: `l3-bothy-corrour.jpg`, `l3-bothy-culra.jpg` (Scottish bothies);
`l3-trail-shelter-spence.jpg` (an Appalachian-style trail shelter interior).*

- **The bothy is a single small gable-roofed stone box, unlocked, unstaffed, in an enormous empty
  landscape.** Corrour sits alone beneath a mountain; Culra reads as two tiny rectangles in a wide
  moor. The composition is entirely about **the smallness of the mass against the largeness of the
  ground** — the building is a landmark by contrast, not by size. This is a free lesson for a
  clearing site: the way shelter is a *scale device*.
- The trail shelter is even more reduced: **three stone walls, one open front, a shed roof, and a
  raised sleeping platform** with a bench and a ladder. In structure-kit terms that is *literally*
  the site-1 inheritance the brief names — **shed roof plane + lean-to bay** — plus one platform.
  There is no door, no window, no lock.
- **Both are the same object as the camp's open-front Bedouin tent, in stone.** Open front, solid
  back, roof, raised sleeping surface. Which suggests the way shelter and the tent are two skins of
  one assembly rather than two designs.
- **The courtesy is legible in the fittings, and the fittings are props:** hooks, a bench, a
  platform, a fire scar, a broom, a notice board. No mechanism, no service, no owner.

## 5. Well / spring stops

*Evidence: `l3-well-stop.jpg` (1892 engraving: a caravan watering at a desert well — camels, a
donkey drinking, men filling skins).*

- The stop has **no building at all**: a water surface, a rim, a trodden apron, palms for shade,
  and animals crowding the edge. All the "architecture" is the ground condition — trampled mud,
  spill, and the radial approach paths.
- **This is rung 0/1 service.** It maps directly onto the brief's "customary ground" rung: the
  place is defined by *use marks*, not by construction. Blackened fire stones and cleared tent pads
  are the temperate version of the same fact.
- **Composition lesson:** the crowding happens on one side (the approach), and the far side stays
  open. A stop reads as a stop because the wear is asymmetric.

## 6. The service-yard grammar (the deliverable)

Pulled from all four types, stated as relations rather than pieces so the composition compiler can
consume it:

1. **APRON BEFORE THRESHOLD.** Every service site puts a widened, hardened, wear-marked ground
   region between the route and the built mass. It is where the wagon stops, the horses stand, the
   goods are unloaded. It is the piece that says "you may stop here." It has no gate.
2. **ONE ENTRANCE, OVERSIZED, ORNAMENTED.** Caravanserai portal, coaching arch, bothy door. The
   threshold is the only place decoration is spent.
3. **THE YARD IS A RESERVED VOID WITH ONE CENTRAL OBJECT.** Fountain, well, fire, mounting block.
   The emptiness is the composition; the object is the landmark.
4. **SERVICE RANGES ARE REPEATED IDENTICAL CELLS** wrapped on one, two, or three sides of the yard —
   never four unless the type is fortified (caravanserai). Coaching inns and waystations leave one
   side open to the road.
5. **THE ANIMAL SIDE AND THE HUMAN SIDE SEPARATE.** Stables/covered hall/corral on one flank,
   lodging/hall on the other. This gives the roller a legitimate reason to make an asymmetric plan.
6. **A WORKING VERTICAL EXISTS AND IS CLIMBABLE** — the caravanserai's wall stair, the inn's
   external gallery stair, the trail shelter's ladder to the sleeping platform. Every type in the
   set has one, and none of them is a defensive tower.
7. **WEAR IS ASYMMETRIC AND POINTS AT THE ROAD.** Trampled ground, rutting, spill, scorch — heaviest
   on the approach, absent behind. This is a condition-layer instruction, and it is what separates a
   waystation from a house that happens to be near a road.
8. **NOTHING CHECKS YOU.** No barrier, no arrow slit facing the road, no commanded threshold. Where
   defence exists (caravanserai), it faces *inward-at-night*, not outward-at-the-route.

## 7. Findings (plain English)

1. **The brief's "frontage without control" survives the entire real typology.** Even the most
   fortified example — a walled, windowless, single-gate caravanserai — controls only its own
   doorway, never the road. Nothing in this lane contradicts the offers-never-commands capsule.
2. **The carriage arch is the proof that the waystation can be assembled from inherited pieces.**
   It is geometrically the guard post's gate arch straddling a route, with the control relation
   deleted. Same piece, opposite relation.
3. **Caravanserais sit one day's travel apart (~30 km).** That is a walk-layer placement rule the
   world generator could use directly, and it gives the waystation a reason to exist at a specific
   spacing rather than at random.
4. **Ornament concentrates at exactly two places** in the caravanserai: the outer portal and the
   inner covered-hall portal. A cheap, culturally-true rule for where trim spend goes on any
   service site.
5. **The yard is an empty room with one object in it.** Fountain, well, fire. That is the same
   grammar as the camp's fire-at-the-centre — which means the permanence ladder is compositionally
   continuous: rung 0's fire ring *becomes* rung 2's yard fountain.
6. **The trail shelter is the site-1 lean-to bay with a sleeping platform and no door.** Three
   walls, open front, shed roof. The waystation's "standing shed" inheritance is validated exactly
   as ruled.
7. **The way shelter's composition is a scale device.** A tiny mass in an enormous empty landscape
   reads as shelter *because* of the contrast. That is a reason to reserve much more quiet ground
   around a rung-2 waystation than the built mass would suggest.
8. **The open-front way shelter and the open-front Bedouin tent are the same object in two
   materials.** Worth considering one assembly with two skins rather than two assemblies.
9. **Every service type has a climbable working vertical, and none of them is a watchtower** — a
   wall stair, a gallery stair, a ladder to a platform. That is a clean way to give the waystation a
   deck without violating the camp's "terrain owns the high ground" ruling *or* borrowing the
   military licence.
10. **Wear is the type's real signature.** Trampled asymmetric ground pointing at the road does more
    to say "waystation" than any structure does, and it is a condition layer, not geometry — the
    cheapest possible expression of the whole site.
11. **Animals get their own flank.** Corral, stable, covered hall — always to one side. This gives
    the generator a licensed asymmetry that reads as function rather than noise.
