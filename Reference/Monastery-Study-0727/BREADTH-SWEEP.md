# BREADTH SWEEP — "what else could this site be," culturally

The original `Reference/Monastery-Study/` packet (2026-07-24) is real, deep, and honest about
its own limits — its own §8 names the gap this lane closes: *"the room-ring table is drawn
primarily from St Gall... plus two Cistercian survivors. It is a Western-Latin ring. The
Orthodox and Himalayan cases do not share it."* That packet's culture spread (Cistercian/
Benedictine England-France, Eastern Orthodox Greek/Bulgarian, Tibetan/Himalayan Buddhist,
Transylvanian-Saxon fortified church, plus a Sinai/Bhutanese pair) is a genuine four-plus-
culture spread — but every one of those cultures is **vocational-religious monasticism**, and
most sit inside the Christian or Buddhist traditions. The 2026-07-27 campaign brief asks for
two distinct things this lane addresses in turn: (1) monastic traditions in **at least four**
building cultures the original packet did not reach, and (2) **commune variants** — the site's
own name's second half, which the original packet never researched at all.

## 1. Four more monastic building cultures

### 1.1 Coptic/Egyptian Orthodox — the fortified desert monastery

The four surviving monasteries of Wadi al-Natrun (Egypt) — Saint Bishoy, Saint Macarius, El
Suryan (Syrian Monastery), and El Baramus — are among the oldest continuously inhabited
Christian monasteries in the world (4th century onward). Each shares one design: a high
fortified perimeter wall enclosing several churches, monks' cells, and a distinctive
**qasr (keep)** — a multi-storey defensive tower, historically entered only via a **drawbridge
on an upper floor**, containing its own well, grain storage, chapel, and refuge cells, built to
let the community survive a raid or siege for weeks without opening the ground-floor gate at
all.

This is independently valuable evidence, not a restatement of the original study's tower
findings:

- **A second, older "retractable threshold."** The original study's `RETRACTABLE_THRESHOLD`
  candidate (original lane-1 table, "Threshold and water") cited Meteora's net-and-winch and the
  dzong's drawn ladder. The Coptic qasr's raised drawbridge is an independent, load-bearing
  precedent for the same LICENSED category from a wholly different building tradition —
  strengthening rather than merely repeating the original finding.
- **The keep is a self-sufficient sub-building, not a taller room.** Unlike the original study's
  bell towers (built from the arcade-bay piece, per original synthesis §5), the qasr is
  provisioned to be a **separate fortified habitat inside the compound** — well, granary, chapel,
  and cells all present at once. This is a genuinely new structural composition, not a height
  variant of an existing one.
- **Images:** `LICENSE-LEDGER.md` M4-01, M4-02.

### 1.2 Ethiopian/Aksumite Orthodox — the cliff-access monastery

Debre Damo (Tigray, Ethiopia), founded traditionally in the 6th century, sits on a steep
trapezoidal plateau roughly 1,000 m × 400 m, accessible **only by a rope climbed up a sheer
cliff about 15 m high** — a leather rope lowered by resident monks. No path, stair, or ramp
reaches the summit at all; the rope is the only ingress or egress for every person, animal, and
supply that ever enters. The church itself is built from **alternating courses of dressed stone
and squared timber**, with the timber ends projecting past the stone face ("monkey-head"
construction), under a steep pyramidal roof.

This is a **stronger vertical-access case than anything in the original study's hillside-vs-flat
lane (lane 2)**, which found the RULED seed's "gate low, climb monumental" pattern well-evidenced
across Palestrina, the Himalayan set, Meteora, Sumela, Rila, and the dzong — every one of which
still has a **walkable route**, even a steep or switchbacked one. Debre Damo has none: it is the
first source in either packet where "the site renounces the route" (the working spec's own
REQUIRED invariant) is total rather than architectural. It belongs in the working spec's
`LICENSED` tower/threshold family as the extreme end of the retractable-threshold spectrum, not
as a challenge to the RULED seed (which remains a walkable-approach hillside compound).

**Image:** `LICENSE-LEDGER.md` M4-03.

### 1.3 Japanese Zen Buddhist — the timber post-and-beam monastery

Sōtō Zen training monasteries (the model case: Eihei-ji, founded 1244 by Dōgen) are built
around a canonical **seven-building set** (*shichidō garan*): the *sanmon* (main gate), *butsuden*
(Buddha hall), *hattō* (dharma hall), *sōdō* (monks' hall), *daikuin* (kitchen), *yokushitsu*
(bathhouse), and *tōsu* (lavatory) — the bathhouse and lavatory carrying the same ritual silence
as the monks' hall itself (the "three halls of silence"). Construction is post-and-beam timber
throughout, with deep bracketed eaves and lattice-panelled walls, not masonry arcade.

Inside the *sōdō*, monks sleep, eat, and meditate on a raised platform called the **tan**, sized
to **one tatami mat per person — approximately 180 cm × 90 cm** — arranged in an unbroken row
along the hall's inner walls, facing a central shrine to Manjushri. This is the single most
directly measured **cell/dormitory module** found in either packet (see `MEASURED-REFERENCES.md`
§2) and a genuinely new structural idea: not a room per monk (Carthusian) and not one open hall
with beds (Benedictine/Cistercian), but **one continuous platform subdivided by a fixed
per-person floor module**, with sleep, meal, and meditation sharing the same footprint in
sequence rather than needing separate rooms.

**Image:** `LICENSE-LEDGER.md` M4-04.

### 1.4 Islamic Sufi — the khanqah/zawiya four-iwan hospice

The khanqah (also zawiya, ribat, or tekke by region and order) is the Islamic monastic-adjacent
institution: a residential hospice for Sufi mystics, typically built on the **four-iwan plan** —
a rectangular open courtyard with a large vaulted hall (iwan) on each of two or four sides, the
largest facing Mecca (the qibla iwan), and rows of small residential cells (*khalwa*, for
solitary retreat) opening directly onto the courtyard on the remaining sides, their doors visible
at ground level with living-quarter windows above. Institutions such as the Khanqah of Baybars
al-Jashankir (Cairo, 1306–1310) combine the hospice with a mosque and a founder's mausoleum under
a ribbed masonry dome beside a tall, tiered, balconied minaret.

This maps cleanly onto the working spec's own chassis language and confirms it independently
from a non-Christian, non-Buddhist tradition:

- courtyard + controlled threshold + repeated cell range = the working spec's `CLOISTER_VOID` /
  `CELL_UNIT` grammar, arrived at from an unrelated architectural lineage;
- the qibla iwan is a **head position claimed by direction, not by height** — a genuinely
  different deck logic from the original study's raised-platform finding (`RAISED_HEAD`,
  original synthesis §2), worth a note rather than a ruling: the working spec's "head platform"
  invariant should be read as "a privileged position with legal access and counterplay," not
  strictly "the tallest point";
- the minaret is a **third independent tower precedent** (after campanile/crossing-spire and
  dzong *utse*), tiered and balconied rather than plain or stepped.

**Image:** `LICENSE-LEDGER.md` M4-07.

## 2. Commune variants — the name's un-researched half

Every source in the original packet is **vocational-religious** — an order, a monastic rule, a
liturgical calendar. "Commune" in Site 4's own name has never been researched. Three real,
building-scaled secular or quasi-secular commune traditions close that gap:

### 2.1 American Shaker villages (18th–19th c.)

The Shakers (United Society of Believers) were a celibate, communal, millennialist Christian sect
organized around productive labor rather than a liturgical office cycle. Their largest buildings
— the **Dwelling Houses** — were symmetrical, multi-storey, brick or stone communal residences
housing 80–100+ members: Pleasant Hill's Centre Family Dwelling House (24,960 sq ft, up to 80
residents), Hancock's 1830 Brick Dwelling (100 residents), Canterbury's T-shaped 56-room dwelling.
Men and women were housed on the same floors but in **segregated wings reached by separate
stairs**, with communal dining, kitchen, and meeting space on the ground floor.

The building reads as a large domestic mansion, not a church — rows of identical shuttered
windows carry the "repeated unit" identity the original study found in the arcade bay
(`ARCADE_BAY`/`FURNITURE_ROW`, original synthesis §2), but expressed through **domestic
fenestration rhythm**, not covered walk arcading. This is a genuinely different repetition
register the working spec's chassis language does not currently name.

**Image:** `LICENSE-LEDGER.md` M4-05.

### 2.2 The Oneida Community (1848–1880, New York)

A secular/millennialist utopian commune (Bible Communism, complex marriage, communal
child-rearing) that built a single enormous residence, the **Mansion House** — a 93,000 sq ft
Second Empire brick mansion, home to ~300 people for nearly 30 years, containing sleeping rooms,
a library, sitting rooms, and a central gathering space (the "Family Hall"). Architecturally it
borrows nothing from monastic vocabulary at all: mansard roofs, dormered windows, quoins, bay
windows — the language of a wealthy Victorian estate house, scaled up.

This is the packet's sharpest **boundary test** for Site 4's own chassis claim. The working
spec's functional capsule requires enclosure-for-separation, court-centered circulation, and a
"rule made spatial" through threshold/schedule/authority. Oneida has communal identity and shared
governance, but **no enclosure, no court, no processional threshold, and no repeated bay** — it
is one big house, not a walled compound around a void. Recorded honestly: Oneida is real,
building-scaled commune evidence, but it argues for treating "secular utopian commune" as a
**licensed guest program that may NOT need Site 4's chassis at all**, not as a confirming case
for the courtyard-institution grammar. A commune that never developed a bounded-court plan is
evidence the chassis is optional for this program, not proof it applies universally.

**Image:** `LICENSE-LEDGER.md` M4-06.

### 2.3 The Israeli kibbutz (1910s onward)

A fully secular agricultural/collective commune built around communal property, communal child
raising (in the classic-era kibbutz), and — most relevant here — a **central dining hall (Hadar
Ochel)** functioning as the community's real social and political heart: not just a place to eat,
but where meetings, celebrations, and daily contact with the whole membership happened. Early
dining halls were plain, functional buildings — the evidence photograph (Kibbutz Maabarot, 1939)
shows rows of plain tables and benches, a serving line, and a wall clock, with no devotional
imagery anywhere.

This directly rebalances the original study's room-ring reading, which found the refectory
positioned "away from the church" as a secondary range (original lane-1 §2). In the kibbutz, the
dining hall **is the primary institutional space** — there is no church at all to be secondary
to. Read alongside Oneida, the kibbutz suggests a real distinction the working spec should carry
forward as a **PROPOSED** classification, not a ruling: a "communal dining/assembly hall as the
head position" variant, for occupancy states where the institution's authority is secular/
collective rather than devotional — the meal-hall claims the void the chapel claims in the RULED
seed, rather than the two sharing one room-ring slot.

**Image:** `LICENSE-LEDGER.md` M4-08.

## 3. What this does and does not license

**Closes:** the "at least four building cultures" ask (four new: Coptic, Ethiopian, Japanese Zen,
Islamic Sufi — on top of the original packet's four) and the "commune variants" ask (three new:
Shaker, Oneida, kibbutz) with real, sourced, imaged evidence.

**Does not close:** none of this is a founder ruling. The Coptic qasr and Debre Damo's cliff
access are offered as `LICENSED` extensions of existing categories (retractable threshold, tower
family), not new REQUIRED facts. The Zen *tan* module is offered to `MEASURED-REFERENCES.md` as
a real comparandum, not a replacement for the working spec's grid hypotheses. The Oneida and
kibbutz findings are offered as a **PROPOSED** boundary distinction (secular commune as a
guest-program-without-chassis case, and a communal-hall-as-head-position variant) for a future
founder question — see `synthesis.md` §3. Nothing here changes a REQUIRED, DEFAULT, or LICENSED
label already recorded in `docs/SITE-4-MONASTERY-COMMUNE-SPEC.md`.
