# Lane 4 — frontage and party-wall construction

## 1. THE FINDING: the medieval shopfront is one piece with two states

Two independent sources say the same thing, and it is the single most useful physical fact
in this study.

> "In medieval shop designs, the opening was protected by two horizontal shutters; the top
> one folding up to double as an awning or 'pet roof', and the lower one folding down to
> form the stall board."
> — *Retail Detail: historic shopfronts*, buildingconservation.com

> "the upper one of which could be hooked up to provide shelter while the lower one folded
> down to form a counter, or one shutter could be hinged to form a counter."
> — *Researching the History of Shops in Britain*, buildinghistory.org

The same source adds that the window was **large, arched and unglazed**, and that "a stall
set up just outside a shop window, perhaps under an awning, can put a tempting array of
goods under the noses of passers-by."

**Why this matters more than it looks.** It means the frontage has exactly two states,
built from the same geometry:

| state | what the bay is | what the street gets |
|---|---|---|
| **shut** | a blank timber panel filling a wide opening | a flat wall, no cover, no interaction, night |
| **open** | opening + a horizontal board projecting at counter height + an awning plane tilted above it | a waist-high counter (`climb-cost` cover), an overhead plane, a place goods sit, day |

One hinge, two poses, and the whole street changes character. This is the cheapest
occupancy/time-of-day lever the site could possibly have, and it is historically exact
rather than invented. It also makes "market" and "shut town" the same asset.

## 2. Shopfront anatomy, with the real names

From the buildingconservation article, the parts of a developed (17th–19th century)
shopfront, from the ground up:

- **stall riser / stallboard** — "the front of the shopfront below the window"; the
  descendant of the folded-down shutter.
- **pilasters** at each side, with **capitals or console brackets** at the top.
- **the window** — unglazed and arched when medieval; small-paned in a grid of glazing bars
  by the 18th century; bow (curved in plan) by the end of it.
- **the entablature**: architrave, **frieze/fascia**, cornice. "The frieze is enlarged to
  carry the sign-written name of the shop." The entablature conceals the beam over the wide
  opening.
- **cornice**, "sometimes incorporating sunblinds and ventilators" — i.e. the awning box is
  part of the cornice.

**Split against STRUCTURE-KIT-CATALOG §5:**

| element | bin | why |
|---|---|---|
| the wide ground opening | **geometry** | you go through it, shoot through it, see through it |
| stall board / counter (open state) | **geometry** | standable-adjacent, waist-high cover, goods sit on it |
| awning plane (open state) | **geometry** | it occludes from above; the fixed camera and any airborne threat both care |
| pilasters | geometry *only if* they project enough to be cover; otherwise paint | one to test in clay |
| fascia / frieze lettering | **paint (decal)** | the sign-written name is literally paint |
| cornice mouldings, console brackets, glazing bars | **paint (trim band)** | pure articulation |
| shutters in the shut state | **geometry**, one flat panel | it is a wall while shut |

## 3. The plot module: how wide a frontage bay actually is

Burgage plots — the medieval urban lot — are the module the street wall is built from.

- Typical street frontage **5–12 m (16–40 ft)**, with real local variation: Alnwick's
  properties are "still roughly 28 feet wide, or an obvious fraction or multiple of 28
  feet"; Birmingham's were "probably originally around five metres wide"; Stratford-upon-
  Avon's averaged "around 15 metres."
- Depth **100–200 ft (30–60 m) or more**, running back to a toft/backyard with gardens,
  workshops and storage.
- Plots are "characteristically long and narrow and arranged in series along the streets";
  standard widths were often laid out in perches.

**On the Genesis grid (5 ft cell):**

| real | cells | reading |
|---|---|---|
| 16 ft frontage | ~3 | the tight urban plot — a narrow shop |
| 20 ft frontage | 4 | a clean module |
| 28 ft (Alnwick) | ~5–6 | a generous burgage |
| 40 ft | 8 | a large or amalgamated plot |
| 100–200 ft depth | **20–40** | far deeper than any tray will be |

**Two consequences worth stating plainly.**

1. **A frontage bay is 3 to 6 cells wide.** That is a real number derived from real plots,
   and it lands sensibly on the grid without fudging. A street wall of six units is 20–35
   cells of frontage.
2. **The plot is deeper than the tray.** At 20–40 cells deep, no site 10 scene will ever
   contain a whole burgage plot. The slice shows the front few cells of each — which is a
   free, historically honest justification for the buildings' backs never being modelled,
   and for a cutaway that stops at the second room.

## 4. How shoulder-to-shoulder buildings actually meet

Read off Quedlinburg's unrestored lane (1972), the York Shambles, and cross-checked against
the FFT cohort (lane 5 §3):

- **Party walls are shared, and the frontage line wobbles.** Adjacent units stand slightly
  forward and back of each other; the street edge is a ragged line, not a straight one. In
  the Quedlinburg photograph the whole west side steps in and out by what looks like a foot
  or two per unit.
- **Every unit is a different height, and the difference is about one storey.** Never a
  uniform eaves line; never a three-storey jump either.
- **Roof directions alternate.** Gable-to-street and eaves-to-street sit side by side in the
  same run, plus half-hips and gablets. That alternation is the cheapest thing you can do to
  a street wall and it does the most.
- **Jetties oversail.** At the Shambles the upper floors project so far that the lane
  narrows toward the top; the corner unit at Little Shambles jetties on two faces at once,
  carried on visible timber brackets. Quedlinburg's jetties are shallower but universal.
- **The ground storey is often a different material from the storeys above** — brick or
  plaster below, timber frame above (Shambles); rendered plinth below, frame above
  (Quedlinburg). The base takes the wear, so it is repaired in whatever was to hand.
- **The street's ground is not one surface.** Both the Shambles and Quedlinburg have a
  **raised flagged footway on each side** and a **cobbled/setted carriageway in the middle**,
  with a channel at the joint. The footway is a real 1-step (well under 1h) kerb.

## 5. The two-level frontage: Chester Rows

The Rows are the extreme case of the multi-storey street wall, and they are worth stating
carefully because site 10's deck ruling is close to them.

What the photographs show: a **continuous covered public walkway at first-floor level,
running through the buildings**, with shopfronts opening off its inner side, a
**balustrade/railing along its open side** overlooking the street below, the upper storeys
carried overhead on exposed beams, and the ground-floor shops at street level underneath.
The walkway is flagged, lit by bracket lanterns, and it **steps** where the ground slopes.

Stated relationally: **the frontage contains a second street.** The building's first floor
is not private — it is public circulation with its own shopfronts. That is the balcony deck
taken to its logical end: instead of N separate balconies, one continuous railed gallery
serving every unit.

Chinchón (lane 1) reaches the same result from the Mediterranean side with two stacked
tiers of open timber gallery. Goland Coal City (lane 5) has the projecting gallery in FFT's
own idiom. **Three independent traditions build a continuous gallery rather than separate
balconies**, which is a real argument about how deck 2 should be constructed.

## 6. Exterior stairs and other honest vertical

- **The external timber flight up the outside of a mass** — Ledbury's market house stair
  (lane 1) is the clean example.
- **The external stone stair inside a close** — visible at the back of Annan's Close No. 18
  Saltmarket, climbing to an upper door.
- **The stair street itself** is lane 3's territory.
- The Rows' access is by **short flights up from the street at intervals**, so the gallery
  is entered repeatedly along its length, not once at an end.

## Findings (plain English)

1. **The medieval shop front is one hinged piece with two poses.** Shut, it is a blank
   panel. Open, the lower shutter folds down into a counter and the upper folds up into an
   awning. Two independent sources say so. That single mechanism gives the street a
   day/night state, a cover object, a goods surface and an overhead plane — for one asset.
2. **The sign is literally paint.** The fascia's whole job is to carry the sign-written
   shop name. Signage never needs to be geometry.
3. **A frontage bay is 3 to 6 cells wide.** Burgage plots run 16–40 ft to the street, with
   28 ft common. That lands on the grid cleanly and gives the street wall a real module.
4. **The plot is 20 to 40 cells deep** — deeper than any tray. The site will only ever show
   the front few cells of each building, which honestly justifies never modelling the backs.
5. **The frontage line wobbles.** Real party-walled streets step forward and back by a foot
   or two per unit. A perfectly straight street wall reads as modern.
6. **Height varies by about one storey per unit, and roof direction alternates.** Gable-to-
   street next to eaves-to-street is the cheapest and strongest thing a street wall can do.
7. **The ground storey is a different material from the storeys above** in most real
   frontages, because the base takes the wear and gets repaired in whatever was available.
8. **The street ground is three surfaces, not one**: raised flagged footway, kerb, and
   setted carriageway with a channel at the joint. That is a one-step (well under 1h) height
   change running the whole length of the scene.
9. **The two-level frontage is a continuous railed gallery, not a row of balconies.**
   Chester's Rows, Chinchón's stacked timber tiers, and FFT's Goland gallery all build one
   run serving every unit. Separate balconies are the more expensive and less-evidenced way
   to reach the same deck.
10. **The gallery is entered repeatedly along its length**, by short flights up from the
    street — so the second level is not gated behind one stair.
11. **Jetties narrow the lane toward the top.** At the Shambles the upper floors nearly meet.
    That is a free way to make a street feel enclosed without making it narrow at the feet,
    and it puts the roofline deck within jumping distance of the opposite side.
12. **The carriage arch is already in the kit.** The wide arched entry through a frontage
    (visible at Lesalia and Gariland in FFT, and at Ledbury's neighbours) is the camp study's
    "gate arch with the control relation deleted", now arriving as an ordinary street bay.

## Declared gaps

- **No dimensions for any shopfront component.** Both text sources state explicitly that
  they give no measurements. Everything about counter height, awning projection and opening
  width is unmeasured; I have described them from photographs and from the mechanism's
  logic, not from a drawing.
- **I did not read a primary building record.** The Weald & Downland 15th-century shops,
  Gladstone's Land in Edinburgh and the Tewkesbury Merchant's House are all named by
  buildinghistory.org as surviving evidence, and **I looked at none of them.** For a lane
  whose central finding is a physical mechanism, that is the gap that matters most — a
  photograph of a reconstructed stall-board shutter would settle proportions this lane can
  only assert.
- **Party-wall construction is argued from photographs of street elevations**, not from any
  section, building act, or archaeological plan. I did not research the post-1666 London
  Building Acts, fire-break party-wall requirements, or how two timber frames actually meet
  at a shared wall. Whether adjacent units share one wall or stand two walls back-to-back
  is **unresolved**, and it is a real construction question for a kit.
- **The burgage figures come from a search summary of several secondary sources**, not from
  the Cambridge *Urban History* paper it cited. The Alnwick 28 ft figure in particular
  should be checked against the original before it becomes a module.
- **Balcony construction is unresearched.** I found corbelled/galleried evidence but nothing
  on how a stone balcony's brackets, slab and rail are actually built, and nothing on iron
  balconettes beyond seeing them in Marville's photographs.
- **Chester Rows was read from two photographs**, one inside the gallery and one from the
  street. I have no plan, no idea how deep the gallery is, and no evidence about how it
  turns a corner.
