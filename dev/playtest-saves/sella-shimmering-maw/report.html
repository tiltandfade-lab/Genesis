Sella Voss · Human Rogue / Charlatan · L1 12 player turns Dawn → Dusk, Day 1 7 checks · 1 fight outcome: alive — "the Seam"

What this was

A faithful dry‑run of a live Genesis session with the transport stripped out. Two independent Sonnet agents held the seats — one Player, one DM — while the real production engine (loaded headless in jsdom) did every mechanical job: it rolled the world, built each digest, resolved the dice, and applied every typed event. Opus stayed out of the play and wrote this up.

No bridge, no browser, no model in the mechanical loop. The DM saw only the engine's private digest; the Player saw only the DM's narration; neither could reach the other's screen. Every die below came from the engine's own RNG — the DM could not touch a number if it wanted to.

Lens One — the story as the player lived it

A con with a guild in her spine

Sella Voss came into the world one step ahead of the people she'd swindled, carrying a sealed parcel that had bought her passage — a debt owed to a power called the Ironwood Circle. She woke in a village hung on chains over a crater of fused, multicolored glass, everyone laid out facing the same way for reasons no one explained.

"You came in on the night‑chains. They been asking after that. The tree folk. Since before you got here." — a boy named Batgal, turn 1

She bought his read of the Ironwood Circle — they never hurry; they carry a piece of whittled wood; one comes alone and sits down friendly — and learned that the only market for old debts and secrets was the Traitor's Tree, where a patient man would happily let her walk to him. So she didn't. First she wanted to know what she was carrying.

She tried to lift the wax seal without leaving a mark — and blew it (Sleight of Hand, 9 vs 15). The ironwood disc split clean across the grain. But she read the slab beneath anyway: a carved map of the crater's anchor‑chains — the chains holding the whole village up — and a scored list of a dozen names, two already crossed out. A hit‑list. She burned the two struck names into memory: Haldrek and Wemric Sunn, a house being dismantled.

And that was when a man across the market stopped, turned a pale sliver of wood over in his fingers, and let her come to him. Corran Vale. He'd had her name before she arrived. She sat down uninvited and pitched herself — worth more carrying your list than crossed off it — and very nearly sold it (Deception, 16 vs 18; a miss by two). He half‑bought it: three days, a leash of ironwood, and a demand she couldn't meet — bring him the source who'd told her about the Sunns, by dusk. There was no source. She'd read it herself. She let him keep watching the wrong hole.

The rest of the morning went badly and kept going badly. She went down into the Lower Decks to find the boy before Ironwood did — and couldn't (Investigation, 9). She tried to read who ran the deck's child‑cutpurses — and misread it (Insight, 9), and a hand closed around her wrist. Talk had carried her all day; on a sagging boardwalk over the glass, it finally ran out.

She dumped a thousand ball bearings across the choke where the rope‑bridge narrows. "Careful footing, boys!" — and bolted, cutting the far tie‑rope behind her. — turn 9, into the fight

The bearings worked. The bridge did not. She went down on the heaving span (Acrobatics, 8) with a cudgel in her ribs — 3 HP, then grabbed, then clubbed unconscious when a last desperate bluff crit‑failed on a natural 3. Capture, not death: the crew wanted her whole. She woke at dusk in Iron‑Strap custody, 1 hit point, bound in a guild knot, disarmed — and both faction heads standing over her, because the net‑mender had noticed her hands didn't flinch at a knot they'd grown up with.

So Corran asked her, with everyone listening: whose girl were you, before you were mine? And Sella — bound, bleeding, one hit point — told the truth and made it a con anyway. Iron‑Strap, a chapter three cities gone. Not a liability to prune. A seam. The one place two rival powers could pass a message without either being seen to bend.

Maddan cut the cord off her wrists in one bored motion. "So. You're the seam." Corran inclined his head the fraction that means yes.
"Three days. Fail either of us and you'll find we're both very patient about the same grave." — the close (Deception, 18 vs 16) · Sella Voss, now "the Seam"

She ended the session alive on one hit point and a whole future of borrowed rope — having convinced two people who wanted her dead that she was worth more as a thread between them than as an example. The cleanest con she'd ever run, and she ran it with the truth. Above her, the whole village was lying down to sleep, all facing the same way in the dark, and no one had yet told her why.

Lens Two — the world as the DM saw it

The board behind the screen

The Player saw a boy, a market, a patient man. The DM saw the whole apparatus — rolled into being before turn one and sitting in the digest every turn, most of it never spoken aloud. Genesis prep cast 41 codex records and three rumored frontiers before Sella opened her eyes. The session revealed the tip; the rest is the iceberg the slow drip draws from.

The DM's whole job here was restraint. It knew the two crossed‑out names, the hidden truths under each front, the secret that Sella herself was Iron‑Strap — and it spent them like a miser. It named the Ironwood Circle only when a boy earned the telling; it turned the setting's own myth, the Traitor's Tree, into the plot's shadow‑market rather than reciting it; and it detonated Sella's buried guild past not as exposition but as a tell — a net‑mender noticing that a bound woman's hands knew the knot.

Whole districts sat in the digest unvisited — Resin‑Gate, the Drowned Port, Cliff‑Row, Coalstack Row, Saltwidow Row, the Refugee Quarter — alongside a drawer of magic the session never opened: a red thread that breaks two objects at once regardless of distance; a music box that plays four bars and skips; a rent‑book with a guest who checked in and never checked out. None of it was needed. That is the point — a world with unspent depth reads as a world, not a set.

The one number the DM couldn't see

Through the whole fight, the digest reported Sella at 9 HP — her maximum. The engine ships sheet.hp, never hpCur, so the DM narrating a life‑or‑death brawl had no idea its player was at 3, then 1. It calibrated the danger by fiction and instinct alone, and got it right — but it was flying blind. (One of the findings below; the coordinator fed the true HP by hand so the climax stayed honest.)

The dice · the engine rolled, the DM never fudged

Seven checks, one that mattered most

The dice were genuinely cruel — six straight failures and near‑misses before the seventh. Because the engine owns the RNG and the DM pre‑authors a branch for every degree, the DM couldn't soften a single one. What it could do was write failure that moved the story forward. Every miss below became a beat, not a wall — and the one success came on the last roll of the night, earned, at DC 16.

What the playtest caught

The real payload: a critical regression the unit tests missed

This is what a Layer‑1 playtest exists to find — the things the mechanical gauntlet structurally can't, because it never calls a model. One critical regression and a cluster of event‑contract gaps surfaced in a single 12‑turn run. Each was routed around by hand to keep the session coherent; none is fixed in the engine yet.

Critical · roll‑branch events silently vanish

resolveBranch stamps every pre‑authored branch event with source:"branch". But the newer DM‑Seam validateEvent only accepts "detected" or "declared" — so it rejects, and every branch event no‑ops. Across this session that meant a broken seal that dealt no mechanical mark, faction clocks that didn't tick, HP that didn't drop, an epithet that wasn't granted — all the consequences of seven checks, dropped on the floor.

Why both suites stayed green: verify-roll-branches asserts events[0].source === "branch" — it checks the label, never that applyEvent mutated state — and verify-dm-seam tests the validator in isolation and never feeds it a branch‑sourced event. The two features are never exercised together. This is precisely the mutation‑test gap: verify the effect, not the label.

Read together, most of these are one theme: the AI↔engine event contract isn't yet discoverable from the digest the DM reads. A capable model reached for the intuitive field name or the obvious event and got a silent no‑op. The typed‑contract work added a validator; the next move is to make the contract legible — echo the accepted shapes, warn on rejects instead of swallowing them, and give the DM a clock and a travel event it can actually reach.

How it ran

The rig

  • The engine, headless. A new harness (dev/playtest-bridgeless.mjs) loads the real genesis.html and every manifest module in jsdom and calls the production seam directly — dmDigest, applyResponse, applyEvent, dmRollFor, resolveBranch, bindWorld, cgBind. No bridge, no browser, state persisted between turns as JSON.
  • Two sealed seats. A Sonnet Player (reasonable persona) rolled the character and made every choice; a Sonnet DM, running as one continuous conversation, saw only the digest and narrated in charter. Neither could read the other's screen.
  • Opus as clerk, not seat. The orchestration ran the engine between the two agents and never authored narration or a decision — so this analysis is independent of the play.
  • Honest bookkeeping. The findings above were routed around by hand mid‑session (a valid clock, a corrected field, a capped HP) to keep the world coherent — the route‑arounds are logged, not hidden, and the engine itself is unchanged.
