# Lane 7 — Counterpoints

**Nothing here is locked. Adam rules; this file evidences.**

The brief's purpose for this lane is explicit: keep the culture axis plural so
`CultureVisualConstitution` has more than one urban tradition to draw on. This lane is
therefore organised by **what each tradition does differently to the same problem**, not by
geography — because the differences are the reusable part.

**Standing honesty note.** Three of the four best sources here are outsiders' testimony:
Marco Polo on Hangzhou, Bernal Díaz on Tenochtitlan/Tlatelolco, Ibn Battuta on the funduq.
Outsider accounts exaggerate scale, misattribute function, and select for the marvellous.
Everything below is flagged where that matters, and the encyclopedic material is separately
flagged where it is thin.

---

## 7.0 The one-sentence result

Every tradition surveyed solves the same four urban problems — **sort the trades · signal the
hours · police the measures · contain the fire and the stranger** — and they solve them with
strikingly transferable machinery (a drum instead of a bell, a *muhtasib* instead of a clerk of
the market, a ward wall instead of a curfew), which means **the culture axis can be built as a
set of swappable *answers* to a fixed set of *questions*, rather than as a set of skins.**

## 7.1 THE SOUK — trade hierarchy as a radial gradient

The European finding (lane 2) is that trades cluster and that some drift. The Islamic-world
finding is stronger: **the clustering is ordered radially by cleanliness and prestige, outward
from the Friday mosque.**

- Markets "that sold the most important or expensive goods were usually located near the city's
  central Friday mosque" (Wikipedia, *Souk*).
- The hierarchy is explicit in the wider literature: "**more prestigious and clean crafts — such
  as perfumers, candle sellers, booksellers, and herbalists — were positioned near the central
  mosque**… **noisier, dirtier, or more disruptive trades — including blacksmiths, tanners,
  potters, and coppersmiths — were relegated to peripheral areas or near city gates to contain
  disturbances and odors.**" Tunis is the named case: "the dyers' souk is situated at the
  outskirts of the medina, far from the center where the Al-Zaytuna Mosque is situated, since
  dyeing is considered a polluting activity." (Search synthesis across several sources — see the
  gap note in §7.9; the individual souk articles for Tunis, e.g. *Souk El Attarine* (perfumers)
  and *Souk Es Sabbaghine* (dyers), corroborate the two poles.)
- The origin story places the market against the mosque from the beginning: the Prophet "built a
  Souq in Medina after the Hijra, close to the *jami'* (the Friday mosque)," and early souks had
  "a primitive layout of humble booths or kiosks in a large open space, **sometimes shaded by
  fabric and umbrellas**, surrounding the outer perimeter of the central mosque."
- The secure core: "a more secure market area, usually centrally located and consisting of
  **streets that were covered or roofed**. This complex is variably known as a **qayṣariyya,
  bedesten, or khān**," hosting "the most prestigious and profitable trades such as jewelry,
  perfumes, and textiles. **In order to protect these goods, the entrances to this area could be
  closed and locked at night or in times of danger.**" (Wikipedia, *Souk*.)
- Trades occupy named streets: "the gold souk, the textile souk, the spice souk, the leather
  souk," an arrangement that "promotes competition among sellers and helps buyers easily compare
  prices" — and, crucially, "**enabled effective regulation by the muhtasib**… and simplified
  oversight of standards."

**Design translation (proposal only).** The souk gives site 10 a *second*, sharper district
rule than Stow's: instead of "trades cluster and drift," it is **"distance from the sacred
centre = cleanliness rank,"** plus **a lockable roofed core for the valuable trades.** The two
rules are compatible and could be culture-selected: European towns drift by advantage, souk
towns are graded by pollution.

## 7.2 THE MUHTASIB — a walking market magistrate

The European clerk of the market and the assize of bread (lane 3) have a close and better-
documented counterpart. The *muhtasib* was "a supervisor of bazaars and trade, the inspector of
public places and behavior in towns," responsible for "the regulation of weights, money, prices,
public morals, and the cleanliness of public places," authorised "to audit the businesses if they
were selling their products at the price limits set by the government." He "inspected if the food
sold was safe and the measuring equipment was accurate," and could "**order pots and pans to be
re-tinned or replaced; all vessels and their contents had to be kept covered against flies and
insects.**" Craftsmen and builders "were usually responsible to the muhtasib for the standards of
their craft." (Wikipedia, *Muhtasib*.)

His jurisdiction is precisely bounded and that is the interesting part: unlike a *qadi* he "had
no jurisdiction to hear cases — only to settle disputes and breaches of the law where the facts
were admitted or there was a confession of guilt." He was "urged to first advise the wrongdoer to
cease the reprehensible act," but "in most instances he also was authorized to use physical force
against the individual." And the staging is memorable: "**muḥtasibs in Cairo markets had a stand
(*dikka*) from which they observed and whipped those who cheated when weighing their goods.**"

There were manuals: al-Shayzari's *Nihāyat al-rutba fī ṭalab al-ḥisba* (d. 1193) and Ibn
al-Ukhuwwa's *Maʿālim al-Qurba fī Aḥkām al-Ḥisba*, containing "practical advice on management of
the marketplace… manufacturing and construction standards."

**Three transferable mechanics:** (1) **a raised inspection stand in the market** from which the
inspector watches and punishes on the spot; (2) **summary jurisdiction limited to admitted
facts** — he can act instantly on what he sees, but a denial kicks the case to a real judge; (3)
**a written manual of standards** that a PC could read, steal, or quote back at him.

Cross-reference: Tlatelolco had "**courts of justice, to which three judges and several constables
were appointed, who inspected the goods exposed for sale**" *inside the market square* (§7.4).
Three independent traditions put a magistrate physically in the market. **That convergence is the
strongest single result in this lane.**

## 7.3 THE CARAVANSERAI — the traveller's institution as architecture

Where the European inn is a private business with statutory price caps (lane 4 §4.2), the
caravanserai is an **endowed institution with a plan**.

- Form: "a central courtyard, almost always open to the sky, which was surrounded by a number of
  identical animal stalls, bays, and chambers"; Safavid examples had "a rectangular courtyard
  surrounded by a gallery of vaulted openings (iwans) and rooms on one or two levels"; and **a
  single entrance "wide enough to permit large or heavily laden beasts such as camels to enter."**
- Spacing: built along major roads at intervals "intended to be equivalent to a day's journey" —
  **"a distance of 30 to 40 kilometres (19 to 25 mi) in open landscapes" or "about 10 kilometres
  (6 mi) or less in more difficult mountainous terrain."**
- Urban variants: **khan** (urban or general), **funduq** (Maghreb inns), **wikala** (urban
  caravanserai "which housed merchants and their goods and served as a center for trade, storage,
  transactions and other commercial activity").
- Funding: "managed through a **waqf**, a protected agreement which gave certain buildings and
  revenues the status of mortmain endowments guaranteed under Islamic law."
- **The night procedure, from Ibn Battuta**: the director "**write[s] down the names of all the
  travellers who will pass the night there, seals it and locks the door of the funduq.**"
- Named survivors: **Khan al-Mirjan** (Baghdad, 1359) — "the oldest surviving urban caravanserai";
  **Sultan Han** (Aksaray, 13th c.); **Izadkhast** (Fars, early 17th c.); **Wikala of Sultan
  al-Ghuri** (Cairo, 1504–05). (All: Wikipedia, *Caravanserai*.)

**The single-wide-gate-for-loaded-camels rule is a hard architectural constraint that Genesis can
use directly**, and the sealed nightly register is a superb arrival mechanic: **your name is
written down, the list is sealed, and the door is locked.** Compare the European version — "the
watchman… lock up the house and take the key with him" (plague orders, lane 5) and "gates locked
every night, and watched" (Bartholomew fair booths, lane 1). Three traditions, one instinct.

## 7.4 TLATELOLCO — the market city, and every commodity in its own street

Bernal Díaz del Castillo's eyewitness account of the great market of Tlatelolco (1519) is,
alongside Polo's Kinsay, the best pre-industrial market description in the corpus (*The Memoirs
of the Conquistador Bernal Diaz del Castillo*, vol. I, PG 32474):

> "The moment we arrived in this immense market, we were perfectly astonished at the vast numbers
> of people, the profusion of merchandise which was there exposed for sale, and **at the good
> police and order that reigned throughout**… **Every species of merchandise had a separate spot
> for its sale.**"

And the sorting, which he immediately recognises as the same system he knows from home: "**and
everything put me in mind of my native town Medina del Campo during fair time, where every
merchandise has a separate street assigned for its sale.**" **A Castilian soldier identifies the
Mexica market's organising principle by pointing at a Spanish fair.** That cross-cultural
recognition is the lane's thesis in one sentence.

The inventory of sections, in his order: gold and silver wares, jewels, feather-work cloths, other
manufactured goods, **and slaves** ("To prevent these from running away, they were fastened with
halters about their neck, though some were allowed to walk at large"); then coarser wares —
cotton, twisted thread, **cacao**; nequen cloth, ropes and sandals; cooked maguey root; skins of
"tigers, lions, jackals, otters, red deer, wild cats" some tanned; beans, sage, herbs and
vegetables; fowls, turkeys, ducks, rabbits, hares, deer and dogs; fruit-sellers, pastry-cooks and
tripe-sellers; earthenware "from the large earthen cauldron to the smallest pitchers"; honey and
honey-cakes; **timber-merchants and furniture-dealers "with their stores of tables, benches,
cradles, and all sorts of wooden implements, all separately arranged"**; paper (*amatl*); "tubes
filled with liquid amber and tobacco"; scented salves; seeds "exposed for sale in the porticoes";
medicinal herbs; salt; flint-knife makers; fish; a bread "made of a kind of mud or slime collected
from the surface of this lake"; and "instruments of brass, copper, and tin; cups, and painted
pitchers of wood."

Two details deserve isolating.

**The waste-as-commodity loop.** "I must also mention **human excrements, which were exposed for
sale in canoes lying in the canals near this square, and is used for the tanning of leather**;
for, according to the assurances of the Mexicans, **it is impossible to tan well without it**…
along every road **accommodations were built of reeds, straw, or grass, by which those who made
use of them were hidden from the view of the passers-by, so that great care was taken that none of
the last-mentioned treasures should be lost.**" Compare the European gong farmer carting waste to
Dung Wharf for barging out as field fertiliser (lane 5 §5.3). **Both cities monetise night soil;
one of them puts screened public privies along the roads to catch it, and sells it in boats
alongside the market.**

**Currency by inspection.** "merchants, who dealt in gold dust as it is dug out of the mines,
which was exposed to sale **in tubes made of the bones of large geese, which had been worked to
such a thin substance, and were so white that the gold shone through them. The value of these
tubes of gold was estimated according to their length and thickness**, and were taken in exchange,
for instance, for so many mantles, *xiquipiles* of cacao nuts, slaves, or other merchandise."
**Money you hold up to the light.**

**Scale and sound**, viewed from the temple platform: "the bustle and noise occasioned by this
multitude of human beings was so great that **it could be heard at a distance of more than four
miles**," and veterans "who had been at Constantinople and Rome, and travelled through the whole
of Italy, said that they never had seen a market-place of such large dimensions, or which was so
well regulated, or so crowded with people." *(Honesty flag: conquistador testimony, written
decades later, in a genre that competes on wonder. The four-mile figure is rhetoric. The
**organisation** — sections by commodity, judges in the square, porticoes — is corroborated by
the internal consistency of his list and by other early accounts, and is the part worth using.)*

The market's relation to the sacred precinct is spatial and immediate: "On quitting the market, we
entered the spacious yards which surround the chief temple. These appeared to encompass more
ground than the market-place at Salamanca, and were **surrounded by a double wall**, constructed
of stone and lime; these yards were **paved with large white flag-stones, extremely smooth**… and
all was kept so very clean that there was not the smallest particle of dust or straw to be seen."
**A scrupulously swept, white-flagged, double-walled precinct immediately beside the loudest
market in the world.** The contrast is a composition brief.

Bernal Díaz also names the city's supply arteries as visible from height: three causeways, "the
aqueduct which ran from Chapultepec, and provided the whole town with sweet water," bridges across
the causeway openings "through which the waters of the lake ebbed and flowed," and a lake "crowded
with canoes, which were bringing provisions, manufactures, and other merchandise to the city." He
notes that "**the only communication of the houses in this city, and of all the other towns built
in the lake, was by means of drawbridges or canoes**."

## 7.5 KINSAY (HANGZHOU) — the fullest alternative urban machine

Marco Polo's Kinsay chapters (*The Travels of Marco Polo*, vol. II, PG 12410) describe a city
whose systems map one-for-one onto the European ones and beat them on almost every axis. Treated
with the standing caveat that Polo's numbers are unreliable and his text is layered with later
editors, the *systems* are corroborated by the Chinese topographies his editors quote.

**Markets on a schedule and a grid.** "In this part are the **ten principal markets**… all
**squares of half a mile to the side**, and along their front passes the main street, which is
**40 paces in width**, and runs straight from end to end of the city… At every four miles of its
length comes one of those great squares… parallel to this great street, but at the back of the
market places, there runs a very large canal, on the bank of which towards the squares are built
**great houses of stone, in which the merchants from India and other foreign parts store their
wares, to be handy for the markets**. In each of the squares is held **a market three days in the
week**, frequented by 40,000 or 50,000 persons."

Compare Leadenhall's Monday–Wednesday showing (lane 2 §2.3). **A three-day market week in both
London and Hangzhou.**

**The market square as an administrative unit.** "All the ten market places are encompassed by
lofty houses, and below these are shops where all sorts of crafts are carried on… **Certain of the
streets are occupied by the women of the town**… **Other streets are occupied by the Physicians,
and by the Astrologers, who are also teachers of reading and writing**; and an infinity of other
professions have their places round about those squares. **In each of the squares there are two
great palaces facing one another, in which are established the officers appointed by the King to
decide differences arising between merchants, or other inhabitants of the quarter. It is the daily
duty of these officers to see that the guards are at their posts on the neighbouring bridges, and
to punish them at their discretion if they are absent.**"

**Two facing court-palaces per market square, whose daily job is (a) merchant disputes and (b)
checking that the bridge guards turned up.** That is a *magistrate whose routine duty is a
personnel roll-call* — a perfect small-scale authority NPC with a repeating, checkable task.

**The watch, the hours, and the marked door.** "each of the 12,000 bridges should be provided with
a **guard of ten men**… Each guard is provided with **a hollow instrument of wood and with a metal
basin, and with a time-keeper** to enable them to know the hour… **when one hour of the night is
past the sentry strikes one on the wooden instrument and on the basin**, so that the whole quarter
of the city is made aware that one hour of the night is gone. At the second hour he gives two
strokes… **Part of the watch patrols the quarter, to see if any light or fire is burning after the
lawful hours; if they find any they mark the door, and in the morning the owner is summoned before
the magistrates**… Likewise if in the daytime they find **any poor cripple unable to work for his
livelihood, they take him to one of the hospitals**… Or if he be capable of work they oblige him
to take up some trade."

**Fire.** Timber houses with "**lofty towers of stone in which articles of value are stored for
fear of fire**"; on an alarm the watch beat the slab and "one or two thousand at the least"
converge, evacuating goods "either by removing them to the towers above mentioned, or by putting
them in boats and transporting them to the islands in the lake"; and "**no citizen dares leave his
house at night, or to come near the fire; only those who own the property, and those watchmen who
flock to help.**" A hill inside the city carries "a Tower, and at the top of the tower is hung **a
slab of wood**. Whenever fire or any other alarm breaks out in the city **a man who stands there
with a mallet in his hand beats upon the slab**, making a noise that is heard to a great
distance."

**Streets and drainage.** "All the streets of the city are paved with stone or brick… **But as the
Great Kaan's couriers could not gallop their horses over the pavement, the side of the road is
left unpaved for their convenience.** The pavement of the main street of the city also is laid out
in two parallel ways of ten paces in width on either side, **leaving a space in the middle laid
with fine gravel, under which are vaulted drains which convey the rain water into the canals**;
and thus the road is kept ever dry."

**A paved street with a deliberately unpaved courier lane and a gravel-topped covered drain down
the centre** is a road cross-section a level designer can build immediately.

**The clepsydra tower.** A later account quoted by Polo's editors describes, on the hill Ching-hoang
inside the walls, "that tower with the watchmen, on which there is **a clepsydra to measure the
hours, and where each hour is announced by the exhibition of a placard, with gilt letters of a foot
and a half in height**." **The hour is *displayed*, not only sounded** — a visual clock for a city
too big to hear one bell.

Consumption, for scale: "the quantity of pepper introduced daily for consumption into the city of
Kinsay amounted to **43 loads, each load being equal to 223 lbs**." *(A characteristic Polo
number; use as flavour, not as economics.)*

## 7.6 THE WARD SYSTEM — Chang'an and Heian-kyō

The East Asian capital solves the night-order problem with **walls inside the city** rather than
with a curfew bell and a watch.

**Chang'an (Tang):** "**160 walled 1×1 li wards**" of "about 50–100 families" each; ward walls "9
to 10 ft (3.0 m) in height"; the smallest ward 68 acres, the largest 233. The city measured "5 mi
(8.0 km) by six miles" with exterior walls "18 ft (5.5 m) high." Street widths: narrowest "82 ft
(25 m)," gate approaches "328 ft (100 m)," and the **Imperial Way "492 ft (150 m) wide."**

And the market signal, which is the best time-fact in the whole study:

> "**The West and East Markets would open at noon, announced by the 300 strikes on a loud drum,
> while the markets would close one hour and three quarters before dusk, the curfew signaled by
> the sound of 300 beats to a loud gong.**"
> (Wikipedia, *Chang'an*.)

**Two official markets only, of nine city blocks each; opening at noon on 300 drum strokes;
closing on 300 gong beats an hour and three-quarters before dusk.** Every element of that is
directly usable, and it makes the European market bell (which I could not source directly, lane 1
§1.10) look under-specified by comparison.

**Heian-kyō (from 794):** 4.5 km east–west by 5.2 km north–south, modelled on Chang'an "**with the
exception that Heian-kyō had no city walls**." The module: 1 *jō* ≈ 3.03 m; 40 square *jō* = one
*chō* of 121.2 m per side. Streets: major (*ōji*) "over 8 jō wide (~24 m)," minor (*koji*) "4 jō
wide (~12 m)." The central **Suzaku-ōji was 28 jō — about 84 m — wide**, dividing the Right Capital
(Ukyō) from the Left (Sakyō). Addresses were coordinates: "Right Capital, jō Five, bō Two, chō
Fourteen." Two official markets, East and West, faced the seventh street. (Wikipedia, *Heian-kyō*.)

**And the failure case is the best part.** "The Right Capital's fate was disastrous. It **overlapped
the wetlands formed by the Katsura River** and, even by the 9th century, little progress had been
made in developing the area." By the tenth century it "had become so dilapidated that it began to
be used as farmland, **something which had previously been forbidden within the city limits**."

**Half a planned capital reverting to fields inside its own boundary, in violation of its own
zoning** is a magnificent condition state for a rolled city — and it is documented, not invented.
It also supplies a hard planning lesson Genesis can reuse: **a grid laid on a floodplain half-fails
along the wet edge.**

## 7.8 THE FIVE-TO-TEN MOST GAME-RELEVANT FINDINGS

1. **THE RADIAL CLEANLINESS GRADIENT.** Perfumers, candle-sellers, booksellers and herbalists at
   the mosque; blacksmiths, tanners, potters and coppersmiths at the periphery and the gates. A
   second, sharper district rule than Europe's drift-by-advantage, and culture-selectable against
   it.
2. **THE LOCKABLE ROOFED CORE** (qaysariyya / bedesten / khan) for the highest-value trades,
   "closed and locked at night or in times of danger." A market with a vault at its centre.
3. **A MAGISTRATE PHYSICALLY IN THE MARKET — in three independent traditions.** The *muhtasib* on
   his raised *dikka*, whipping cheats where they stand; Tlatelolco's three judges and constables
   inspecting goods in the square; Kinsay's two facing court-palaces per market square. The
   European clerk of the market is the fourth. This convergence is strong enough to be a law rather
   than a flavour.
4. **THE SEALED NIGHTLY REGISTER.** Ibn Battuta's funduq director writes down every traveller's
   name, seals the list, and locks the door. The cleanest arrival mechanic in the study.
5. **THE CARAVANSERAI'S SINGLE CAMEL-WIDE GATE**, and its placement one day's journey apart —
   30–40 km in open country, ~10 km or less in mountains. A hard constraint and a hard spacing rule.
6. **300 DRUM STROKES TO OPEN THE MARKET AT NOON; 300 GONG BEATS TO CLOSE IT AN HOUR AND
   THREE-QUARTERS BEFORE DUSK.** The single most precisely specified market signal recovered
   anywhere in this study.
7. **THE MARKED DOOR.** Kinsay's patrol marks the door of any house showing a light after the
   lawful hour; the owner is summoned in the morning. Compare the plague red cross (lane 5) — the
   *marked door* is a recurring urban technology across traditions.
8. **THE COURIER LANE AND THE CENTRAL COVERED DRAIN.** A paved street with a deliberately unpaved
   verge so couriers can gallop, and a gravel strip down the middle over vaulted drains. A road
   cross-section, ready to build.
9. **WARD WALLS INSTEAD OF A CURFEW.** Chang'an closes itself in 160 pieces, each 3 m walled, each
   holding 50–100 families. A city that locks internally is a fundamentally different tactical
   space from one that locks its outer gate.
10. **THE HALF-FAILED GRID.** Heian-kyō's Right Capital reverted to marsh and then to illegal
    farmland inside the city limits. A documented, non-invented condition state for a rolled city —
    and a planning lesson about grids on floodplains.
11. **WASTE IS A COMMODITY IN BOTH HEMISPHERES** — sold from canoes for tanning at Tlatelolco (with
    screened roadside privies built to catch it), barged from Dung Wharf as fertiliser in London.
12. **A conquistador recognised the Mexica market's organising principle by comparing it to a
    Castilian fair.** The best single argument that "trade sections" is a convergent human answer
    rather than a cultural signature.

## 7.9 DECLARED GAPS

- **Three of my four richest sources are outsider travel accounts** (Polo, Bernal Díaz, Ibn
  Battuta at second hand). This is a structural weakness of the lane, not an incidental one. No
  indigenous urban record — no Chinese city topography read directly, no Mexica source, no Arabic
  *hisba* manual — was consulted. Everything here is "how the traveller described it."
- **The souk trade-hierarchy claim rests on a web-search synthesis, not a single citable
  authority.** The Wikipedia *Souk* article confirms mosque-adjacency for valuable goods and the
  lockable core, but **does not state the outward pollution gradient**; that came from a search
  that aggregated several sources of mixed quality (including a Grokipedia page and travel sites),
  with the Tunis dyers'-souk example as the concrete anchor. **The gradient is well established in
  the scholarly literature on the Islamic city, but this study has not cited a scholarly source for
  it.** Flagged as the lane's most-quoted, least-solidly-sourced claim.
- **The call to prayer is missing from my time-signal row**, which is an embarrassing hole given
  that it is the obvious Islamic-world answer to the bell. I did not source it and will not assert
  it from general knowledge.
- **Nothing on South Asian, sub-Saharan African, or Southeast Asian urbanism.** No Vijayanagara, no
  Kano or Timbuktu, no Angkor, no Melaka. The lane as it stands is Islamic-world + East Asian +
  Mesoamerican, which is plural but not comprehensive.
- **Ibn Battuta's *Travels* was not obtained.** The Gutenberg catalogue search returned nothing
  usable, so the funduq quotation reaches me through the *Caravanserai* article. His account of
  Cairo, Damascus and Delhi would be the single highest-value addition to this lane.
- **Marco Polo's text is editorially layered.** The Yule edition I read interleaves Polo with later
  commentators and Chinese topographies (the *Si-hu-Chi*), and I have not always been able to tell
  Polo's voice from his editor's. Where I quote, I have taken passages presented as Polo's own
  narrative; the clepsydra-placard passage is explicitly from a *later* account quoted in the notes
  and is labelled as such.
- **No non-European fire regime except Kinsay's.** The comparison table has three empty cells in
  the fire row and I have left them visibly empty rather than filling them by inference.
- **The Aztec market's regulation is described only by its conqueror**, weeks before he helped
  destroy it. That should be said plainly whenever the Tlatelolco material is used.
