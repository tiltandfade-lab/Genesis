# Lane 1 — Cloister morphology, and the relational grammar it yields

**The question.** Site 4's banked rulings assert a plan wrapped around a courtyard, an
arcade bay as the signature repeated piece, a room ring (chapel / hall / bedroom row /
garden), a well at the yard's heart, and one ceremonial gate. This lane goes to the ideal
plan, the standardised order plan, and built survivors, and extracts the relations that
actually govern a cloister.

Evidence: `l1-stgall-plan-original.jpg`, `l1-stgall-plan-redraw.png`,
`l1-stgall-cloister-detail.jpg` (the Plan of Saint Gall, c. 820–830) ·
`l1-fontenay-plan-dehio.png`, `l1-fontenay-cloister-01.jpg`,
`l1-fontenay-cloister-arcade.jpg` (Fontenay, Cistercian) · `l1-stratafloridaplan.png`
(Strata Florida ground plan) · `l1-zwettl-brunnenhaus.jpg`, `l1-poblet-fountain.jpg`
(water) · `l1-battle-dorter.jpg` (dormitory range) · `l1-easby-refectory.jpg` (refectory) ·
`l3-certosa-chiostro.jpg`, `l3-carthusian-cell-plan.png` (the cell-per-monk exception).

## 1. The cloister is never a free-standing courtyard

This is the single most load-bearing relation in the lane, and it is visible on every plan
read: **one of the cloister's four sides is the church wall.** The square is tucked into
the re-entrant angle between the nave's flank and the transept — at St Gall, at Fontenay,
at Strata Florida. The cloister is not "a building wrapped around a void"; it is **a void
pushed up against the one mass that is not negotiable**, with the other three sides built
to close it.

Two consequences worth carrying:

- The church is always on the same side of the yard, and it is always taller than the other
  three ranges. The yard therefore has a permanent *head*, established by mass, before any
  elevation is applied.
- The three built ranges are secondary structures against a primary one. Their heights,
  roofs and openings can all be modest, because the composition's dominance is already
  spent on the church.

## 2. The room ring is fixed by adjacency logic, not by taste

The St Gall plan labels its rooms, so the ring can be read directly, and Fontenay/Strata
Florida confirm the same ring in built stone. Reading clockwise from the church:

| Range | Rooms, in order from the church | Why it sits there |
|---|---|---|
| **Church side** | nave flank; the door from the walk into the church | the community's destination |
| **East** | sacristy → chapter house → parlour → warming room, **dormitory above the whole range**, with a night stair straight down into the transept | the dormitory must reach the church without going outdoors, at night, in silence |
| **South** (far side) | refectory, with the kitchen beyond it, away from the church | fire and smell are put at maximum distance from the altar |
| **West** | cellar, stores, lay-brothers'/servants' range | the outer world's side, nearest the gate and the carts |

The **laver/fountain sits on the walk opposite the refectory door** — you wash on the way
in to eat. The **latrine block (reredorter) hangs off the far end of the dormitory, over
running water**. Both are placements produced by function, and both are legible at a
glance in play: the tap is by the dining hall, the privy is over the drain.

**This is a stronger result than "a ring of rooms."** It is a ring with a *direction of
travel* and two fixed hinge points (church door, refectory door), which is exactly the kind
of thing a solver can honour without hand placement.

## 3. Water is three different objects, not one

The banked ruling says "a well at the yard's heart." The evidence says water appears in
**three distinct roles**, and the big houses have all three:

1. **The lavabo / fountain house.** At Zwettl it is a polygonal vaulted *pavilion* that
   breaks out of the cloister walk and projects into the garth, containing a large circular
   basin with a central jet. At Poblet it is a two-tier basin — an upper dish overflowing in
   a continuous scalloped curtain into a lower drum — standing under the pavilion arcade,
   with the planted garth visible behind. It is on the walk, not in the middle of the yard,
   and it is opposite the refectory.
2. **The well or cistern in the yard.** The plainer version, and the one that survives at
   non-monastic institutions (the Hôtel-Dieu's yard well, lane 3).
3. **The channel.** Cistercian practice is explicitly hydraulic — abbeys were sited in
   valleys partly for the water, and the drains/leats run under the reredorter and the
   kitchen. Water arrives as infrastructure, not as a bucket.

**The fountain house is the more interesting piece for Genesis**, because it is a *break in
the arcade rhythm*: a place where the repeated bay stops and one richer object steps out
into the void. That is a cheap, legible way to keep a fully repeated arcade from reading as
wallpaper.

## 4. The arcade bay unit, measured off the photograph

Fontenay's cloister arcade (`l1-fontenay-cloister-arcade.jpg`) gives the clearest single
bay in the set. The unit is:

> **buttress-pier → one large semicircular relieving arch → within it, two smaller arches
> springing from a single slender central colonnette → below, a solid dado wall about
> waist-to-chest height.** Above the arch, a plain wall band, then the eaves of a tiled
> **shed roof sloping back to the range wall**.

Two proportional readings, stated as proportions because I did not measure metres: the bay
is roughly **as wide as it is tall to the arch crown**, and the **covered walk is about one
bay deep**. Inside (`l1-fontenay-cloister-01.jpg`) the walk is stone-**vaulted** with a
flagged floor; the shed roof is the weather layer over the vault.

**The repeat is nested — bay rhythm plus sub-bay rhythm.** That is why a plain arcade reads
rich without more pieces: one repeated unit is doing two frequencies at once. If Genesis
ships a single arcade piece, the nested version buys the most identity per piece.

Note also `l1-easby-refectory.jpg`: where there is no opening to make, the same arcade motif
returns as **blind arcading** — recessed arches as pure wall articulation along the
refectory's lower walls. The motif is separable from the function.

## 5. The garth is a reserved void, but it is not always empty grass

- Merton's quad (lane 3) and Certosa di Pavia's great cloister: mown grass with a paved
  perimeter path.
- Poblet and Zwettl: **planted** — shrubs, small trees, a hedge behind the fountain.
- Certosa's great cloister is enormous; its garth reads as a *field*, and the arcade runs
  something over twenty bays per side.

The invariant is not "empty" and not "grass." It is: **a single reserved region with at most
one central object, surrounded by a continuous perimeter path.** That matches the
caravanserai finding already banked from the camp study (Camp-Study lane 3 §2 and §6.3 —
"the yard is a reserved void with one central object").

## 6. Scale varies by an order of magnitude, and the arcade absorbs it

Fontenay's cloister is a small square; Certosa di Pavia's great cloister is a field with a
row of individual monks' houses behind the arcade. **The same grammar spans both** because
the bay is the unit of growth: you get a bigger cloister by adding bays, not by changing
pieces. For a procedural generator this is the best news in the lane — courtyard size can be
a rolled scalar with no new geometry.

## 7. The "identical bedroom row" needs a correction

`l1-battle-dorter.jpg` (Battle Abbey) shows what a monastic dormitory range actually is: a
long two-storey block, an **undercroft below and one continuous open hall above**, with a
strict rhythm of lancet windows and buttresses on the outside and a gable end pierced by
stepped lancets. There is no row of doors, because **there is no row of rooms** — the
Benedictine/Cistercian dorter is one room with beds in it, later subdivided by curtains or
low partitions.

The **cell-per-person plan is a specific order's invention**: the Carthusians.
`l3-carthusian-cell-plan.png` (a modern charterhouse, drawn as a plan) shows what a real
cell is — a **two-storey house**: workshop above; below, an entrance lobby, a *cubiculum*
with bed, altar, desk and stove, a wet cell, and **its own walled private garden**, entered
off the cloister walk through a door with a **serving hatch (Durchreiche)** beside it.
`l3-certosa-chiostro.jpg` shows the exterior consequence: behind the great cloister's arcade
runs a line of identical little gabled houses, each with its own chimney.

**So the banked "identical bedroom row along the bottom wall" is true of one monastic family
and false of the mainstream one** — and the Carthusian version is far more gameplay-legible
(door + hatch + private walled garden per cell) than the shared dorter. This is a founder
question, not a correction I can make.

## 8. The chassis already repeats itself inside one monastery

The St Gall plan is not one courtyard. It contains, at minimum:

- the **main claustral square** (church + three ranges);
- a **novices' cloister** — its own small quadrangle with its own chapel, refectory,
  warming room, dormitory and master's room;
- an **infirmary cloister** — the mirror of the novices' one, the two sharing a
  **double-ended chapel** between them;
- the **outer court**, a loose yard of workshops, barns, mills, stables and lodgings;
- two **guest houses**, segregated by rank ("for those of superior rank" and "for the
  poor"), each with its own small court and bath;
- an **outer school** with a schoolmaster's lodging.

That is the institutional-chassis claim proved inside a single historical document: **one
kit, four-plus courts, sorted by who you are.** It also supplies a free composition rule —
the small courts mirror each other across a shared building, which is a cheap way to make a
big site read as planned rather than accreted.

## 9. Two courts, not one — and the gate filters between them

Documentary evidence (Historic England / Cistercians-in-Yorkshire / Britannica-tradition
summaries, see SOURCE-LEDGER "documentary sources") is consistent: a monastic precinct is
divided by an internal wall into an **outer court** and an **inner court**, and the
**gatehouse is the only means of access**, staffed by a porter whose job is to establish who
you are and route you. Visitors were normally admitted only to the **outer** court; the
cloister was closed to them.

Outer court holds: barns, granaries, stables, workshops, smithy, mill, brewhouse,
bakehouse, guest hall, almonry, and often the lay cemetery. Inner court holds: the church,
cloister, and the claustral ranges.

**This pressures the exemplary seed's single yard.** A one-yard monastery is a small
monastery — which may be exactly right for a tactical map. But if the site ever wants a
"you may come this far and no further" beat, the two-court plan is where it lives, and the
inner gate is a second, quieter threshold than the ceremonial one.

## 10. Repetition escapes the buildings and lands on the ground

On the St Gall plan the **garden is drawn as a grid of identical rectangular beds**, and the
**cemetery as a grid of graves with trees planted between them**. The service ranges
(workshops, stables, sheds, poultry houses) are drawn as **rows of identical cells**.

The site's repeated-bay identity is therefore not only an arcade fact. It is a **site-wide
habit**: identical units in rows and grids, on the ground plane as much as in elevation.
Independently, FFT's Public Cemetery map (lane 5, map 100) does exactly this at gameplay
scale — identical stones in loose rows across terraced grass.

## Findings (plain English)

1. **The cloister is always attached to the church, never free-standing.** One of its four
   sides is the church wall, and the church is always the tallest thing on the yard. The
   yard has a head before anyone applies elevation to it.
2. **The room ring is produced by adjacency rules, not by preference.** Dormitory over the
   east range with a night stair into the church; refectory on the far side from the church;
   kitchen beyond the refectory; stores and lay-brothers on the gate side. A solver can
   honour this without hand placement.
3. **The washing point is opposite the refectory door and the privy is over the drain.**
   Two fixed hinge points on the ring that a player can read without being told.
4. **Water is three objects, not one:** a fountain-house pavilion that breaks out of the
   arcade into the garth, a plain yard well, and a buried channel. The pavilion is the
   valuable one — it is the licensed interruption in the repeated bay.
5. **The arcade bay is a nested repeat:** one big relieving arch containing two small arches
   on a shared colonnette, over a solid waist-high dado, under a shed roof. One piece,
   two frequencies. That is where the "rich without more props" economy comes from.
6. **The covered walk is about one bay deep and vaulted inside, with the shed roof as the
   weather layer over the vault.** The banked "covered walkway → shed" assignment is correct
   from outside; the inside wants a vault, not exposed rafters.
7. **Courtyard size is a free variable.** Fontenay's square and Certosa di Pavia's field use
   the same grammar because growth is by adding bays. Yard size can be rolled with no new
   geometry.
8. **The garth is not necessarily empty or grass** — planted garths are common. The
   invariant is one reserved region, at most one central object, continuous perimeter path.
9. **The mainstream monastic dormitory is one long room, not a row of cells.** The row of
   individual cells is the Carthusian exception — and there the cell is a small two-storey
   house with a private walled garden and a serving hatch onto the walk.
10. **A real monastery contains several courtyards of the same kind at different scales** —
    novices, infirmary, guests by rank, outer service court. The chassis is proven to repeat
    inside a single institution, not just across institutions.
11. **The precinct is two courts with a filter between them.** The gatehouse is the only
    entrance and the porter is a router; outsiders get the outer court, never the cloister.
12. **Repetition is a site-wide habit, not an arcade feature.** Gardens as grids of beds,
    cemeteries as grids of graves, service ranges as rows of identical cells.

## Declared gaps

- **No metric bay dimensions.** Everything proportional above is read off photographs, not
  measured drawings. If a bay width in metres is needed for the grid, it has to come from a
  measured survey drawing, which this lane did not obtain.
- **No plan of a *terraced* cloister.** Every plan in this lane is of a level site. That gap
  is the whole subject of lane 2, and it is not closed there either.
- **The room-ring table is drawn primarily from St Gall (an ideal, unbuilt plan) plus two
  Cistercian survivors.** It is a Western-Latin ring. The Orthodox and Himalayan cases
  (lanes 2 and 4) do not share it.
