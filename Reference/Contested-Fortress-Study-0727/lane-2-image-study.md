# Lane 2 — The real-image reference lane

Fifteen images, all from Wikimedia Commons, all opened and read. Licences, sources and
per-image justification are in [`LICENSE-LEDGER.md`](LICENSE-LEDGER.md). What follows is what
each image actually shows and what relation it establishes. Every spatial statement here is
an eyeball reading off a screen image — **nothing in this lane is measured.**

---

## A. The multi-ward machine

### A1 — Beaumaris plan (`a1-beaumaris-plan-eb1911.jpg`)

A 1911 encyclopaedia line plan, tiny (259 × 313 px), reduced to pure topology — which is
exactly why it is the keystone.

What is drawn: a **square inner ward** with a fat drum tower at each corner and two further
drums at the middle of two opposite faces; on the north and south faces, **two great
twin-towered gatehouses** projecting inward as building masses. Wrapped around it, at a
short distance, a **lower polygonal outer curtain** studded with about a dozen small round
turrets. The space between the two walls is a **narrow continuous ring** — nowhere wide.
At the bottom right the outer circuit carries its own gate with a small outwork, and a
channel runs away from it.

The relations that matter:

- **Two closed rings, not one wall.** The identity is the *pair*, and the ring between them.
- **The outer gate is not aligned with the inner gatehouse.** Getting in means entering the
  outer circuit at one point and then travelling along the narrow ring, between two walls,
  to reach the inner gate somewhere else. The lengthening of the path *is* the defence.
- **Turret rhythm on the outer, drums on the inner.** The outer circuit's job is continuous
  flanking coverage — many small projections at regular spacing. The inner's job is mass —
  few large ones.
- **The inner gatehouses are buildings**, comparable in footprint to a whole range. A gate
  at this scale is not an opening; it is the largest single structure on the site.

Honest limit: at this resolution the plan evidences ring count, gate offset and turret
rhythm. It evidences no dimension, no thickness, and nothing interior.

### A2 — Caerphilly from the air (`a2-caerphilly-aerial.jpg`)

The concentric core sits on an island; two lakes wrap it; a long **buttressed screen wall /
dam** runs across the foreground carrying a second, entirely separate gate front; a further
outwork sits across the water at the right.

- **Most of the site is not built on.** Water and open platform dominate; the stone core
  occupies perhaps a fifth of the frame. This is the single best photographic support for
  the honest-empty-space law at fortress scale: a huge footprint with one dense knot.
- **The principal defensive work is water management.** The dam is the fortification. That
  makes the fortress's *infrastructure* the strategic object — a lever, not scenery.
- **There are two separate fronts.** The eastern gatehouse-and-dam problem and the island
  core problem are not the same tactical situation, and an attacker who solves one has not
  solved the other. Two fronts on one site, visible from one viewpoint.

### A3 — Krak des Chevaliers, historic aerial (`a3-krak-aerial-oblique.jpg`)

A spur-top fortress: an outer curtain with regularly spaced towers running round the flank,
and above it the inner group with the tall masses. Below, on every side, a **bare shaved
hillside** falling away, with one road climbing from the lower right and wrapping the base.
A village clings to the slope outside the walls at the left.

- **The approach is the contested ground.** The glacis is enormous, empty and completely
  exposed; crossing it *is* the first tactical problem, and it is made of terrain, not
  masonry.
- **The second population is outside.** A settlement that depends on the fortress but does
  not shelter inside it is a relationship the site owns and the walls do not express.
- **Two enceintes read as two silhouette bands** at gameplay distance — a low ring and a
  high cluster. That is a two-mass composition, which is what the FFT constitution's
  "elevation has hierarchy" rule asks for.

## B. The gate sequence

### B1 — Castle Rushen gate (`b1-rushen-gatehouse-portcullis.jpg`)

One frame, front-on, containing the entire sequence: a **narrow timber bridge** spanning a
deep dry ditch → a **pointed stone arch** → a **lowered spiked portcullis** → behind it a
**studded timber door** → behind that a **second arch** and a further passage.

- **A fortress gate is a sequence of barriers in one opening**, each independently
  operable, with space between them. Four states, not one door state.
- **The approach is single-file by construction.** The bridge is one narrow deck; the ditch
  makes any other line impossible.
- **The flanking wall on both sides is low**, so the gate mass towers over the bridge —
  height differential is built into the approach.

### B2 — Marqab, outer gate seen from the inner works (`b2-marqab-outer-gate-from-inner.jpg`)

Looking back down from inside: the outer gate tower stands at the left with its pointed
passage; from it a **long ramped corridor** climbs to the right along a **battered stone
revetment**, walled on both sides, entirely overlooked from where the camera is. Vaulted
casemates line the far flank; the ward is strewn with fallen blocks.

- **The killing corridor is real geometry, and it is long.** Between outer and inner gate
  the attacker walks a committed, walled, rising path under observation the whole way. This
  is the single most transferable tactical noun in the packet.
- **The corridor climbs.** The defender's advantage is not just cover; it is grade.
- **Casemates flank the corridor** — occupiable interior space opening onto the route,
  which means the corridor has doors into it as well as walls beside it.

### B3 — Castle Rushen portcullis chamber (`b3-rushen-portcullis-chamber.jpg`)

A museum reconstruction inside the gate's upper floor: a **floor slot** through which the
portcullis passes, two figures working a **timber lever** over it, and a third **shooting
from a window** in the same room. Straw on a plank floor; whitewashed rubble walls; a deep
window embrasure.

- **The gate machinery is a staffed room.** "Open the gate" is an interior objective with a
  position, a crew, an occupied approach, and its own defenders — not a lever on a wall.
- **The room does two jobs at once**: it operates the barrier *and* it shoots the ground the
  barrier controls. Taking it does both.
- Honest limit: this is an interpretive display. The costumes are a museum's reading. It is
  cited for the *spatial* claim — slot, lever, crew stations, window — only.

### B4 — Râșnov barbican (`b4-rasnov-barbican.jpg`)

A single tall whitewashed **drum**, wider than a tower and lower than a keep, pierced by one
broad arch. Directly above the arch, a **vertical stack of slots**. Scattered across the
face, small **rectangular recessed loops** at irregular heights. A timber bridge runs into
the arch. Crenellations along the top; a tiled roof visible behind.

- **The cheapest legible barbican in the packet.** One cylinder, one arch, a stack of drop
  slots over the arch, a scatter of recesses. No mouldings, no tracery, no articulation.
  Directly usable by the low-poly translation law.
- **The drop slots are the whole message.** Openings placed *directly above the door* read
  instantly as "this door is defended from above," at any distance.
- **The loops are irregular.** Real defensive openings are placed where the interior floor
  and stair put a person, not on a decorative grid.

## C. The fighting surfaces

### C1 — Cross loophole from inside (`c1-loophole-from-inside.jpg`)

An almost entirely black interior; a cross-shaped slot cut through a deep reveal; through it,
a courtyard in full daylight — a paved yard, a masonry pier, a strip of grass.

- **The sight cone is genuinely tiny.** The vertical slot is perhaps a hand wide; the
  cross-bar widens the lateral view a little, and that is all. If arrow loops are given a
  sight relation in the generator, it must be narrow enough to be a real constraint.
- **The exposure ratio is extreme.** Interior near-black, exterior near-blown. This is the
  hardest possible test of the dark-readability requirement, and it is the *normal*
  condition inside a fortification, not an edge case.
- **The reveal is deep and splayed** — the wall thickness is the feature. This is exactly
  the "openings have depth" law: a dark rectangle on a texture cannot produce this image.

### C2 — Brouage rampart walk (`c2-brouage-rampart-chemin-de-ronde.jpg`)

A **broad paved terreplein** running out of frame, a low **banquette step**, then a tall
ashlar **parapet** above it. To the left, below the parapet's outer face, the tiled roof of a
building at a markedly lower level.

- **At fortress scale the deck is a room, not a ledge.** This walk is several bodies wide
  and runs the length of the work. Site 1's ruled flat walkable deck is a *position*; Site
  9's wall-walk is a *route* — a linear tactical space with its own length, cover, entrances
  and chokepoints.
- **The step-then-parapet section is the cover mechanism.** Stand on the walk: full cover.
  Step up onto the banquette: shoot over, partial cover. Two cover states from one piece of
  construction, chosen by the standee.
- **The walk is above the roofline of what it protects.** The wall overlooks its own town.

### C3 — Golconda merlons (`c3-golconda-machicolation.jpg`)

Two ranks of parapet, one behind and above the other. Each **merlon** is a tall arched mass
carrying its own small openings and, at its base, a **corbelled drop-box** projecting out
over the wall face. Below, a rubble-masonry curtain of large irregular blocks.

- **The defensive edge is a rollable family with radically different silhouettes at the
  same cover class.** This does the same job as a European crenellation and looks nothing
  like it. Site 1's ruled cover-class-equality law generalises upward without change.
- **The merlon is a piece of architecture, not a tooth.** Each carries loops and a
  machicolation. That is a much better geometry buy than notching a wall.
- **Two ranks means two firing levels** — the second rank shoots over the first. Vertical
  layering of the *same* surface.

## D. Non-masonry and cross-cultural

### D1 — Maiden Castle lidar (`d1-maiden-castle-lidar.jpg`)

A lidar hillshade of an oval hilltop wrapped in **three to four concentric bank-and-ditch
rings**, tightest and most numerous at the ends. At the left end, an **entrance made of
overlapping outworks** — banks that run past each other so the way in doubles back on itself.
The interior is a large, near-flat, featureless plateau.

- **The grammar is not masonry-dependent.** Nested enceintes plus a deliberately lengthened
  entrance, executed entirely in earth. The same relational sentence as Beaumaris.
- **The convoluted entrance is Beaumaris's offset gate, built out of dirt.** Both solve the
  same problem — make the way in long, overlooked, and turning — with completely different
  material.
- **The interior is quiet ground and it is the largest single region.** Again: big footprint,
  activity at the edges.
- Honest note: lidar strips vegetation and exaggerates relief through hillshading. It is
  ideal for topology and misleading about how visible any of this is at eye level.

### D2 — Marqab outer ward (`d2-marqab-outer-ward.jpg`)

Foreground: a **wall-walk** on the outer curtain running toward a projecting **round tower**.
Middle: a bare, sloping, rubble-and-scrub **terrace**. Behind and above: the **inner works**
on higher ground, with their own wall line and towers. Far right: the modern road, far below.

- **This is what a front looks like.** Two defended lines at two levels, and between them a
  strip of exposed sloping ground with no cover, overlooked from both sides. Nobody would
  choose to stand there — which is precisely what makes it the contested space.
- **The two lines are at different heights.** The relationship is not just "inner and outer"
  but "lower and upper," which changes every sightline.
- **The outer wall's walk is a real occupiable surface** even where the wall is ruined.

## E. Contested and degraded states

### E1 — Arundel sally port (`e1-arundel-sally-port.jpg`)

Low resolution (593 × 399). At the foot of a stair inside a curved wall, a **grated opening
in the floor**, framed in dressed stone, beside an interpretation board naming it the sally
port.

- Cited only for the **character of the noun**: small, concealed, floor-level, reached from
  inside the wall's thickness, and emphatically **not a gate**. One body at a time.
- This matters because a sally port is the defender's *offensive* affordance, and its cost
  is exactly its size: a counterattack through it is committed, slow and single-file.

### E2 — Corfe keep, slighted (`e2-corfe-keep-slighted.jpg`)

The keep's walls stand in **split, leaning slabs**, one tower half-dropped, the masonry
sheared rather than crumbled. Wall stubs run down a **vast dry grass slope** to a lower gate
mass at the left. The foreground is empty hillside.

- **Slighting is not weathering, and it does not look like it.** Undermined masonry falls in
  coherent leaning blocks, leaving voids and tilted planes. That is a distinct damage
  vocabulary from collapse-by-neglect, and it carries its own story: somebody made sure this
  could never be held again.
- **The glacis survives the ruin.** The great empty slope is still the dominant region.
- **This is the Site 3 boundary in one image.** The building is destroyed; the *relationship*
  is what decides which site owns it.

### E3 — Beaumaris inner ward (`e3-beaumaris-inner-ward.jpg`)

Low resolution panorama (640 × 158). The inner ward is a **large, flat, entirely empty lawn**
ringed by the curtain with drum towers and by the roofless stubs of the ranges, their door
and window openings still legible in the wall face.

- Cited for one fact, which it shows unambiguously: **the most defended space in a concentric
  castle is an empty court.** The ranges are against the wall; the middle is nothing.
- Corollary for the generator: **buildings inside a fortress line the wall.** The centre is
  reserved. That is a placement rule, not a decorating preference.

## Lane findings

1. **The site's identity is two closed lines and the space between them** (A1, A3, D1, D2),
   and that space is deliberately narrow, overlooked, and unpleasant to stand in.
2. **The entrance is lengthened on purpose** — offset gates (A1), a walled climbing corridor
   (B2), or overlapping earthwork outworks (D1). Three materials, one intention.
3. **The gate is a sequence, and its machinery is a staffed room** (B1, B3). Four barrier
   states in one opening; an interior objective above it that both operates and covers it.
4. **The fortress's deck is a route, not a position** (C2), several bodies wide, with a
   two-state cover mechanism built into its section.
5. **The defensive edge is culturally free at constant cover class** (C3) — the strongest
   available support for generalising Site 1's ruled edge-equality law upward.
6. **Openings are deep and their sight cones are narrow** (C1), and the interior/exterior
   exposure ratio inside a fortification is brutal.
7. **Most of a fortress site is quiet ground** (A2, A3, D1, E2, E3) — glacis, water, plateau,
   empty ward. The activity clusters at gates and along walls.
8. **Buildings line the wall; the middle stays open** (E3).
9. **Damage has two distinct vocabularies** — slighted (E2) versus decayed — and only the
   relationship, not the rubble, decides whether the site is still Site 9.
10. **The second population is sometimes outside the walls** (A3), which the architecture
    does not express at all. Relationship is not readable from geometry alone.

## Declared gaps

- **No plan drawing above 259 px.** The only true plan in the packet is A1, and it is tiny.
  A properly legible multi-ward plan and a gatehouse floor plan are both missing.
- **No interior barracks, casemate, granary or cistern image.** Given that lane 4 shows the
  demand is overwhelmingly for fortification *interiors*, this is the most consequential hole
  in the lane.
- **No image of a fortress containing an inhabited civilian quarter** — the reading lane 1
  and lane 4 both identify as dominant. The fortified-town case is entirely un-evidenced
  photographically here.
- **No dzong, broch, kasbah, or timber/palisade fortification.** The cultural axis rests on
  one earthwork (D1) and one Indian merlon detail (C3).
- Nothing measured. Every "narrow," "broad," "several bodies wide" in this lane is eyeball.
