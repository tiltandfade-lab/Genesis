# Lane 3 — Hillside streets, stair streets, and the street that looks down on another street

**The question.** Site 10's third deck expression is ruled as *terrain-borne street elevation* —
Adam's words: "imagine elevation differences, like a winding park trail that is descending, with
a level street on either side that looks over the trail's descent… think of that road in San
Francisco." This lane went looking for whether that is a real, repeating way of building towns or
a one-off tourist curiosity, what physically sits at the boundary between the upper street and the
lower one, and what it would cost Genesis to build.

**Short version.** The ruling survives, and it survives more strongly than the phrasing suggests.
Lombard Street is a novelty, but the *class* it belongs to is enormous, has its own municipal
category in at least two countries, and has been built continuously since the Middle Ages. What
does **not** survive contact with the evidence is the idea of putting Lombard Street itself on a
tray: at true scale the famous block is roughly 82 cells long and drops something like ten storeys.
The pattern has to be sampled, not reproduced. The good news is that the repeating unit — one
hairpin and one planted bed — is about ten cells square and drops about 5h, which is exactly
tray-sized.

Units used throughout: one grid cell = 5 ft; one height unit **h** = 2.5 ft; one storey = 4h = 10 ft
(`STRUCTURE-KIT-CATALOG` §1). Where a number is **cited** I say so and name the source; where it is
my own arithmetic on a cited number I mark it **derived**; where I measured it by eye off a
photograph I mark it **eyeball**. There are more eyeball reads in this lane than I would like — see
Declared gaps.

## 1. Lombard Street, read cold

*Evidence: `lane3-lombard-crooked-section.jpg` (inside the switchbacks, at eye level);
`lane3-lombard-crooked-block-aerial.jpg` (the whole block from Coit Tower).*

**Cited (Wikipedia, *Lombard Street (San Francisco)*):** the crooked block is "about 600 feet
(180 m) long (412.5 feet (125.7 m) straight line)", has "eight sharp turns", was "built in 1922",
was "first suggested by property owner Carl Henry", is "one-way (downhill) and is paved with red
bricks", runs "between Hyde and Leavenworth streets", and was "intended to reduce the hill's
natural 27 percent grade, which was too steep for most vehicles". The recommended speed at the top
is 5 mph. **Cited but secondary only** (agreed across a dozen travel and local-history sites, never
found in a primary engineering document): the design was executed by city engineer **Clyde Healy**
and brought the grade down to **16%**.

What the photographs actually show, which the text does not:

- **It is not a road with scenery beside it. It is a road with garden in the middle of it.** Every
  hairpin is filled by a kerbed planting bed. The bed is what makes the turn legible — remove the
  planting and you have an ordinary wide apron of brick. The *kerb* is doing the gameplay work
  (it stops you cutting the corner); the flowers are decoration.
- **The stairs are part of the street, not an addition to it.** Both flanks of the switchback are
  pedestrian stairs running straight down the fall line while the roadway zigzags across them.
  So the block contains, simultaneously, a `walk` ramp for wheels and a `walk` stair for feet, and
  they cross each other eight times.
- **The houses do not follow the road.** Each one sits on its own flat pad held up by its own
  retaining wall, with its own railed garden terrace. The road threads between the pads. The grey
  house on the left of `lane3-lombard-crooked-section.jpg` is entered from a level *above* the
  hairpin its garden overlooks — a domestic version of exactly the ruled relation.
- **The level cross streets are real and they matter.** In the aerial you can see Leavenworth at
  the bottom and Hyde at the top, both dead level, both lined with parked cars, both looking
  straight into the descent. The switchback is a *thing framed by two level streets*, which is the
  ruling almost verbatim.

**Derived, and this is the number that should govern the design:**

| quantity | value | how |
|---|---|---|
| block length, plan | 412.5 ft = **82.5 cells** | cited length ÷ 5 ft |
| road length, actual | 600 ft = 120 cells of pavement | cited |
| path lengthening bought by the zigzag | **1.45×** | 600 ÷ 412.5 |
| total drop, upper bound | ~111 ft = **~44h ≈ 11 storeys** | 27% × 412.5 ft |
| drop per hairpin | ~14 ft = **~5.5h** | ÷ 8 turns |
| plan size of one hairpin + its bed | **~10 × 10 cells** (eyeball off the aerial) | — |

A consistency note in the interests of honesty: 27% ÷ 1.45 = 18.6%, not the 16% everyone reports.
Either the natural grade was not a uniform 27% across the whole block, or there was cut-and-fill as
well as lengthening. I could not resolve it. Treat the 111 ft drop as an **upper bound**.

**The mechanism, stated plainly, because it generalises:** a switchback buys gradient with plan
area and nothing else. It is the only move available when you may not re-cut the hill and may not
close the street. That is why it recurs on mountain roads, mine haulage roads, and castle ramps —
and it is already named in Genesis's own list of twelve structural coverage dimensions
(`BATTLEMAP-TOWNTRAY-COMPOSITION` §10.3: "switchback ascent"). The site 10 deck ruling and that
coverage dimension are the same thing arriving from two directions.

## 2. Does it generalise? Yes — three named classes, one of them inventoried by a city

This is question (a), and the answer is unambiguous.

**Class 1 — the stair street: a street right-of-way that is stairs instead of pavement.**

- *San Francisco.* Filbert Street's eastern end simply stops being drivable and becomes the
  **Filbert Steps**, "a residential step street that climbs Telegraph Hill over a series of 400
  steps, with houses and public gardens on either side of the steps", divided into upper and lower
  portions by Montgomery Street (**cited**, Wikipedia *Filbert Street (San Francisco)*). Popular
  inventories put the city's public stairways somewhere between 369 documented and "900+" claimed
  — the count varies by methodology, so I would not quote a figure, only "hundreds".
- *New York.* The strongest single citation in this lane. NYC's Department of Design and
  Construction states flatly: **"There are 64 step streets in the Bronx."** These are municipal
  infrastructure with capital budgets — the Clifford Place Step Street rebuild cost $9.4 M, the
  West 229th Street one $10.7 M (**cited**, nyc.gov DDC press releases, 2019).
- *Everywhere in the Mediterranean.* `lane3-norma-via-cavour-steps.jpg` (Norma, Lazio) and
  `lane3-accettura-stepped-lane.jpg` (Accettura, Basilicata) are ordinary residential streets that
  happen to be made of steps, wall to wall, with front doors on them. Matera's
  `lane3-matera-gradoni-santantonio.jpg` shows one with a **street name plate** — *Gradoni
  Sant'Antonio* — which is the clearest possible proof that the culture treats it as a street and
  not as a staircase.

**Class 2 — the two-level street: one frontage serving two datums at once.**

- *Chester.* The Rows are a continuous first-floor walkway set back inside the buildings, covered
  by the storey above, with "railings and an area which was used as shelves or stalls for the
  display of goods" on the street side; the ground-floor premises are "entered by going down a few
  steps" and sit "a half-storey rather than a full-storey level below the street" (**cited**,
  Wikipedia *Chester Rows*). About twenty medieval stone undercrofts survive beneath.
  `lane3-chester-rows-watergate.jpg` and `lane3-chester-rows-bridge-street.jpg` show it: two shop
  levels stacked, the upper one open to the air behind a balustrade.
- *Edinburgh.* Victoria Street descends in a curve; **Victoria Terrace** runs level along the top
  of the shop range on its north side, railed, with its own buildings fronting onto it. This is the
  ruled relation built in stone: `lane3-edinburgh-victoria-street-and-terrace.jpg` and
  `lane3-edinburgh-victoria-terrace-railings.jpg`.

**Class 3 — the street bridged over by another street.**

- *Edinburgh again.* South Bridge (1788) carries a level street over the Cowgate. **Cited:** 19
  arches over a chasm "just over 1000 feet long", "at its highest point it stood 31 feet above
  ground", the Cowgate span "looks about 9m (30ft)" and the other sixteen are ~6 m. Only the
  Cowgate arch is visible; the rest are buried in buildings. See
  `lane3-cowgate-south-bridge-1829.jpg` (Shepherd's engraving — the relation drawn at its clearest),
  `lane3-cowgate-under-south-bridge.jpg` (from the lower street today), and
  `lane3-cowgate-from-bridge-above.jpg` (the overlook, from the upper street).

**Verdict on (a):** Lombard Street is a one-off. "Level street overlooking a lower one" is not.
It has at least three distinct construction traditions, one of them with a municipal inventory
attached, and it is the normal condition of any town built on a slope.

## 3. What is actually at the boundary — the answer to (b)

Six real solutions were observed. Ranked by what they cost in low-poly geometry, cheapest first.

| # | solution | where seen | geometry cost | access reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Blind retaining face, no top edge at all** — the upper ground just stops | Filbert Steps' rendered flank wall; Matera's terraces | one extruded box; the kit already owns "terrace retaining face (1h/2h)" | face is `climb-dc` if smooth, `climb-cost` if rubble; the top is `walk` |
| 2 | **Rubble parapet / stone upstand on top of the retaining face** | Matera (`lane3-matera-scalinate-sassi.jpg`) | same box, taller; kit's "parapet run" | grants cover at 2h; edge is not crossable |
| 3 | **Kerbed planting bed as the separator** | Lombard's hairpins | a low box + a paint-and-prop fill | blocks the corner-cut; not standable |
| 4 | **Building whose roof IS the upper pavement** | Victoria Terrace over the Victoria Street shops | free — you were building the shop range anyway | the upper street is a roof plate; the shop is the retaining structure |
| 5 | **Open iron railing / balustrade** | Victoria Terrace; Chester Rows; South Bridge parapet; Filbert Steps | expensive — thin repeated members, the classic low-poly tax | see-through, no cover, `none` to cross |
| 6 | **An arch or bridge carrying the upper street clean over the lower** | South Bridge / Cowgate; Matera's *Gradoni* portal | a gate arch (kit already owns "gate arch") plus a deck | the lower street stays `walk` and passes underneath |

Two things worth flagging to Adam rather than deciding here:

- **Solution 4 is the cheapest and the most beautiful, and Genesis is already built for it.** At
  Victoria Terrace the level upper street is literally the flat roof of the row of shops below.
  Genesis's site 10 is already buying a multi-storey street wall with flat-deck roofs; making one
  side's roof deck *be* the upper street costs nothing new and gets the ruled relation for free.
- **Solution 5 is the one that will hurt.** Real overlooks are railed, not walled, precisely
  because people want to see over them — and open railings are the worst shape for a low-poly
  budget. Worth a founder ruling on whether the game's overlook edge is a solid upstand with the
  railing painted on, or a genuine open rail reserved for a handful of hero edges.

## 4. How a building meets a sloping street — the answer to (c)

**Buildings step. They do not ramp.** Every photograph in this lane agrees, and there are no
counter-examples in the set.

- `lane3-norma-via-cavour-steps.jpg` is the clearest: a continuous wall plane runs up the stepped
  lane, but the *openings* jump. Each front door sits on a landing at its own level, with one or
  two private steps up to the threshold. The floor inside is level; the street outside is not.
- `lane3-chester-rows-watergate.jpg` shows the same rule applied to a walkway: the Row itself steps
  from building to building, with short flights between neighbours, rather than sloping.
- `lane3-lombard-crooked-block-aerial.jpg` shows the San Francisco version — each house on its own
  levelled pad behind its own retaining wall, and the road ramping past the pads.
- The **splayed base** (a single wedge-shaped plinth that follows the slope under one long
  building) appears only where one structure must cross the slope without being subdivided. The
  best example in the set is the triangular rendered flank at the foot of the Filbert Steps
  (`lane3-filbert-steps-from-base.jpg`) — and that is a retaining wall, not a house.

**What the door does:** it sits on a level landing, one to three steps above the tread it faces.
Nobody puts a door mid-flight.

**Why this matters for Genesis:** the engine already requires it. `STRUCTURE-KIT-CATALOG` §1 says
"every standable surface sits at n×h". So the honest translation is: *buildings snap to n×h
datums; the street between them is what slopes.* The hillside street costs the buildings nothing —
it costs the ground.

## 5. Stair-street construction — the answer to (e)

**There are two species of stair street, and they behave completely differently on a 2.5-ft
height quantum.**

**Species A — the Mediterranean *gradonata* (ramp-steps).** `lane3-norma-via-cavour-steps.jpg`
and `lane3-accettura-stepped-lane.jpg`. Treads are very long and risers very low — **eyeball**
roughly 1.0–1.5 m tread against a 0.15 m riser, giving an effective slope near 12%, barely steeper
than a footpath. Construction: cobble or rough flag infill between a single dressed stone nosing
course per step. The steps run the **full width of the lane, wall to wall** — there is no separate
footway, because there is no traffic to be separated from. This form exists to let a loaded mule up.

*Grid consequence:* a 1.2 m tread is about ¾ of one 5-ft cell and a 0.15 m riser is a sixteenth of
one h. **You cannot model this step-for-step.** The truthful translation is a run of cells at one
datum, then a 1h break, then another run — roughly **one 1h break every 4 to 8 cells**. Every part
of it is `walk` (the kit's free step-up is ≤1h). The individual steps become paint.

**Species B — the engineered stair street.** Filbert Steps, and the Bronx step streets. Risers are
proper stair risers. The Clifford Place rebuild is **cited** in useful detail: "78 steps and seven
landings", "800 square feet of stone" of retaining wall, "more than 300 feet of new hand rails",
"eight new street lights featuring 75-watt LED lights", "a new internal drainage system", and "a
new granite bike channel helps people walk their bikes up and down".

*Derived, on an assumed 6–7 in riser (I could not find the actual riser height):* 78 steps ≈ 39–46 ft
of rise ≈ **16–18h ≈ 4 storeys**, broken by 7 landings, so a landing roughly every 11 steps ≈ every
2h of climb. Two handrails over ~150 ft of plan ≈ **30 cells** of stair.

*Grid consequence:* a 1h rise takes four or five real steps, so Species B is a **built stair run**
piece — 1h per cell, continuous, `walk`.

**Landings.** Both species use them, and they do three jobs at once: they let the flights change
direction (`lane3-filbert-steps-from-base.jpg` shows a clean dogleg), they give doors somewhere
level to open onto, and they are where the street furniture goes. In Genesis terms a landing is
simply a floor plate at n×h — and it is the natural place to put the fight.

**What happens where a stair street meets a cross street.** Two patterns:

1. **It just arrives.** `lane3-filbert-steps-treads.jpg` shows the Filbert Steps meeting Napier
   Lane at a landing, with a cast-iron pole carrying a normal green-and-white **street name sign**.
   The junction is signed like any other junction.
2. **It goes through a hole in a building.** `lane3-matera-gradoni-santantonio.jpg` is the best
   image in this lane: the level upper street (Via Duomo, broad stone flags) meets the stepped
   street through a **vaulted arch cut through the ground floor of a house**, with the house's
   upper room bridging over the top of the stair. You do not see the descent until you are in the
   arch, and then you see the roofs of the lower town framed by it.

Pattern 2 is worth Adam's attention: it is a **gate arch** (a piece the kit already owns), it is
the single strongest "there is a lower world down there" reveal in the whole image set, and it
converts a mere elevation change into a threshold you can hold, block, or ambush.

**Doors on a stair street** open directly onto the treads and landings, at whatever level the
tread happens to be, each with its own one-to-three-step stoop. Openings are frequent — this is a
residential frontage, not a blank flank.

## 6. What makes the overlook READ — the answer to (d)

Judged from the two photographs in the set that are taken from an actual fixed overlook —
`lane3-cowgate-from-bridge-above.jpg` (from South Bridge, looking down the Cowgate) and
`lane3-matera-scalinate-sassi.jpg` (from an upper terrace over the Sassi) — seven visual facts do
the work, in roughly this order of strength:

1. **You can see the tops of things.** Roofs in the near field. In the Cowgate photograph the first
   thing in frame is the slate roof of the corner building — that single fact establishes "I am
   above this" before you read anything else.
2. **A continuous edge line crosses the frame, with a beat of nothing behind it.** The parapet or
   railing, then a gap, then the lower ground resumes. The gap is the whole trick; without it the
   edge reads as a wall.
3. **Figures at two sizes.** People on the lower street read small at the same screen height as
   people on the upper one. Both Matera and the Cowgate shots have this.
4. **The lower surface is visible as a band** — you see the road's plan, not just its far wall.
5. **A connector is in shot.** A stair or a ramp physically joining the two, so the eye can walk it.
   Matera shows three separate connectors in one frame.
6. **The upper edge casts its shadow onto the lower surface.** Extremely strong in
   `lane3-matera-scalinate-sassi.jpg` — the parapet's shadow lies across the lower terrace and
   instantly says "that thing is up, this thing is down".
7. **Something vertical spans both levels** — a lamp post, a tree, or best of all a building whose
   windows appear on both levels (the Cowgate tenements do exactly this).

**The failure mode**, stated as a warning: if the camera sits so that the parapet is at the bottom
edge of the frame and the lower street is hidden behind it, the overlook reads as a plain wall and
the whole deck is wasted. **A fixed diorama camera must be able to see the upper surface, the lower
surface, and at least one connector simultaneously.** That is a real constraint on where the
descent can be placed in the tray, and it is the one thing in this lane I would put in front of
Adam before anything is modelled.

## 7. The geometry/paint split for hillside-street parts (§5 applied)

**Geometry** (gameplay-legible): retaining faces · parapets and copings (cover at 2h) · stair runs
and terrain stair cuts · landings and floor plates · the kerb of a planting bed (it blocks the
corner-cut) · gate arches over a sunken lane · the building bridging over a stair · doorways onto
landings · any surface a figure can stand on.

**Paint** (dressing): individual tread nosings and cobble coursing on a *gradonata* · the flowers
and shrubs inside a planting bed · wear-dishing in the middle of the treads · the bike/barrow
channel beside a stair (a 3-ft strip is sub-cell) · water staining and moss on the retaining face ·
the paving pattern of the upper street.

**The one genuinely contested item is the railing** — see §3, solution 5. It is fall protection and
a strong overlook cue, which argues geometry; it is thin repeated members with no cover value,
which argues paint on a solid upstand. Not mine to rule.

## 8. Who owns the light (the causal light law, in town, on a hill)

Every light source visible in this lane's photographs has a physical owner, and there is a clear
pattern in *what kind* of owner.

- **Wall-bracket lanterns dominate.** Matera's stepped terrace carries a black conical-shaded
  lantern on a bracket bolted into the masonry (`lane3-matera-scalinate-sassi.jpg`); the *Gradoni*
  arch has a bracket lamp beside it; Victoria Terrace has one mounted on the church wall
  (`lane3-edinburgh-victoria-terrace-railings.jpg`); Chester's Rows carry a large hanging lantern on
  a wrought-iron bracket at walkway level (`lane3-chester-rows-bridge-street.jpg`).
- **The reason is structural, not aesthetic:** on a stepped or sunken street there is no verge to
  plant a pole in, and there is a wall within arm's reach on both sides. So *hillside streets are
  bracket-lit; open streets are pole-lit.* The Filbert Steps, which run between gardens rather than
  walls, are the exception that proves it — there the lamp hangs off a timber utility pole
  (`lane3-filbert-steps-from-base.jpg`).
- **Cited count:** the Bronx's Clifford Place step street got "eight new street lights" over 78
  steps — roughly one lamp every ten steps, or one per ~5 ft of climb. A stair street is well lit,
  and it owns its lamps.
- **On a two-level street the owners appear at both levels**, and the upper lamp spills light onto
  the lower surface. That spill is itself an overlook cue (§6, fact 6) — the light and the read
  come from the same object.

## 9. Sunken lanes — the ruled image, without any buildings at all

*Evidence: `lane3-hollow-way-clun.jpg`.*

Worth separating out because it is the closest thing in the set to Adam's actual words. A hollow
way is a track worn and eroded below the level of the land on either side — here, near Clun in
Shropshire, the surface sits **eyeball 4–6 ft (about 2h)** below the fields, with earth banks and
exposed tree roots on both rims and a hedge line along the top of each bank. There are no buildings
and no masonry. It is purely "a descending lane with level ground on either side that looks over
it".

Two useful consequences:

- **The relation does not require architecture.** A 2h earth cut with a rim on both sides already
  produces the overlook, the height advantage, and the ambush. This is the cheapest possible form
  of the ruled deck, and it would work in a wilderness tray as readily as an urban one.
- **It gives the town's version an origin story.** A sunken urban lane is a hollow way that got
  built on: the banks become retaining walls, the rims become the level streets, and the buildings
  arrive on top. That reading connects site 10's third deck to the road-grown-walls capsule without
  inventing anything.

## 10. What it costs — the answer to (f)

Compared honestly against the other two ruled deck expressions.

**In new geometry, the terrain-borne street is the cheapest of the three, by some distance.**
Everything it needs is already on the fine-tier piece list in `STRUCTURE-KIT-CATALOG` §7 for other
sites: *terrain slab · terrace retaining face (1h/2h) · terrain stair cut · built stair run ·
parapet run (merlons + coping) · floor plate · gate arch.* It introduces **no new piece family**.
By contrast:

- **The roofline deck** needs walkable pitched roof planes with a walkable/guarded split per piece,
  eave trim, honest access, and it carries an open visual dependency — the standee-base rework that
  lets figures stand on slopes without looking broken (already flagged in the catalog).
- **The balcony deck** has **no piece in the fine-tier list at all**. It is a new cantilevered
  frontage element with an open rail — the expensive shape — and it is needed per bay, across the
  whole frontage.

**But it is the most expensive of the three in plan area, and in per-instance authoring.**

- Slope eats cells. A switchback spends its whole length on the turn; a retaining wall spends a
  cell on its face; a stair spends cells on its run. The Lombard block wants 82 cells before you
  have placed a single building.
- It removes the frontage's cheapest trick. A level street lets you repeat one bay extrusion along
  a wall. A sloping street forces every bay to declare its own datum. That is authoring cost per
  instance, not modelling cost per piece — but it is real and it recurs on every tray.

**And one thing it does *not* cost, which is worth saying plainly.** Genesis's walkable-slope limit
is ≤30°, which is a **58% grade**. San Francisco's steepest legal street block is **31.5%** (cited,
Filbert Street) = 17.5°. Lombard's untamed hill was 27% = 15.1°. **Every real hillside street in
this lane is comfortably walkable ground under the existing slope law.** The access classes only
ever come into play at the *edges* — the retaining faces, which are `climb-dc` when smooth and
`climb-cost` when rubble — never on the road surface itself.

## Findings (plain English)

1. **The ruling holds.** "A level street looking over a descending one" is a real and very common
   way of building on a slope. It has at least three distinct traditions — the stair street, the
   two-level street, and the street bridged over by another street — and New York's own DDC says
   there are **64 step streets in the Bronx** alone. Lombard Street is a novelty; its class is not.

2. **Lombard Street itself will not fit on a tray, and shouldn't be asked to.** At true scale the
   crooked block is about 82 cells long and drops roughly ten storeys. But the *repeating unit* —
   one hairpin plus the planted bed inside it — is about ten cells square and drops about 5h.
   Build the unit, not the street.

3. **A switchback buys gradient with plan area and nothing else.** Lombard's eight turns make the
   pavement 1.45 times longer than the block, and that ratio *is* the gradient reduction. That is
   the whole mechanism, it applies to castle ramps and mine roads too, and Genesis already lists
   "switchback ascent" as one of its twelve structural coverage dimensions.

4. **The cheapest and prettiest boundary between an upper and a lower street is a building.** At
   Victoria Terrace in Edinburgh the level upper street is literally the flat roof of the shop
   range below it, edged with a railing. Site 10 is already buying multi-storey street walls with
   flat-deck roofs — making one side's roof deck *be* the upper street gets the ruled relation
   essentially for free.

5. **Buildings step; they never ramp.** On every stepped street in the evidence the wall runs
   continuously but the doors jump, each one on its own landing with a one-to-three-step private
   stoop. Genesis's rule that every standable surface sits at n×h is already the correct rule. The
   hillside street costs the buildings nothing — it costs the ground.

6. **There are two kinds of stair street and they translate differently.** The Mediterranean kind
   has treads about a metre long and risers about six inches — too shallow to model step by step;
   it becomes a run of level cells with a 1h break every four to eight cells. The engineered kind
   (Filbert, the Bronx) has real stair risers and becomes a built stair run at 1h per cell. Both
   are `walk` throughout.

7. **Landings are where the game happens.** Real stair streets break into flights at landings —
   Clifford Place has 78 steps and 7 landings — and landings are simultaneously where the flights
   turn, where the doors open, and where the street furniture stands. In Genesis a landing is just
   a floor plate at n×h, and it is the natural fighting platform on a stair.

8. **Where a stepped street meets a level one, the best move in the evidence is an arch through a
   building.** Matera's *Gradoni Sant'Antonio* is entered through a vaulted hole cut under a house
   whose upper room bridges the stair. It uses a piece the kit already owns, it hides the descent
   until you are inside it, and it turns a change in level into a threshold you can hold.

9. **An overlook reads from a fixed camera when you can see roofs in the near field, an edge line
   with a beat of nothing behind it, the lower street's surface as a band, and at least one stair
   or ramp joining the two.** The upper edge's shadow falling across the lower surface is the
   single most convincing cue. The failure mode is real: if the parapet sits at the bottom of frame
   and hides the lower street, the whole deck reads as a plain wall.

10. **Hillside streets are lit from brackets on walls, not from poles.** Because there is no verge
    to plant a pole in and there is always a wall within reach. Matera, Chester, and Edinburgh all
    do it; the Filbert Steps, which run between gardens rather than walls, use a pole instead. On
    a two-level street the lamps appear at both levels, and the upper lamp's spill onto the lower
    surface is itself part of what makes the overlook read.

11. **The sunken lane gives the same relation with no architecture at all.** A hollow way is a
    track worn about 2h below the fields with banks on both sides — Adam's "winding descending
    trail with level ground either side" almost exactly, and the cheapest possible version of the
    deck. It also supplies an origin story for the urban case: the banks become retaining walls,
    the rims become the level streets, and the buildings arrive on top.

12. **The cost verdict: cheapest in new geometry, dearest in plan area.** The terrain deck needs no
    new piece family — every part is already on the fine-tier list for other sites. The balcony
    deck has no piece at all yet, and the roofline deck carries the standee-base dependency. But
    the terrain deck eats cells for its slope, and it forces every frontage bay to declare its own
    datum instead of repeating one extrusion. And it never needs a climb roll on the road: at 30°
    the walkable limit is a 58% grade, and San Francisco's steepest legal street is 31.5%.

## Declared gaps

State these as limits on how far the above can be trusted.

- **No historic tread/riser figures were found for stepped streets.** Everything in §5 about
  Mediterranean *gradonata* proportions (roughly 1.0–1.5 m tread, 0.15 m riser) is **my eye on two
  photographs**, calibrated against the people and doors in frame. The only cited numbers I have
  are modern code bounds (riser 4–7 in, tread ≥11 in; accessibility guidance max 0.15 m riser,
  min 0.30 m tread), which describe a different kind of stair. If the 1h-break-every-4-to-8-cells
  translation is going to become law, someone should measure a real one.

- **The Bronx step street's rise is derived from an assumed riser height.** "78 steps and seven
  landings" is cited; "16–18h" is my arithmetic on a guessed 6–7 in riser. The real figure is not
  in the source.

- **Lombard's 111 ft drop is an upper bound and the numbers do not fully reconcile.** 27% ÷ 1.45 =
  18.6%, not the 16% that every secondary source reports. Either the natural grade was not uniform
  or there was regrading. I could not find a primary engineering source; the 16% figure and the
  attribution to city engineer Clyde Healy are **secondary only**.

- **Every vertical dimension not explicitly cited is an eyeball read.** Specifically: Victoria
  Terrace's height above Victoria Street (~5–6h), the Chester Rows walkway above the pavement
  (~3–4h), the Clun hollow way's banks (~2h), and the ten-cells-square figure for one Lombard
  hairpin. None of these were measured from a drawing.

- **No measured drawing, section, or survey was obtained for anything in this lane.** I looked for
  a Chester Rows section and a Filbert Steps HABS drawing and found neither on Commons. Every
  geometric claim here comes from photographs plus prose descriptions, which is a weaker evidence
  base than the guard-post study's.

- **Chester's own sources give no heights.** The Wikipedia article explicitly provides no dimensions
  beyond "a half-storey rather than a full-storey level below the street"; I did not consult the
  Chester Rows Research Project or the Victoria County History, which probably do.

- **Whole subjects went uncovered.** Guanajuato's tunnel streets, Lisbon's *calçada* stairways,
  Greek island stepped lanes, Perugia's escalators threaded through medieval fabric, and Chefchaouen
  all appear in the brief and none are represented in the image set — Commons' full-text search kept
  returning scanned books rather than photographs, and I stopped at the sixteen-image budget rather
  than keep fighting it. The Italian and Anglo-Scottish examples I did get are probably not
  representative of the whole Mediterranean range.

- **The San Francisco stairway count is unreliable.** Sources range from 369 documented to "1,000+".
  I have quoted "hundreds" and would not go further.

- **I did not test any of this against the FFT corpus.** That is lane 5's job, and I deliberately
  stayed out of it. In particular, the claim in §6 about what makes an overlook read from a fixed
  camera is derived from *photographs* taken by people standing at overlooks — not from a diorama
  camera, and not from a single game frame. It should be re-tested against FFT's hillside maps
  before anyone relies on it.

- **Nothing here is a ruling.** In particular the railing question (§3, §7), the
  camera-must-see-both-surfaces constraint (§6), and the arch-as-threshold suggestion (§5) are
  offered as things for Adam to decide, not as decisions.
