# Urban Culture Study — synthesis

**Nothing here is locked. Adam rules; this file evidences.**

## 1. The one-sentence result

Across seven lanes and fifteen texts, pre-industrial urban life converges on this: **a town is a
machine for sorting — sorting the trades into streets, the hours into signals, the strangers into
admissions, the offenders into processions, and the year into states — and every one of those
sortings is owned by somebody, marked on something physical, and contested.**

The design consequence, stated once: **for site 10, "what time is it" and "whose law is running"
are the same question**, and both of them are more powerful than "what does it look like."

## 2. The six structural findings

These are the results that survived across multiple lanes and multiple sources. Candidates, not
schema.

**(1) TIME IS A RULES PARAMETER, NOT A LIGHTING PARAMETER.** The same plaza at the market signal
and at curfew is a different legal regime with a different population and different consequences
for standing in it. Evidence: the forestalling crime window (cockcrow to six); the double curfew
ringing and the half-hour street-emptying lag; night-walking punished at *double* the fine for
shooting at a proctor; the market hall's three showing days a week; the 4 a.m. mayor; the raker's
horn; the gong farmers' nine-o'clock start; and, from outside Europe, Chang'an's markets opening
on 300 drum strokes at noon and closing on 300 gong beats an hour and three-quarters before dusk.

**(2) THE SIGNAL HAS AN OWNER, AND THE OWNER MAY NOT BE THE TOWN.** Winchester's curfew bell rang
from land that defied the mayor; the French communes received and forfeited the right to a belfry
by charter; the belfry doubled as the prison, and at Ypres the magistrates were locked inside it.
"Who rings the hours here" is a faction fact that costs nothing to roll and changes everything
about an arrival.

**(3) TRADES SORT, DRIFT, AND LEAVE THEIR NAMES BEHIND — AND THE ONES THAT DON'T DRIFT ARE THE
ONES BOUND TO WATER OR TO NUISANCE.** Stow's migration inventory is the cleanest evidence in the
study: mercers, goldsmiths, grocers, drapers, skinners and ironmongers all moved "as they have
found their best advantage," while brewers stayed "near to the friendly water of Thames," butchers
stayed in the shambles, founders stayed in Lothbury, and the patten-makers' street outlived the
patten-makers. The souk supplies a second, sharper rule for a different culture: **distance from
the sacred centre equals cleanliness rank**, with perfumers and booksellers at the mosque and
tanners and dyers at the gates.

**(4) THE INSTITUTION'S AUTHORITY IS AN OBJECT, AND THE OBJECT CAN BE TAKEN OR BROKEN.** The
standard weights are carried off to the bishop's Pavilion for the fair; the common beam is kept in
the hall and rented out; the Gloucester burghers rang the common bell and destroyed the lord's
tumbrel and pillory; the Exeter mayor lowered his mace at a boundary line and handed back a livery
coat in a private chamber. And a magistrate physically stationed **in** the market appears in four
independent traditions — the European clerk of the market, the *muhtasib* on his whipping stand,
Tlatelolco's three judges and constables, and Kinsay's two facing court-palaces per square.

**(5) PUNISHMENT AND PROCESSION ARE THE SAME MACHINE — A ROUTED PUBLIC WALK THAT DECLARES RANK.**
The baker is dragged on a hurdle through the busiest street and then through the dirtiest ones,
with the loaf round his neck; the quack is led round the whole city with trumpets, a whetstone and
two chamber pots; the Corpus Christi host stops at named stations under arches of green boughs;
the marching watch covers a measured 3,200 yards with 700 cressets whose supply quota *is* the
civic hierarchy. Shame and glory use the same streets, the same crowd, and the same logic.

**(6) THE STRONGEST DRESSING IS A MARK APPLIED BY AN AUTHORITY TO SOMEBODY ELSE'S PROPERTY.** A
slit ear on a condemned pig; a red cross a foot long with four words above it; a door chalked
overnight for showing a light; a name posted on the Disfranchised Table; a painted badge on a
hired man's straw hat; a whetstone hung round a liar's neck. Six items, one grammar, and every one
of them is a sentence an in-world observer can read.

## 5. THE FOUNDER PACKET

Ten questions, in plain English. Every one of them is a genuine fork the evidence opened; none of
them is a request to approve something I have already decided.

**1. Should the hour of the day change what is *allowed* at an urban site, and not just how it
looks?**
Everything I read points the same way. Buying before the market signal is a crime; being outdoors
after curfew is a crime in itself, punished at Oxford at double the rate for shooting at a
proctor; the market hall only shows goods three days a week; the mayor inspects at four in the
morning in summer; the night-soil men may not start before nine. If time of day is a rules
parameter, then an urban arrival at dawn and an urban arrival at dusk are genuinely different
encounters generated from the same site. If it is only a lighting parameter, most of what this
study found stays on the page.

**2. Should "who rings the hours here" be one of the things the dice decide about a town?**
At Winchester the curfew bell hung on ground where the mayor's writ did not run, and everybody
knew it. In the French communes the right to have a belfry and a town hall was granted by charter
and taken away when the commune was suppressed. The bell tower was also the prison, and at Ypres
the rioters locked the magistrates in it. It costs nothing to roll and it tells a player who is
really in charge before anyone speaks.

**3. Do you want a "the fair is on" state that suspends the town's ordinary law while the party
is there?**
This is the strongest single time-based switch I found and it is not a fantasy. At Winchester,
once a year, the bishop took the city: all civic government was suspended, every shop in and
around the town was closed by his order, the town's own weights and measures were physically
carried off to his Pavilion to be tested, and the citizens had to bring even their bloodshed cases
to his court. It is a complete alternate ruleset laid over the same plaza for a fortnight, and it
comes with its own fast court, its own booths inside a walled churchyard, and its own gates locked
and watched every night.

**4. The fountain at the plaza's centre is ruled — but should the centre object be allowed to be a
*stack*, and should it be breakable?**
The evidence keeps putting the water and the punishment in the same spot. In Cornhill there was a
well; the well was planked over; a strong timber cage with stocks inside it was built on the
planking, for night-walkers; and a pillory was set on top of the cage. When the conduit was later
enlarged they rediscovered the forgotten well underneath and turned it into a pump. Separately,
the burghers of Gloucester rang the common bell and smashed their lord's pillory and tumbrel as a
political act. So: does the centre object stay one clean thing, or can it be water-with-a-history
carrying the town's punishment furniture on its back — and can its condition (new, worn, rebuilt,
conspicuously absent) be read as the town's recent politics?

**5. Should a gate toll always carry a stated purpose and an expiry?**
Town wall taxes were granted by the king for a few years at a time, collected at the gates, and
earmarked — four pence a boatload at Shrewsbury in 1220, a ten-year grant at London for repairing
the wall and cleansing the ditch — and they were renewed over and over because the wall was never
finished. Coventry's was revoked because it was hurting trade. If the toll has a purpose and a
clock, then "the toll went up this week," "the toll still funds a wall that was finished forty
years ago," and "the toll funds a wall that was never begun" are three different hooks at three
different temperatures, all of them merely true.

**6. Should a street remember the trade that used to be there?**
Stow lists trade after trade that moved as it found advantage — the goldsmiths, the grocers, the
drapers, the hosiers who moved three times — while the brewers stayed by the water and the
butchers stayed in the shambles, because those trades were tied to something. And the
patten-makers' lane outlived the patten-makers, and one lane was still named for a market right
that nobody could remember any more. That gives a street three readable states — the trade is
here, the trade just arrived, the trade is long gone and only the name is left — and the third one
is free mystery.

**7. Should the water bearer be a named fixture rather than a rolled extra?**
By the custom of Totnes, whoever served as the town's water bearer was also required to **cry the
hour of the day** and to **carry the holy water through the whole town every Sunday**. One person,
three jobs, a fixed route, a daily rhythm and a weekly religious duty — and when a craft was
ruined by foreign competition, it was the wives and children of the ruined men who became water
carriers. He is the cheapest recurring face an urban site could have, and he is a clock, a
sacristan and a barometer of the town's fortunes at once.

**8. Should the town's new decal family be *marks that an authority puts on somebody else's
property*?**
The most legible things in this study are all of that kind: a slit in a condemned pig's ear; a red
cross a foot long painted mid-door with "Lord, have mercy upon us" above it; a door chalked
overnight because a light burned after the lawful hour; a name written up on the Disfranchised
Table in the guild hall; a painted badge on a hired lamp-carrier's straw hat; a whetstone hung
round a liar's neck to signify that he lied. They are cheap to make, they read instantly, and each
one is a whole sentence. If site 10 can only afford one new decal family, this is my candidate.

**9. Should the party wall visibly declare that it is a fire law?**
The party wall exists because towns burned. The London ordinance made two neighbours each give up
a foot and a half of ground and build a stone wall between them, three feet thick and sixteen feet
high, and required thatch to be replaced by tile on pain of forfeiting the building to the city —
and left everything else free to be timber. If the site shows that honestly, the frontage reads as
**stone spines between timber bodies, with tile above** — which is a silhouette rule, a material
rule and a piece of town history in one move. (Fair warning: these numbers are the least
well-sourced load-bearing figures in the whole study, and I have said so in the ledger.)

**10. Should the culture axis be built as different *answers to the same questions*, rather than
as different skins?**
Every tradition I looked at was solving the same four problems: sort the trades, signal the hours,
police the measures, and contain the fire and the stranger. But the answers are wildly different
and completely swappable — a bell against three hundred drum strokes; a curfew and a night watch
against a hundred and sixty internal ward walls that lock the city up in pieces; a clerk of the
market against an inspector on a raised stand who whips cheats where they stand; an innkeeper you
haggle with from horseback against a warden who writes down every traveller's name, seals the
list, and locks the door. If the axis is built as answers, one urban kit produces several genuinely
different towns. If it is built as skins, it produces one town in several palettes.

## 6. What this study could not do

Stated plainly, and repeated from the lane files so it is in one place.

- **This study never looked at a town.** No images were retrieved or examined, by the brief's own
  allowance. Every conclusion is textual. Nothing here supports a visual claim, and the deck
  ruling (rooflines, balconies, stair-streets) got nothing at all.
- **The FFT comparison was not run.** The catalogue names the trade-city cohort as part of site
  10's owed depth pass; that belongs to the sibling brief and THE FFT BOUNDARY was not approached.
- **Riley's *Memorials of London* — the keystone source for exact London ordinances — was never
  read.** Every London ordinance quoted anywhere in this study is a quotation of a quotation,
  passing through Green, Coulton or Jusserand. The same goes for Toulmin Smith's *English Guilds*
  and Hudson's Norwich records, on which the fines-as-licence-fee finding depends.
- **The two most design-load-bearing numbers are the least well sourced.** The party-wall figures
  (1189) and the baker's-hurdle wording (*Liber Albus*) both reached me through web searches
  returning consistent secondary retellings. Editions of both exist and neither was fetched.
- **Three downloaded books were never opened** — Eileen Power's *Medieval People* (which would
  have fixed the women's-work gap), Okey's *The Story of Paris* (which would have supplied the
  French night watch and the Paris cries), and Marco Polo vol. I. They are in the ledger marked
  HELD, evidencing nothing.
- **Women's work is thin, and servants are thinner.** What I have is brewing, water-carrying,
  foot-washing, plague-searching, tapstering and almshouse residency — mostly recovered as
  by-products of other topics.
- **Ordinary urban death is missing.** Burial practice, churchyard management, the passing bell,
  the gravedigger's trade, burial fees and where the poor went are all absent. I have the plague
  emergency in great detail and the normal case not at all.
- **No exact fine tariff was recovered**, which the brief explicitly asked for ("the exact fine for
  a false measure"). I have a good stock of *sums* — wages, levies, prop rentals, a cresset's night
  of light — but no schedule of penalties.
- **The watch's working establishment is unknown.** How many men, on what rota, paid what, with
  what equipment, in an ordinary borough. I have the ceremonial Midsummer watch in extraordinary
  detail and the working night watch almost not at all.
- **No mystery-play cycle was read**, so lane 6 has the economics and politics of the pageants and
  none of their content or staging.
- **Lane 7 rests on outsiders' testimony** — Polo, Bernal Díaz, Ibn Battuta at second hand — with
  no indigenous urban record consulted, and its headline finding (the souk's radial cleanliness
  gradient) is supported by a web-search synthesis rather than a citable scholarly source.
- **Jewish urban life appears in this study almost only through violence against it**, which is a
  distortion produced by my source selection. Nothing about a Genesis minority quarter should be
  built on this evidence alone.
- **Defoe is a novel.** The 1665 Orders it reproduces are genuine; the narrative around them is
  1722 fiction, and every use of it in these files says so.
