# Extract — DM lore fodder: the specifics worth quoting

**Nothing here is locked. Adam rules; this file evidences.**

**What this sheet is.** The quotable specifics — cries, fines, oaths, customs, exact phrases —
pulled out of the lanes so a DM can reach for one without reading a sourcebook. **Everything in
quotation marks is a period source's words** (or a nineteenth-century editor's transcription of
them, where flagged); everything in plain text is a fact stated plainly enough to speak aloud.

**Usage note.** These are for the DM's *mouth*, not for the engine. Per the anti-drift doctrine
the DM narrates from rolled facts; this sheet gives him the *register* those facts should be
spoken in, and a stock of true detail so he never has to invent a fine, a cry or a custom.

---

## 1. THE CRIES — say these aloud, unchanged

Fifteenth century, from *London Lickpenny*:

- "**Hot peascods!**"
- "**Strawberry ripe, and cherries in the rise!**" (*rise* = on the branch)
- "**Hot sheep's feet!**"
- "**Mackerel!**"
- "**Rushes green!**"
- "**Ribs of beef, and many a pie!**"
- "**Pepper and saffron!**"
- "**Here is Paris thread, the finest in the land.**"
- "**Master, what will you copen or buy? Fine felt hats, or spectacles to read? Lay down your
  silver, and here you may speed.**" (the Flemings at the courthouse door)
- "**Sir, will you our wine assay?**" (the taverner, taking you by the sleeve)
- And the shop's own, for centuries: "**What do ye lack, what do ye lack? What lack ye?**"

Later, if a rougher register is wanted (17th–19th c., flagged as later):

- "**Hot green peas! all hot, all hot! Here's your peas, hot, hot, hot!**"
- "**Buy my diddle dumplings, hot! hot!**"
- "**Old iron I buy.**" · "**Old chairs to mend.**" · "**Knives to grind.**"
- "**I sweep your chimnies clean, O!**"

And the two cries that are not selling anything:

- "**Bring out your dead**" — the bellman before the dead-cart.
- "**More rushes, more rushes**" — the grooms, ahead of a procession.

## 2. THE REFRAIN

*London Lickpenny*'s countryman is refused at every door in London with the same line, and it is
the best available one-phrase summary of an urban arrival with no money:

> "**But, for lack of money, I might not speed.**"

Worth having in the DM's pocket verbatim. Its companion, spoken by the man himself as he gives in
to a taverner: "**A penny can do no more than it may.**"

## 4. THE PUNISHMENTS, TOLD AS STORIES

- **John Penrose, vintner, 1364.** Sold corrupt wine. Sentenced to the pillory, "**was made to
  drink publicly there his own stuff, had what he could not drink poured over his head, and was
  besides sentenced to renounce his trade for ever.**"
- **The quack physician.** "carried to the pillory **through the middle of the city, with trumpets
  and pipes**, on a horse without a saddle, **his parchment and a whetstone round his neck**,
  unseemly pottery hanging round his neck and down his back, **in token that he had lied**."
- **The quack astrologer.** "set on a horse with **the beast's tail in his hand for a bridle**, and
  two pots which in the vulgar tongue we call *Jordans* bound round his neck, with **a whetstone in
  sign that he earned all this by his lies**; and thus he was led round the whole city."
- **The false jurors, 1509.** Rode about the city "**with their faces to the horse tails, and papers
  on their heads**," stood in the pillory, were brought back to Newgate "**where they died for very
  shame**."
- **The baker.** "**let him be drawn upon a hurdle from the Guildhall to his own house through the
  great street where there be most people assembled, and through the great streets which are most
  dirty, with the faulty loaf hanging from his neck.**" *(Verify before it becomes canon.)*
- **The fraudulent tradesman**, generally: in the pillory "with **their putrid wares burning under
  their noses**."
- **The submission ceremony, Southampton.** Sit "in the open stocks under the pillory, till the
  mayor and his brethren and the king's lieutenant walked down the street," then plead for mercy
  and confess. The mayor's own record of releasing them: "**And thereupponne [he] commaunded them
  owt of the stokkes, and hadd them to the audite hous, and bound them by obligacon to be good
  aberying ageynst the kinges grace and the mayor and his brethryn hereafter, and so delyveryd
  them.**"
- **Fordwich.** The mayor could sentence to death, and order the winning prosecutor "to carry the
  condemned criminal to the '**Thefeswell**' and himself throw him into it with hands and feet
  tied, '**knebent**' as it was called."

## 5. THE OATHS, ACCUSATIONS AND OFFICIAL PHRASES

- The jurors' brief at the View of Frankpledge, on suspicious persons: "**of such as continually
  haunt taverns, and no man knoweth whence they come; of such as sleep by day and watch by night,
  eat well and drink well, and possess** [nothing]."
- Why taverns must not keep open after curfew: "**because such offenders as aforesaid, going about
  by night, do commonly resort and have their meetings and hold their evil talk in taverns more
  than elsewhere, and there do seek for shelter, lying in wait and watching their time to do
  mischief.**"
- Against night work: "**sight is not so profitable by night, or so certain, as by day — to the
  profit, that is, of the community.**"
- Against the spurriers: "**when they have become drunk and frantic they take to their work to the
  annoyance of the sick and all their neighbourhood… and then they blow up their fires so
  vigorously that their forges begin all at once to blaze… and all the neighbours are much in
  dread of the sparks which so vigorously issue forth in all directions from the mouths of the
  chimneys in their forges.**"
- The nuisance jury on a pig-pen: "**which smells very badly and is abominable to all men coming to
  market, as well as to all dwelling in the town.**"
- The nuisance jury on a street, over and over: "**almost stinking and a nuisance**"; and on
  another: "**There was no carrying through Brokhellislane.**"
- The Norwich leet jurors: "**All the men of Sprowston sell sausages and puddings and knowingly buy
  measly pigs; and they sell in Norwich market the aforesaid sausages and pigs, unfit for human**
  [consumption]."
- Scotland, 1386, on condemned food: "**Gif ony man brings to the market corrupt swine or salmond
  to be sauld, they sall be taken by the bailie, and incontinent, without ony question, sall be
  sent to the leper folke; and, gif there be na lepper folke, they sall be destroyed all
  utterlie.**"
- Canterbury, 1490, on the lapsed watch: "**all manner of harness within the city is decayed and
  rusted for lack of the yearly watch.**"
- Norwich's mayor and sheriffs on travelling players: they "**took so large a share of the earnings
  of the poor operatives as to cause great want to their families.**"
- Bridgnorth's licence to stop paying players: whoever wants to see such things must see them
  "**upon their own costs and charges.**"
- On a disfranchised burgher: he was "**blotted out of the book of the bailiff**," and must not
  "**come among them in their congregations.**"
- The Totnes water-bearer's terms of office: "**If any person shall be a water bearer in Totnes he
  shall cry the hour of the day and shall carry the holy water every Sunday throughout the whole
  ville of Totnes.**"
- The Leadenhall petition, on the beam: "**for reason it is, that a common office, occupied upon a
  common ground, bear a charge to the use of the commonalty.**"
- The plague order on the door mark: "**That every house visited be marked with a red cross of a
  foot long in the middle of the door, evident to be seen, and with these usual printed words,
  that is to say, 'Lord, have mercy upon us,' to be set close over the same cross, there to
  continue until lawful opening of the same house.**"
- The plague order on the wands: "**the searchers, chirurgeons, keepers, and buriers are not to
  pass the streets without holding a red rod or wand of three feet in length in their hands, open
  and evident to be seen.**"
- The plague order on the rakers: "**that the raker shall give notice of his coming by the blowing
  of a horn, as hitherto hath been done.**"
- The plague order on drink: no company "to drink after **nine of the clock in the evening**,
  according to the ancient law and custom of this city."
- The Lord Mayor of London on the fire that burned his city: "**A woman might piss it out.**"

## 6. THE INN — a complete arrival, verbatim

The servant, sent ahead: he hopes "**that there are no fleas, nor bugs, nor other vermin.**"
The host: "**No, sir, please God, for I make bold that you shall be well and comfortably lodged
here — save that there is a great peck of rats and mice.**"

The next morning, two men who shared a bed: "**William, undress and wash your legs, and then dry
them with a cloth, and rub them well for love of the fleas, that they may not leap on your legs,
for there is a peck of them lying in the dust under the rushes. … Hi! the fleas bite me so! and do
me great harm, for I have scratched my shoulders till the blood flows.**"

And the traveller of rank, before dismounting, satisfying himself he will get "**good supper, good
lodging, and the rest.**"

Sleeping arrangements, from a French ambassador complaining about Germany: "**No one lies apart,
but two and two in a dark room, or oftener three and three, in the same bed as it chances.**"

The pardoner's pitch, for livestock insurance by annual subscription: give of your corn and your
oats "**to the intent that the blessed St. Anthony may keep watch over your beeves and asses and
swine and sheep; and, beside this, you use to pay… that small due which is payable once a year.**"

## 7. CUSTOMS WORTH DESCRIBING IN ONE BREATH

- **The proverb about the belled pig:** "**Such an one will follow such an one, and whine as it
  were an Anthonie pig.**" (Because the market officer slit its ear as unfit, the hospital's
  proctor hung a bell on it, nobody would dare touch it, it learned to beg — and when it got fat
  the hospital took it.)
- **The bonfires of good amity**: bonfires were so called "**as well of good amity amongst
  neighbours that being before at controversy, were there, by the labour of others, reconciled, and
  made of bitter enemies loving friends**; and also for the virtue that a great fire hath to purge
  the infection of the air."
- **Midsummer doors**: "**every man's door being shadowed with green birch, long fennel, St. John's
  wort, orpin, white lilies, and such like, garnished upon with garlands of beautiful flowers, had
  also lamps of glass, with oil burning in them all the night; some hung out branches of iron
  curiously wrought, containing hundreds of lamps alight at once.**"
- **The Chester football**: at the cross on Shrove Tuesday, in the mayor's presence, the shoemakers
  give the drapers "**a football of leather 'to play at from thence to the Common Hall'**"; the
  saddlers give a painted wooden ball on a spear-point, carried on horseback; and everyone married
  within the year pays toward it.
- **The Plymouth church-ale**: the council shuts the taverns for the day, each ward builds a booth
  in the cemetery, everyone must come and bring friends "**for the increasing of the said ale**,"
  you may bring your own victuals but must buy the bread and ale at the booth.
- **The perambulation**: an ox skull carried on two poles at the head of the procession, then
  dragged along the boundary "**as if it were a plough**"; the boundary holes dug afresh and "**a
  boy thrown into each hole and struck with a spade**"; refreshments at a named point; the skull
  raised again for the return.
- **York's Corpus Christi feast**: wine and fruits sent at public expense to the great nobles and
  ladies of the city, "**till perhaps supplies ran out and the town was 'drunken dry.'**"
- **The traders in the churchyard**: they were "**ever wont and used… to lay open, buy and sell
  divers merchandises in the said church and cemetery and special in the king's highway there.**"
- **The bishop who takes the town**: at fair time "**for the time all civic government was
  altogether suspended**"; the town's weights were carried to his Pavilion for assay; the citizens
  had to bring their bloodshed cases to him.
- **The Exeter mayor**: went to the earl's lodging chamber with the commons hammering at the door
  and **took off his livery coat and gave it back**.
- **The junior burgomaster of Nuremberg**: two burgomasters served in pairs, one old and one young,
  four weeks at a time — and "**one of the duties of the younger was to be present at the function
  of torturing prisoners.**"
- **The funduq at night**: the director "**write[s] down the names of all the travellers who will
  pass the night there, seals it and locks the door.**"
- **Chang'an's markets**: open at noon on **300 strokes of a drum**; close on **300 beats of a
  gong**, an hour and three-quarters before dusk.

## 8. TWO SCENES A DM CAN RUN FROM MEMORY

**The night chase.** "night wraps in its shadows the crooked lanes of the unlit city; the stranger
is perhaps a robber, perhaps an honest man, who has lost his way… **his fault is not to be within
doors by curfew**; he gropes his way as best he can; the watch perceives and challenges him;
fearing the result he takes to his heels, and behold! the hue and cry begins, the watch runs, **the
town wakes up, lights appear**, and one after the other the more zealous join in the chase. **If
the town is fortified, the postern gates have long been closed, and he will be surely taken.**
Scarcely can he hope to cast himself into some unshut doorway at a turning of the street, behind
which he will cower…"

Calibration for the DM: at Oxford, night-walking carried **double the fine for shooting at a
proctor with intent to injure**.

**The omen in the church, 1230.** At mass on the feast of the Conversion of St Paul, with a great
multitude present, the weather darkened, lightning struck, "**all the church seemed to be on fire,
whereupon such a stench ensued, that all men thought they should have died; thousands of men and
women ran out of the church, and being astonied, fell upon the ground void of all sense and
understanding; none of all the multitude tarried in the church save the bishop and one deacon,
which stood still before the high altar, awaiting the will of God. When the air was cleansed, the
multitude returned into the church, and the bishop ended the service.**"

## 9. HANDLE WITH CARE

- Everything drawn from **Defoe** is 1722 fiction built on records. The **Orders** are genuine
  printed civic orders; the dead-cart dialogue, the pit's dimensions and the green rug are Defoe's
  reconstruction. Both are excellent to speak aloud; only one is evidence.
- The **baker's hurdle** wording and the **Fitz-Elwyne party-wall figures** are quotation-of-
  quotation and are flagged for verification in the lanes.
- The **beating-the-bounds** description is a Victorian newspaper report of a surviving custom.
- The **gong-farmer punishment** and the **Richard the Raker** cesspit death are folklore-grade.
- The later street cries (§1, second list) are **not medieval**; keep them out of a
  strictly-medieval register.
- The **Tlatelolco** material is a conqueror's testimony about a city he helped destroy, written
  decades later. Say so if it is ever used as an in-world "history."
