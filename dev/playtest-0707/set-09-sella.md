## Turn 1
**PLAYER — Sella Voss:** Whole at last, and for the first time in five days nobody's hunting me this exact minute — so I use it, because a patient man's patience is the one thing I can borrow against. But I don't go charging upriver after a dream; a leashed woman who bolts for the town line is a woman who's just told her owner where to point. First I turn the bearing into a name. So I go quiet and careful back to the fen-wife who mended me — one old woman, not the whole network, and a woman who already knows both my faces and kept her mouth shut anyway. I take the long way, doubling my path twice and watching the slower faces for the one that turns when I turn, because if Corran's put eyes on me I'd rather feed them a boring morning than a Sunn. When I'm sure of my back I sit down with her low and warm and I ask her the old question the young ones can't answer: before the Circle, before the chains hung heavy — the Sunns who kept the Throat, the house that went into the glass. Did all of them go down? Or is there a room upriver that still remembers its own name.

**DM:** The fen-wife does not answer at once. She looks at the doorway you came through, and the one you didn't, the way a woman checks a pot for a crack before she trusts it with water — and only when she's satisfied your back is your own does she let the old thing up out of her.

"Went down," she says, testing the words like a sore tooth. "That's the Circle's grammar, girl. Houses don't go down. They get put down, slow, a name at a time, so no one morning ever looks like the murder it is." Her hands keep working out of habit, folding a strip of linen that doesn't need folding. "The Sunns kept the Throat before there was a Circle to keep anything. The well was theirs — the burning water, the whole wound of it. And a house that owns the one thing everybody needs is a house the patient ones have all the time in the world to unwrite. Haldrek. Wemric. You'll have seen those two lately, I think, by the look you came in with. They were only the last of the crossings-out. It's been a hundred years of them."

She stops folding. This is the part she has to decide to say, and you watch her decide it. "There was a girl they got out. Before the end of it — smuggled up past the Leat when she was small enough to be somebody's niece instead of somebody's heir. If she lived, she's up where the river bends and the old well-house sits half-sunk in the glass — not all the way buried, they say, which in this town is the most dangerous thing a thing can be." Her eyes come up to yours, flat and frightened and kind all at once. "They called her **Wren**. It's not her name. Her name is the thing the Circle's spent a hundred years making sure dies quiet." A beat. "And you, wearing the good coat, asking after Sunns — girl, that's not a question you ask twice. A patient man will let a lot ripen. Not that. Ask after Wren where the wrong ear hears it, and you'll find out how fast the patient ones can move when the well's what's on the table."

## Turn 3
**PLAYER — Sella Voss:** I go upriver the way I do everything now — wearing the errand honestly, because a true report is the only kind that survives Corran, and because the errand and my own hunt run the same road anyway. So I actually work it: I read the flood-scars along the Leat, mark where the old channels blew out and where the water came from, and I keep my eye peeled for the wrecker sign — a beached boat, a cache, a trampled bank, anything I can carry back down the string as the diligent thing I promised. But every step of the honest errand carries me toward the bend, and I let it, unhurried, one more Circle creature walking her owner's flank. As the light goes long I come up on where the river turns and the glass shallows out, watching for the thing the fen-wife named — a well-house half-sunk in the color, not all the way buried — and reading the ground around it before I go a foot closer, because a house that's outlived a hundred years of crossings-out didn't do it by being easy to walk up on.

**DM:** The Maw thins behind you as the chains give way to bank and reed, and the errand pays out honest coin the way you hoped: a mile up the Leat you find where the flood was born — an old sluice-gate rotted through and never mended, the channel behind it silted to uselessness, so the river had nowhere to go but over the town. That alone is worth the walk to Corran; a count-keeper likes to know his disaster had an address. And half a mile on you find the wreckers' sign — a flat-bottomed scavenger boat drawn up under a cut bank, a cache of pried fittings and waterlogged goods under oiled canvas, cold ashes, no men. A den, empty in daylight, exactly where an organized crew would keep it. You mark it all, the diligent creature's true harvest, and you carry it toward the bend.

And then the river turns, and there it is. The glass runs shallow here, gone from wound-color to a milky green like old ice, and rising up out of it — canted, one whole corner drunk-deep in the fused ground — stands a squat stone **well-house**, older than the chains, its roof-ridge still sharp against the failing light. Not all the way buried. The fen-wife's exact words, and there they are in stone. But you did not outlive a hundred years by walking up to lit windows, and you don't now — you stop in the reeds and read it, and the ground reads back. There is a thread of smoke, thin and smokeless-careful, the fire of someone who does not want a fire seen. There is a line of pale cord strung ankle-high across the only dry approach, set by a hand that expects company it won't like. And every shutter on the canted house faces east — squared to the crater, the way the drowned lie, the way the whole Maw sleeps — except one, on the near side, cracked a finger's width and dark, and angled at you. Something in that house is already awake, already facing the wrong way on purpose, and already watching the reeds you're standing in.

## Turn 4
**PLAYER — Sella Voss:** A body that's already watching me is a body I do not sneak up on — sneaking's what the last hundred years of killers did, and this house is still standing because it knows that walk. So I do the opposite. I step out of the reeds into the last of the light where the broken shutter can see me whole, and I stop dead at the pale cord, deliberate, and I look down at it and then up at the dark gap so whoever's behind it knows I saw the trap and chose not to cross it. I open both hands, empty, out from my sides. And I give the truth first, because truth is the only key that fits a lock this old — pitched low and steady, no closer. "I know whose house this is. I know they called the girl Wren, and I know that's not her name, and I know why it isn't." A breath. "I'm wearing the wrong coat. I know it. I wear it because the same patient man who's spent a hundred years crossing your family off a list keeps me on a string too — and I got tired of being on it. Haldrek. Wemric. Struck out on his own slate not a week gone. I'm not here to finish the count. I'm here because a house that's not all the way buried is the one thing in the whole Maw that scares Corran Vale — and I would very much like to scare him." I don't move. I let the cord stay uncrossed between us, and I wait to be believed or shot.

**DM (setup):** For a long moment the only answer is the river and the tick of cooling glass, and the dark gap in the shutter does not change at all — which is its own answer, because a frightened person slams a shutter and a dangerous one holds it still. Then, without the shutter moving, a sound: the small dry double-click of a crossbow's catch being thumbed off, unhurried, from somewhere the angle of the wall says is NOT behind that shutter at all — she moved while you were talking, and the broken shutter was the decoy, and you have been reciting your pretty truths at an empty window. The voice comes from your right, low and level and much closer than it should be, a young woman's voice with a hundred years of caution filed into it: "Everyone who ever came to kill my family knew all the right names too." A pause, the bolt steady on you across the reeds. "You've got the length of my patience to tell me why a Circle coat that knows my dead should walk away from this bend still breathing. And I warn you — I've heard every lie the good coats tell. My whole life is the list of them." She does not step into the light. She waits, and everything now rests on whether the woman who outlived the crossings-out reads truth in you, or one more clever mouth sent up the river to finish what the slate started.

**DM (success branch, Persuasion 18 vs DC 15 adv):** The bolt does not lower, but the silence changes weight — she is listening now, not just aiming. "A Circle string that got tired of being on it," she repeats, tasting it for the rot, and finding, to her own visible anger, none. She steps out of the dark at last: a young woman, lean and river-weathered, older in the eyes than the rest of her, the crossbow still up but no longer pointed at the middle of you. "Wren," she says, giving you the child-name like a coin she despises. "Since you already spent it." She looks at the cord you didn't cross, and at the coat you disowned, and something in her makes a hard, cold decision to gamble on you because a hundred years of hiding has bought her family exactly nothing. "You want to scare Corran Vale. Coat." A thin, humorless breath. "Come in off the cold, then, before his next-best hunter reads the same reeds you did. But you cross that threshold knowing: everyone who's ever learned where I sleep is dead. You'll be the first exception or the next entry."
