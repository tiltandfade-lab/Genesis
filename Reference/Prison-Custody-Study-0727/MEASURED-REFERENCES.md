# MEASURED REFERENCES — Site 6 cross-cultural custody architecture

The original packet's evidence spine (`Reference/Prison-Custody-Study/SOURCE-LEDGER.md`) is
built almost entirely on Anglo-American and British sources: Colonial Williamsburg, Historic
England, two U.S. HABS surveys, Eastern State Penitentiary, and the UK National Archives, with
Fremantle/Robben Island/UNESCO Australian convict material as the only non-European breadth,
all of it colonial-administered. Its own declared gap says so directly (§14): *"the measured-
plan lane is strongest for British and U.S. examples."* This lane adds four independent, dated,
citable custody traditions from outside that lane, with real measured or quantified facts
where they exist, and records honestly where a tradition has **no** architecture to measure at
all — which is itself a load-bearing finding, not an absence of evidence.

## 1. Qing-dynasty China — the yamen as a fused judicial/custodial compound

**Source class:** the yamen (衙門) was the standard unit of Chinese imperial local government
from the Tang through the Qing dynasty — an administrative office, courtroom, and residence in
one walled compound. [Wikipedia, "Yamen"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamen); the Neixiang
County Yamen Museum, Henan — "China's best preserved county-level magistrate office,"
[Henan provincial government](https://english.henan.gov.cn/2023/11-23/2853070.html) and
[People's Daily](http://en.people.cn/english/200012/12/print20001212_57543.html) — first built
1304, present structure mainly 1894/1882 (sources differ slightly on the exact rebuild year;
both agree on late-Qing), covering **over 47,000 square metres** across **an 18-courtyard
complex of more than 260 rooms**, with a dedicated jail section now preserved as a museum
exhibit.

**Spatial organization (OBSERVED, from the Yamen article):** *"a front gate, a courtyard and a
hall (typically serving as a court of law); offices, prison cells and store rooms; and
residences for the bureaucrat, his family and his staff."* The jail is not a separate building
elsewhere in the settlement — it is one wing of the same compound that holds the courtroom and
the magistrate's own home. This is a materially different organizational answer from both of
the original packet's doctrine cards, which both assume the keeper/staff quarters are attached
*to a dedicated custodial building*, not the reverse (a custodial function attached to the seat
of government itself).

**Staffing (OBSERVED):** three named classes of yamen runners divided custody-adjacent labor —
*zao* (皂, bodyguards/courtroom order), *zhuang* (壯, guards for "castle gates, the court,
prison, and warehouse"), and *kuai* (快, arrests/summonses/tax collection, most public-facing).
Pay was minimal ("enough for only one meal" per the sourced account) and runners routinely
extracted informal fees (*陋規*, "dirty regulation") from litigants and prisoners' families —
i.e., **corruption is a structural, sourced operating fact of this tradition**, not local color
invented for flavor. [Wikipedia, "Yamen"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamen).

**Restraint hardware (OBSERVED, via the preserved Neixiang jail and the William Alexander
1805 engraving):** the cangue (*jia*, 枷) — a locking wooden neck-board weighing roughly
9–90 kg depending on the sentence, worn for one month to six months — functioned as **portable,
public, worn restraint**, distinct from a cell wall. Grokipedia's summary of the device (not
independently verified here beyond corroboration by the two primary images) gives the weight
range; the two images (`LICENSE-LEDGER.md` PC-03, PC-08) independently confirm the device's
form and its use in transit under armed escort, not only static display. The Neixiang jail also
preserves a freestanding wooden slat cage (`PC-02`) as a distinct furniture-scale confinement
object separate from any cell wall.

**What this changes for Site 6:** the accepted form-family table
(`Reference/Prison-Custody-Study/synthesis.md` §7) already lists "adapted host" as a form (a
house, camp, monastery, fortress, or workhouse changed into custody). The yamen is evidence for
the **inverse relationship** — a seat of civil/judicial authority that was *built from the start*
with custody as one of its permanent, co-equal functions, never a later insertion. This is a
new, distinctly organized candidate: see `DOCTRINE-PAIR-TWO.md` card `PR-DOC-10`.

## 2. Edo-period Japan — Tenmachō/Kodenmachō rōyashiki (伝馬町牢屋敷), a measured large-capacity jail

**Source class:** the Edo (now Tokyo) city jail, in use 1613–1875, at the site now called
Jisshi Park/Jisshi Square in Nihonbashi Kodenmachō, Chūō ward.
[Tour Guide Japan](https://www.tour-guide-japan.jp/single-post/japan-s-largest-prison-the-so-called-tenma-cho-royashiki-existed-in-present-time-kodenma-cho-toky);
[Tokyo Weekender](https://www.tokyoweekender.com/art_and_culture/history/denmacho-prison-edo-period-jail/).

**Measured facts (OBSERVED):**

- **site area: approximately 8,700 m²** (one source states 8,637 m² / 2,618 *tsubo*, the other
  ~8,850 m²; both agree to within ~2%) — the modern Jisshi Park replica occupies "roughly 80% of"
  that historic footprint, itself a useful, honestly-stated fact about how much of a real large
  custodial site typically survives into the present;
- a perimeter of **"massive walls... over 2 metres high, with wide moats outside"** — a
  genuinely dual-boundary perimeter (wall *and* water), distinct from every wall-only boundary
  in the original packet's evidence;
- **capacity: "somewhere between 300 and 900 prisoners"** at one time — this is the single
  largest sourced capacity figure available to Site 6 across either packet, roughly one to two
  orders of magnitude above the Rung-B four-cell golden seed and directly useful as a real
  Rung-D "prison complex" capacity anchor;
- **staffing: "over 100 staff members"** including *rōnanushi* — cell-block leaders drawn from
  among long-term prisoners themselves, a **prisoner-as-operator relationship** not present in
  either existing doctrine card;
- **internal classification by legal/social status**, not merely by offense: the *Agari-zashiki*
  and *Agariya* sections (built 1683) held "direct vassals of the shogun, nobles, prominent
  priests and political prisoners" in semi-private rooms with baths, better food, and writing
  materials — ordinary cells had no windows and poor ventilation. This is a **measured, dated
  example of the working spec's own §"Population and relationships" classification-by-status
  principle**, independent of the Nelson Mandela Rules citation already in the original packet
  (`PR-06`).

**A genuine measured floor plan exists and is now reachable in English.** `PC-04` in this
packet's `LICENSE-LEDGER.md` is a labelled architectural plan of the same rōyashiki, sourced
from a 1929 compilation (*Kojirui Ruien*) digitized by Japan's National Diet Library — the same
institution the original packet's `PR-17` cited as "untranslated here" and unable to "carry
detailed claims." This Commons file carries an English caption and a confirmed public-domain
licence, which the original NDL page did not offer. **This directly closes the specific
untranslated-NDL-source gap the original packet declared**, though the plan's internal Japanese
labels remain untranslated in this pass — a residual, honestly declared limitation (see §6).

**One somber, historically load-bearing fact, stated without euphemism because it materially
answers a "what is the honest cost of failure" gameplay question:** across its 262-year
operation, an estimated **200,000 people died at Tenmachō** (disease, exposure, and execution
combined, per the Tokyo Weekender account). This is not offered as flavor. It is direct,
sourced evidence that the original packet's own §5 "Larger realization" and §"disposition"
circuit need to admit death-in-custody as a real, non-narrated disposition outcome at scale —
already implied by the accepted spec's "casualties" and "aftermath" runtime facts, now grounded
in a real historical rate rather than only implied by genre convention.

## 3. Custody without a dedicated building — two independently convergent traditions

This is the most consequential finding in this lane, and it does not come with a photograph,
because the thing it documents does not have a building.

### 3.1 Frankpledge / tithing, England (documented from the Anglo-Saxon period; regularized under
Cnut, 1016–1035)

**OBSERVED** (Wikipedia, "Frankpledge"; corroborated by
[LegalClarity](https://legalclarity.org/what-is-the-frankpledge-system-and-how-did-it-work/) and
a University of Texas Tarlton Law Library primary-source exhibit): free men over 12 (excepting
clergy and the wealthiest freemen) were organized into **tithings** — groups nominally of ten
households, under an elected or appointed "tithing-man" or "chief-pledge." The tithing's legal
duty was to *produce* any accused member in court. Failure to do so meant the **entire tithing**
was fined — collective liability, not individual guilt. There was no jail: *"A tithing member was
responsible for 'arresting' and holding an accused for trial, as there were no jails and no
police."* The accused remained embedded in ordinary domestic and community space under the
tithing's collective legal exposure, not behind a wall.

### 3.2 Gonin-gumi (五人組), Tokugawa Japan (formalized c. 1643–1725, continuing through the Edo
period)

**OBSERVED** (Wikipedia, "Gonin Gumi"; corroborated by
[Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/gonin-gumi) and a dedicated study,
["Under the Watchful Eye of the Neighbor"](https://ukiyo-japan.pl/en/under-the-watchful-eye-of-the-neighbor-gonin-gumi-and-collective-responsibility-in-the-time-of-the-shogunate)):
households — nominally five, in practice five to ten depending on district — were grouped under
a locally elected or appointed leader, reporting up through the village/town headman (*nanushi*/
*shōya*). Members were **"duty bound to keep watch not only over the doings of their families,
but also of their employees and tenants,"** with joint liability extending to both criminal
conduct and tax default. The mechanism the sourced study identifies as *primary* is social, not
architectural: **"the fear of losing face and becoming the subject of gossip working far more
effectively than the prospect of punishment from officials."**

### 3.3 Why this pair matters, and why it is presented as a pair rather than two isolated facts

Frankpledge and gonin-gumi arose in unconnected legal traditions, seven centuries and a
hemisphere apart, and independently converged on the same structural answer: **distribute the
custody obligation across an ordinary residential group, and make the enforcement mechanism
collective liability plus reputational exposure rather than a wall.** Two independent
attestations of the same relational pattern is a stronger evidentiary basis for a Genesis
doctrine card than either fact alone — this is exactly the standard the original packet's own
evidence-discipline section (`synthesis.md` §3, "Synthesized for Genesis") asks for: *"several
observed relationships support a generator rule."*

`DOCTRINE-PAIR-TWO.md` drafts this pattern as `PR-DOC-11 — Ward-and-Surety Custody` and pairs
it against the Yamen finding above (`PR-DOC-10`) as the campaign's requested "next doctrine
pair." **This candidate stress-tests the working spec's own REQUIRED invariant "custody
boundary. At least one controlled threshold separates a held population from free exit, with a
named operator or active replacement system"** — under this doctrine, the "threshold" is legal
and reputational, not spatial, and the "operator" is a rotating neighbor rather than a keeper or
office. Whether the existing REQUIRED wording is broad enough to license this honestly, or needs
an explicit amendment, is flagged as an open question in `synthesis.md` §6 and is **not**
resolved here.

## 4. Aztec teilpiloyan — a documented pre-Columbian wooden-cage custody, distinct construction

**Source class:** colonial-era Spanish ethnohistorical accounts of Aztec (Nahua) custody,
summarized via the Wired Humanities Nahuatl dictionary project and a Book of Mormon Central
evidence page citing the same primary chroniclers (Durán and colonial-period sources).
[Nahuatl Dictionary, "teilpiloyan"](https://nahuatl.wired-humanities.org/content/teilpiloyan);
secondary summary: [B.H. Roberts / Evidence Central](https://bhroberts.org/records/2ftKjf-0qZjDc/evidence_central_discusses_the_presence_of_prisons_in_mesoamerica).

**OBSERVED, with the sourcing caveat below:** *teilpiloyan* (also *teilpilcalli*) were **"dark
houses with small doors, within which people were kept inside wooden cages"** — access was
through a **trap door in the roof**, not a ground-level door; the sourced account states the
floor of the cage itself was "covered with small sharp blades and fragments of sharp stone."
The institution held both ordinary offenders and war captives.

**Construction implication, honestly separated from the source's punitive-severity claims:**
regardless of how the specific "sharp floor" detail is weighed as later colonial-source
elaboration versus first-hand fact (this pass did not reach a primary Nahuatl-language source
to adjudicate that), the **trap-door-in-roof access pattern is architecturally distinct** from
every access pattern in either packet's form-family table: not a barred door, not a grille, not
a portal, but a **top-entry pit/box** — closer in spatial logic to a below-grade oubliette than
to any cell-front variant currently named. This is worth recording as a licensed small-form
variant, not a doctrine card of its own (there is not enough independent corroboration here to
support a full matched card the way §3's pair does).

**Honest limitation:** no image was located and downloaded for this entry this pass (see
`LICENSE-LEDGER.md`'s closing note) — the relevant Aztec codices (Mendoza, Durán, Florentine)
were searched but a specific folio depicting the teilpiloyan itself was not confidently
identified in the time available. This is recorded as a real gap, not smoothed over: **treat
the teilpiloyan as text-sourced only until a specific codex folio is confirmed.**

## 5. Precolonial Africa — an honest negative finding, and why it is evidence

**Source class:** a research-guide/survey pass across academic secondary sources.
[Emory Libraries, "Africa — Global Prison Studies"](https://guides.libraries.emory.edu/c.php?g=1348380&p=9949174);
["The Evolution of the Prison System in Ghana, 1841–2007"](https://www.ajol.info/index.php/zahir/article/download/253364/239398);
["Settler Colonialism: The Root of Kenya's Brutal Penal System"](https://www.theelephant.info/analysis/2020/08/27/settler-colonialism-the-root-of-kenyas-brutal-penal-system/).

**OBSERVED, stated as the sources state it:** across the societies these sources survey,
"decentralized societies tended to use systems of compensation or traditional punishments such
as banishment or ostracism" rather than confinement; disputes were typically resolved by
councils of elders through restitution, and "the idea of confinement became increasingly
familiar to many on the continent, though it was not yet linked to punishment; that came with
colonialism." One source states plainly: "there is no evidence of the existence of pre-colonial
prisons in Kenya."

**Why this belongs in a measured-breadth packet even though it yields no dimensions:** the
original packet's own honesty discipline (`synthesis.md`, opening framing) already treats
"prison" as a *controlled lifecycle*, not a mandatory building. This finding is direct,
independently sourced confirmation that **confinement-as-punishment is not architecturally
universal** — several documented legal traditions answer the same custody questions (who
decides, what is owed, how is the situation closed) through **restitution and banishment
rather than a held body at all.** This reinforces, from an entirely separate continent and
sourcing tradition, the same structural conclusion §3 reaches from England and Japan: Site 6's
family must be able to express custody as a *social and economic* claim, not only a
*spatial* one. It is folded into `DOCTRINE-PAIR-TWO.md`'s `PR-DOC-11` card as corroborating
(not primary) evidence, and is explicitly **not** used to claim that any specific precolonial
African polity is the origin of that card — the card's primary evidence spine remains §3's
matched English/Japanese pair, per the existing doctrine-card law that a card answers a
correlated set of institutional questions, not an ethnic or regional label
(`Reference/Prison-Custody-Study/culture-doctrine-cards.md` §1).

**Declared limitation:** this is a survey-level pass across a small number of secondary sources
covering a small number of the continent's legal traditions (Ghana, Kenya named directly); it
is not a claim that no precolonial African society ever used spatial confinement — only that
the sources reached this pass converge on restitution/banishment as the more commonly
documented answer where confinement and colonialism's introduction of it are explicitly
discussed. A dedicated study would need direct primary-source or specialist-secondary coverage
of specific documented polities (e.g. Asante, Buganda, Benin) rather than survey guides, and
is flagged as a real, unclosed gap.
