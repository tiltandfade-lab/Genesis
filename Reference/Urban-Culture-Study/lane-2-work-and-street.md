# Lane 2 — Work and the street

**Nothing here is locked. Adam rules; this file evidences.**

---

## 2.0 The one-sentence result

A pre-industrial town sorted itself every morning into **trade territories**, and those
territories were not decorative: they were the addressing system, the reputation system, and
the enforcement system all at once — which means **the single most information-dense thing
about an urban street is which trade owns it**, and the second most is *whether that trade
has moved.*

---

## 2.1 The morning sort

FitzStephen's line is the lane's spine: **"Men of all trades, sellers of all sorts of wares,
labourers in every work, every morning are in their distinct and several places"** (in Stow,
*A Survey of London*, PG 42959, "Of Orders and Customs").

Two implications, both usable:

1. **Position is a licence.** To be selling in the wrong place is an offence in itself; the
   Leadenhall ordinance below makes this explicit ("there and no where else… upon pain of
   forfeiture of all the said wares").
2. **The street's name records the sort.** Stow's whole *Survey* is, functionally, an index
   of trade-derived toponyms: Milk street "so called of milk sold there"; Cornhill ward "so
   called of a corn market, time out of mind there holden"; Grass street "took the name of
   grass, or herbs, there sold"; Oyster gate "of oysters that were there of old time,
   commonly to be sold"; Hosier lane, Cordwainer street, Bowyers' row, Paternoster row,
   Patten-makers' lane, Ironmonger lane, Lothbury ("candlestick founders placed"), Currier's
   row, the Poultry, the shambles (Stow, PG 42959, passim).

## 2.2 THE MIGRATION FINDING — trades move, and which ones don't is the law

This is the lane's best structural result and it comes from a single passage. Stow, writing
c. 1598, sets FitzStephen's twelfth-century sort against his own day and lists the moves:

> "Men of trades and sellers of wares in this city have oftentimes since **changed their
> places, as they have found their best advantage.** For whereas mercers and haberdashers
> used to keep their shops in West Cheape, of later time they held them on London Bridge…
> The goldsmiths of Gutheron's lane and Old Exchange are now for the most part removed into
> the south side of West Cheape, the peperers and grocers of Soper's lane are now in
> Bucklesberrie… The drapers of Lombard street and of Cornehill are seated in Candlewick
> street and Watheling street; the skinners from St. Marie Pellipers, or at the Axe, into
> Budge row and Walbrooke; the stock fishmongers in Thames street; wet fishmongers in
> Knightriders street and Bridge street; the ironmongers, of Ironmongers' lane and Old Jurie,
> into Thames street; the vintners from the Vinetree into divers places. **But the brewers
> for the more part remain near to the friendly water of Thames; the butchers in Eastcheape,
> St. Nicholas shambles, and the Stockes market**… the founders remain by themselves in
> Lothberie; cooks, or pastelars, for the more part in Thames street… bowyers, from Bowyers'
> row by Ludgate into divers places, and almost worn out with the fletchers; pater noster
> makers of old time, or bead-makers, and text-writers, are gone out of Pater noster row, and
> are called stationers of Paule's churchyard; patten-makers, of St. Margaret, Pattens' lane,
> **clean worn out**; **labourers every work-day are to be found in Cheape, about Soper's lane
> end**; horse-coursers and sellers of oxen, sheep, swine, and such like, remain in their old
> market of Smithfield."
> (Stow, PG 42959.)

**Read the pattern, not the list.** Three classes fall out of it, and they are exactly the
three classes a generator would want:

| class | behaviour | examples from Stow | why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MOBILE** — follows advantage | migrates, sometimes twice | mercers, haberdashers, goldsmiths, grocers, drapers, skinners, ironmongers, hosiers (Smithfield → Cordwainer street → Birchin lane) | high-value, low-nuisance, customer-facing; chases footfall and rent |
| **ANCHORED** — bound to a resource or a nuisance | stays put for centuries | brewers ("near to the friendly water of Thames"), butchers (shambles), founders (Lothbury), cooks (Thames street), horse-coursers and livestock (Smithfield) | needs water, or *is* the nuisance, or needs space and open ground |
| **EXTINCT** — the trade dies and the street outlives it | name persists, occupants gone | patten-makers "clean worn out"; bowyers "almost worn out with the fletchers"; bead-makers become stationers | technology or fashion killed it |

**Design translation (proposal only):** a rolled urban street can carry a *trade identity*
plus a *drift state* — held / newly arrived / departed-but-named-for-it. Stow supplies the
third state ("Mart lane, so called of a privilege sometime enjoined to keep a mart there,
long since discontinued, and therefore forgotten, so as nothing remaineth for memory but the
name") — **a street named for a right nobody remembers** is a free mystery hook with a
period pedigree.

Stow also records forced concentration: "in the third of Edward IV., **all basket-makers,
wire-drawers, and other foreigners, were permitted to have shops in this manor of Blanch
Apleton, and not elsewhere, within this city or suburbs thereof**" (PG 42959). Trade
territory can be a *penalty* zone as much as a prestige one.

## 2.3 THE MARKET HALL, HOUR BY HOUR — the Leadenhall dossier

The 1503 petition of the commons of London about Leadenhall is the closest thing in the
corpus to an operating manual for a market hall, and every clause is buildable (Stow, PG
42959):

> "that all Frenchmen bringing canvass, linen cloth, and other wares to be sold, and all
> foreigners bringing wolsteds, sayes, staimus, coverings, nails, iron work, or any other
> wares, and also all manner of foreigners bringing lead to the city to be sold, **shall
> bring all such their wares aforesaid to the open market of the Leaden hall, there and no
> where else to be sold and uttered**, like as of old time it hath been used, **upon pain of
> forfeiture of all the said wares** showed or sold in any other place than aforesaid; **the
> show of the said wares to be made three days in the week, that is to say, Monday, Tuesday,
> and Wednesday**; it is also thought reasonable that **the common beam be kept from
> henceforth in the Leaden hall**, and the farmer to pay therefore reasonable rent to the
> chamber… also, that **foreigners bringing wools, felts, or any other merchandises or wares
> to Leaden hall, to be kept there for the sale and market, may pay more largely for the
> keeping of their goods than free men.**"

From that one paragraph:

- **The hall is a compulsory venue for outsiders**, not a convenience. Selling elsewhere
  forfeits the goods.
- **The show has days** — Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday — so the hall has a hot half-week and a
  cold half-week. A rolled arrival on Thursday finds a different hall than on Monday.
- **The public weighing beam lives inside the hall**, and is *farmed* (rented to a
  concessionaire who pays the city). The beam is therefore a physical object with an owner, a
  rent, and a motive to cheat.
- **Non-freemen pay a higher storage rate.** Stranger-status is priced, not merely social.
- **The hall doubles as the city granary.** Stow proves it: in 1512 "there was not found one
  hundred quarters of wheat in all the garners of the city," and when the Stratford bread
  carts arrived "there was such press about them, that one man was ready to destroy another,
  in striving to be served for their money." The mayor then bought up wheat until "the bakers,
  both of London and Stratford, were weary of taking it up," and "stored it up in Leaden hall,
  and other garners of the city."

And the single best occupancy detail in the lane: that same mayor, Roger Achley, **"kept the
market so well, that he would be at the Leaden hall by four o'clock in the summer's mornings;
and from thence he went to other markets, to the great comfort of the citizens"** (Stow, PG
42959). **The chief magistrate of the city is physically in the market hall at 4 a.m. in
summer.** That is a first-rank arrival hook (the mayor is *here*, at dawn, in person, and
whatever he is inspecting he is inspecting now).

**The porters and the beam.** The Weyhouse in Cornhill "where merchandises brought from
beyond the seas are to be weighed at the king's beam. This house hath **a master, and under
him four master porters, with porters under them: they have a strong cart, and four great
horses, to draw and carry the wares from the merchants' houses to the beam and back again**"
(Stow, PG 42959). A four-tier labour hierarchy plus a heavy vehicle, dedicated to moving
goods to a scale and back — a complete secondary occupancy set for a market site, with its
own traffic pattern.

## 2.4 The shop was a booth, and the shopkeeper shouted

Shops did not have glazed fronts; they had shutters that dropped to become counters, and the
shopkeeper's sales technique was indistinguishable from the street seller's. Hindley states
it in the course of glossing *London Lickpenny*: the Cheapside shopkeepers' premises "were
rude booths, the owners of which bawled as loudly as the itinerants; **and they went on
bawling for several centuries, like butchers in a market**" (Charles Hindley, *A History of
the Cries of London*, PG 37114).

The shop-cry itself is on record with its exact words, and the anecdote that preserves it is
a gift: in 1628 Alexander Gell, a bachelor of divinity, "was sentenced to lose his ears and
to be degraded from the ministry, for giving his opinion of Charles I, that he was fitter to
stand in a Cheapside shop with an apron before him, and say **'What do ye lack, what do ye
lack? What lack ye?'** than to govern a kingdom" (Hindley, PG 37114). *Honesty flag: the
punishment as reported is severe enough to warrant checking against the State Papers; what
the anecdote reliably preserves is the cry.*

The physical grab is recorded too. In *London Lickpenny*: "Then to the Chepe I began me
drawn, / Where much people I saw for to stand; / One offered me velvet, silk, and lawn; /
**Another he taketh me by the hand**, / 'Here is Paris thread, the finest in the land.'"
Cheapside sellers physically take hold of a passer-by. And the taverner does the same at the
end of the day: "**The taverner took me by the sleeve**, / 'Sir,' saith he, 'will you our
wine assay?'"

## 2.5 THE CRIES — the exact words

The fifteenth-century layer is the valuable one, because it is contemporaneous with the
target period. *London Lickpenny* (attributed to John Lydgate; text as printed by Hindley,
PG 37114, and Andrew White Tuer, *Old London Street Cries*, PG 61861) walks a countryman
through London and records the cries as he passes each district. Reproduced here as a route,
because that is how the poem is organised and it is exactly the structure an urban tray
wants:

**Westminster Hall (the law courts).** Clerks calling case-names: "There stood up one and
cryed about, / Richard, Robert, and John of Kent. / I wist not well what this man meant, /
He cried so thick there indeed." At the door, foreign hawkers: "Where **Flemings** began on me
for to cry: / '**Master, what will you copen or buy? / Fine felt hats, or spectacles to
read? / Lay down your silver, and here you may speed.**'"

**Westminster Gate, at noon.** "Cooks to me, they took good intent, / And proffered me
**bread, with ale and wine, / Ribs of beef, both fat and full fine**; / A fair cloth they gan
for to spread."

**Entering London.** "**Hot peascods!** one began to cry; / **Strawberry ripe, and Cherries
in the rise!** / One bade me come near and buy some spice; / **Pepper and saffron** they gan
me bede."

**Cheapside.** "One offered me **velvet, silk, and lawn**… 'Here is **Paris thread**, the
finest in the land.'"

**Candlewick (Cannon) Street.** "**Drapers** much cloth me offered anon; / Then comes in one
crying '**Hot sheep's feet**;' / One cried '**mackerel**', '**rushes green**', another gan
greet; / One bade me buy **a hood to cover my head**."

**Eastcheap.** "One cries **ribs of beef, and many a pie**; / **Pewter pots they clattered on
a heap**; / There was **harp, pipe, and minstrelsy**; / 'Yea by Cock! Nay by Cock!' some began
cry; / Some sung of Jenkin and Julian for their meed."

**Cornhill.** "Then into Cornhill anon I yode, / **Where was much stolen gear among; / I saw
where hung mine own hood / That I had lost among the throng; / To buy my own hood I thought
it wrong; / I knew it well, as I did my creed.**"

That Cornhill stanza is worth its own line in the report: **the second-hand market is where
you find the thing that was stolen from you an hour ago at the other end of town, hanging for
sale.** It is a complete, sourced, medieval quest beat.

Stow independently confirms the Cornhill character: "This lane, and the high street near
adjoining, hath been inhabited for the most part with wealthy drapers, from Birchover's lane…
down to the stocks, in the reign of Henry VI., had ye for the most part dwelling **Fripperers
or Upholders, that sold old apparel**" (PG 42959). Fripperers and upholders = the old-clothes
trade, right beside the pillory and the cage.

**Later cries** (17th–19th c., from Hindley PG 37114 and Tuer PG 61861) are *secondary* for
our period but preserve the cadence and the trade set: "Hot green peas! all hot, all hot!
Here's your peas, hot, hot, hot!"; "Buy my Diddle Dumplings, hot! hot!"; "I sweep your
Chimnies clean, O"; "Old iron I buy"; "Fine writing-ink"; "Come buy my images"; "Here's ye
houseleek"; "Buy any horehound"; "Holloway cheesecakes"; "Old chairs to mend"; "Knives to
grind." Hindley also preserves the *service model* for the hot-pea seller, which is the sort
of thing a prop census wants: the sellers "have no stands but carry them in a tin pot or pan
which is wrapped round with a thick cloth, to retain the heat. The peas are served out with a
ladle, and eaten by the customers out of basins provided with spoons by the vendor. Salt and
pepper are supplied at discretion." **A mobile food seller carries a wrapped pot, a ladle,
basins and spoons, and takes the vessels back.**

One vanished cry is a dressing law all by itself: **"rushes green."** Hindley glosses it —
"In ages long before the luxury of carpets was known in England, the floors of houses were
covered with rushes. The strewing of rushes in the way where processions were to pass is
attributed by our poets to all times and countries. Thus at the coronation of Henry V, when
the procession is coming, the grooms cry — '**More rushes, more rushes.**'" Jusserand confirms
the interior use: "Such a litter of herbs or rushes was in constant use, and was to be found
in the king's palace as well as in the houses of mere merchants in the city: **it was spread
in lieu of a carpet, to keep the room warm**" (PG 58530). And the sour side of the same fact
appears in the inn dialogue in §2.8. **Floors are strewn, not paved-and-bare; there is a trade
that sells the strewing; and processions demand fresh strewing on the street.**

## 2.6 The trades that are *services*, not shops

**The water bearer.** The best single fact in the lane, and it bundles three roles into one
municipal office. From the customs of Totnes: **"If any person shall be a water bearer in
Totnes he shall cry the hour of the day and shall carry the holy water every Sunday
throughout the whole ville of Totnes."** (Green, *Town Life in the Fifteenth Century* I, PG
50392, note 294, citing Hist. MSS. Com. iii. 344.)

Unpack it: the man who brings your water is **also the town's speaking clock** and **also the
parish's holy-water carrier**. One NPC, three functions, all diegetic, all recurring, all
route-based. If a single occupancy roster entry deserves promoting to a fixture, it is this
one.

Water-carrying is also the **destitution trade**. Green records that when a foreign company
displaced the English cordwainers — "There had once been 140 Englishmen of the cordwainers'
livery but now there were only twenty" — "the wives and children of those who had been ruined
**were turned into water-carriers and labourers**" (Green II, PG 51637, note 204). **Ruin
converts a craftsman's household into water-carriers.** That is a fall-of-fortune ladder with
one rung named.

A named water-cart man survives at Hythe in the nuisance presentments: "**the water in the
cart of Geoffrey Waterleader by which the whole community is refreshed**" was "obstructed and
spoiled by the refuse of the butchers' shambles" (Green II, PG 51637). Note the collision:
the water supply and the butchery are the same street.

**Porters** are covered in §2.3 (the beam's four-tier hierarchy). **Criers** appear as a civic
office: Green indexes "Criers in the towns" and records that forfeiture of a burgher's
freedom "was declared by open proclamation of the common crier, or by sound of the town bell,
or by having his name written up on **a Disfranchised Table in the Guild Hall**, so that all
the town should know his shame" (Green I, PG 50392). **A public board of the shamed, in the
guild hall.**

## 2.7 Masters, journeymen, apprentices — and the town over the craft

**The hierarchy** is standard and is not the interesting part. The interesting parts are:

**(a) Incorporation could be compulsory.** Canterbury in 1490 complained that the Corpus
Christi Play, the City Watch on St Thomas's Eve, and the Pageant of St Thomas had all
"fallen into decay," and would not accept poverty as an excuse — the trouble was "for lack of
good ordering of certain crafts within the same city **not corporate**." The Burghmote's
remedy: every trade "being not corporate for the nonsufficience of their craft be associate,
incorporate, and adjoining to some other craft most needing support, if they will not labour
to be corporate within themselves"; any obstinate craft that did not petition to be
incorporated by next Michaelmas was to pay 20s. **"and give up their bodies for punishment."**
The resulting mergers are recorded: "The shoemakers were accordingly joined in one guild with
the leather-sellers and pouchmakers, **the apothecaries with the grocers and chandlers**"
(Green II, PG 51637). **A town can force two trades into one guild.** That is a faction-merge
mechanic with a documented precedent.

**(b) Guild membership carried a *performance obligation*.** "when the tailors of Plymouth
were incorporated in 1496, they had to bind themselves to provide a pageant every year on
Corpus Christi Day"; "The York hostellers contracted in 1483 to bring forth yearly for the
next eight years a pageant of their own, *The Coronation of Our Lady*" (Green I, PG 50392 and
note 282). **A guild owes the town a play, by contract, with a term of years.**

**(c) Craft rules were argued in the language of public nuisance, not of monopoly.** The
anti-night-work case is put as consumer protection — "sight is not so profitable by night, or
so certain, as by day — *to the profit, that is, of the community*" — and the spurriers'
ordinance is pure neighbourhood complaint (drunk smiths, blazing forges, sparks from the
chimney mouths, "the annoyance of the sick and all their neighbourhood") (Green II, PG
51637). **Trade regulation is written as anti-social-behaviour law.**

**(d) The whole apparatus could be abolished.** Nuremberg is the counter-case and it is
severe. After the 1348 rising, the restored Council of 1349 consisted of "eight artisans and
thirty-four patricians. The artisans, however, only attended on special occasions. **They had
no real power. The Guilds were suppressed**, and even such Unions as had existed before the
Revolution were put under the control of the Council, who kept in their own hands the
decision of even the smallest matters, which were wont to be decided in other towns by the
Guilds" (Cecil Headlam, *The Story of Nuremberg*, PG 46401, note 7). Even the Meistersinger —
a singing fraternity of weavers and shoemakers — "had to get special leave from the Council
each year to maintain their singing schools. **This leave was sometimes refused, on the ground
that the Masters sang lascivious songs, and bawled them rather than sang**" (Headlam, PG
46401).

**Design translation (proposal only):** guild strength is an axis, not a constant.
*Guild-run town* (Flanders: deans of the weavers and butchers lead risings) ↔ *council-run
town with guilds tolerated* (English boroughs) ↔ *council-run town with guilds abolished*
(Nuremberg). All three are attested, and each produces a different set of NPCs holding the
market hall.

## 2.8 Work below, live above — and what the corpus actually supports

The brief names "the shop-house pattern (work below, live above)" as a target. **My corpus
supports it only obliquely, and I will not overclaim it.** What the sources *do* give:

- Stow repeatedly describes street frontages as "**a fair front of tenements towards the
  street**" attached to a larger house or hall behind (e.g. the Weyhouse: Sir Thomas Lovell
  "built this house, with a fair front of tenements towards the street"). **The frontage as a
  rentable skin over a bigger institutional body behind it** is well attested and is exactly
  site 10's declared frontage grammar.
- Stow describes London Bridge as "replenished on both the sides with large, fair, and
  beautiful buildings, inhabitants for the most part rich merchants… mercers, and
  haberdashers" (PG 42959) — a built-over bridge as a shopping street with residents.
- The over-street obstruction hazard is explicit — "projecting staircases and penthooses"
  making the night street dangerous (Coulton, PG 37277) — and jettied upper floors "narrowing
  the streets" are recorded as a standing fire and passage hazard, with royal proclamations
  against overhanging jetties in 1661 and 1665 that were ineffective (Wikipedia, *Great Fire
  of London*).
- Working *from* the frontage into the street is documented in the negative, by the nuisance
  presentments: at Hythe, "the butcher and swine-keeper as well, threw the waste of house and
  shambles and swine-cote into the open street **till there was scarcely any passage left for
  the wayfarer**"; a skinner's pits occupied "the 'Cherche Weye'"; "There was no carrying
  through Brokhellislane" (Green II, PG 51637). **A trade's process spills into the street
  until the street is unusable — that is the real relationship between shop and street.**

What I could **not** evidence from these texts: the internal section (shop–hall–solar), stair
positions, or the standard bay width of a shop-house. Flagged in §2.10.

## 2.9 THE FIVE-TO-TEN MOST GAME-RELEVANT FINDINGS

1. **The three trade classes — MOBILE / ANCHORED / EXTINCT.** Stow's migration passage yields
   a rule with no invention required: high-value customer-facing trades chase advantage and
   move; water-bound and nuisance trades (brewers, butchers, founders, cooks, livestock) stay
   put for centuries; dead trades leave the street name behind. Streets can carry
   trade-identity + drift-state, and "named for a right nobody remembers" (Mart lane) is a
   free hook.
2. **THE MARKET HALL HAS DAYS AND A COMPULSORY CATCHMENT.** Leadenhall 1503: outsiders must
   sell *there and nowhere else* on pain of forfeiture; the show runs Monday–Wednesday only;
   the common beam lives in the hall and is rented out; non-freemen pay more for storage. Four
   rollable knobs on one fixture.
3. **The mayor is in the market hall at four o'clock on a summer morning.** Roger Achley,
   1512. The highest civic authority personally present at dawn is a first-rank arrival hook
   and a natural "why is everyone tense today" answer.
4. **The water bearer cries the hour and carries the holy water.** One NPC, three functions,
   a fixed route, and a weekly religious duty. The strongest candidate in the study for
   promotion from roster entry to standing fixture.
5. **Ruin turns a craftsman's family into water-carriers.** A documented downward rung
   (cordwainers displaced → wives and children become water-carriers and labourers). Fortune
   in an urban site can fall along a named path.
6. **The second-hand market sells you your own stolen hood.** Cornhill, *London Lickpenny*,
   corroborated by Stow's fripperers and upholders on the same street — and the pillory and
   the cage stood there too.
7. **The porters' four-tier hierarchy plus the beam-cart.** Master → four master porters →
   porters, with a strong cart and four great horses shuttling goods to the scale and back. A
   whole secondary traffic pattern for a market tray.
8. **A town can force two crafts to merge, on pain of a 20s. fine and "give up their bodies
   for punishment."** Canterbury 1490. Faction merge with a documented trigger (failure to
   fund the pageant and the watch).
9. **A guild can owe the town a play by contract** — York hostellers, eight years, *The
   Coronation of Our Lady*. Obligations, not just privileges.
10. **The nuisance presentments are the honest description of a working street:** waste from
    house, shambles and swine-cote thrown out until "there was scarcely any passage left for
    the wayfarer."

## 2.10 DECLARED GAPS

- **The shop-house section is not evidenced here.** Frontage-as-rentable-skin is well
  supported; shop-below/solar-above is not directly attested in the texts I read. It is a
  commonplace of the secondary literature and is very likely true, but **this lane has not
  proved it** and no dimension of any kind (bay width, storey height, stair position) was
  recovered.
- **No guild ordinance was read in full.** Toulmin Smith's *English Guilds* is cited
  repeatedly by Green and I never obtained the text; the Gutenberg catalogue search for it
  returned nothing. Everything said here about craft rules is at second hand through Green.
- **Apprenticeship terms, numbers and indenture wording are absent.** No seven-year term, no
  fee, no ceremony was recovered from these sources. This is a real hole for an occupancy
  roster that wants apprentices in it.
- **Street-cry evidence is chronologically lopsided.** One rich fifteenth-century source
  (*London Lickpenny*) and then a large eighteenth/nineteenth-century corpus. The
  sixteenth–seventeenth-century middle is thin, and none of the later cries should be treated
  as medieval.
- **Continental cries were not gathered at all.** The Paris *cris* tradition (Guillaume de la
  Villeneuve's *Les Crieries de Paris*, thirteenth century) is exactly the parallel document
  to *Lickpenny* and I did not obtain it. That is the highest-value single gap in this lane.
- **Nothing on market stall construction.** Booths, standings, trestles, awnings, canvas —
  named repeatedly (Stow's Bartholomew booths, Bruges' "stalls, with white canvas awnings")
  but never described structurally. The dressing census will need a source that isn't here.
- **The Leadenhall dossier is a single-market sample.** Whether Monday–Wednesday showing,
  compulsory venue, and in-hall beam are typical or peculiar to London is untested.
