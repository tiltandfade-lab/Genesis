# Lane 4 — The social fabric

**Nothing here is locked. Adam rules; this file evidences.**

---

## 4.0 The one-sentence result

The town's social order was expressed as **a set of admissions** — to the gate, to a bed, to
the freedom of the borough, to a guild, to a quarter, to a burial — and **arrival is the
moment when every one of those admissions is decided at once**, which is precisely why the
arrival hook law is historically correct rather than merely convenient.

---

## 4.1 Class geography — centre, wall, and the ground outside the wall

The corpus supports a three-zone reading, with a fourth zone (the liberties) cutting across
all of them.

**(a) The good street.** Stow's markers of wealth are consistent: "divers fair houses for
merchants"; "large, fair, and beautiful buildings, inhabitants for the most part rich
merchants, and other wealthy citizens, mercers, and haberdashers" (of **London Bridge**, of
all places — the built-over bridge is a *prestige* address); "this lane, and the high street
near adjoining, hath been inhabited for the most part with **wealthy drapers**" (Birchin lane
/ Cornhill). Great lords keep town houses too: FitzStephen — "Most part of the bishops, abbots,
and great lords of the land have houses there, whereunto they resort, and bestow much when they
are called to parliament by the king" (all Stow, *A Survey of London*, PG 42959).

**(b) The wall's inner face and the ditch.** Here the record is of encroachment. The city
ditch, "being originally made for the defence of the city, was also long together carefully
cleansed and maintained, as need required; but now of late **neglected and forced either to a
very narrow, and the same a filthy channel, or altogether stopped up for gardens planted, and
houses built thereon; even to the very wall, and in many places upon both ditch and wall houses
to be built**" (Stow, PG 42959). **People build *on* the wall and *in* the ditch.** That is the
"who lives against the wall" answer, and it is squalid, informal, and militarily alarming —
Stow says so: "to what danger of the city, I leave to wiser consideration."

**(c) The suburb and the shanty ground.** Two Stow passages carry it:

> "this common field, I say, being sometime the beauty of this city on that part, **is so
> encroached upon by building of filthy cottages, and with other purpressors, inclosures, and
> laystalls** (notwithstanding all proclamations and acts of parliament made to the contrary),
> that in some places it scarce remaineth a sufficient highway for the meeting of carriages and
> droves of cattle."

> \[St Katharine's by the Tower] "being now of late years **inclosed about, or pestered with
> small tenements and homely cottages, having inhabitants, English and strangers, more in
> number than in some city in England**."
> (Both Stow, PG 42959.)

**"Pestered with small tenements and homely cottages," population larger than a whole city,
English and strangers mixed, on ground belonging to a religious liberty** — that is the poor
quarter, evidenced, and it is *outside* the wall and *inside* somebody's franchise. Stow adds
the riverside version east of the Tower: where the pirates' gallows had stood, "since the
gallows being after removed farther off, **a continual street, or filthy strait passage, with
alleys of small tenements, or cottages, built, inhabited by sailors' victuallers**, along by
the river of Thames, almost to Radcliff, a good mile from the Tower." **The shanty street grows
along the road where the gallows used to be, and it is occupied by the trade that serves
sailors.**

**(d) The liberties cut across everything.** See lane 3 §3.1 (Godbeate at Winchester, opposite
the guild hall). The point for social geography is that **a sanctuary or franchise is a
jurisdictional hole in the middle of the map** which attracts exactly the population that wants
one.

Bruges gives the almshouse texture: after the trade collapsed, "so many of the people were
reduced to living on charity that almshouses sprang up all over the town. **God's Houses
('Godshuisen') they called them**, and call them still. They are to be found in all directions —
quaint little places, planted down here and there, **each with a small chapel of its own, with
moss-grown roofs and dingy walls, and doors that open on to the uneven cobbles. Every stone of
them spells pauperism.**" With the number, as observed c. 1905: "there are 10,000 paupers in
Bruges out of a population of about 55,000" (Omond, *Bruges and West Flanders*, PG 18670).
**Charity housing is scattered in small pockets throughout the town, not concentrated** — each
unit tiny, each with its own chapel. That is a different urban form from the shanty street and
should not be conflated with it.

Stow's English equivalents are specific and small: alms houses "**for fourteen sole women**"
built on Tower Hill; a plot "with certain small cottages" converted to them (PG 42959).
**Almshouse foundations are counted in individuals, and often gendered.**

## 4.2 HOW ARRIVAL ACTUALLY WORKED

This is the lane's centre of gravity for site 10, so it is assembled step by step, each step
sourced.

**Step 1 — the gate, and the toll.** Murage/pavage/pontage collected on goods entering for
sale; rates by load type (lane 3 §3.6). Great gate closed at sunset, wicket until curfew, laxly
enforced (lane 1 §1.2).

**Step 2 — the stranger's legal disabilities.** English petitions asked "that the former laws
may be put in force, ordering **strangers only to dwell in the houses of Englishmen, to sell
only in gross and not by retail, and to remain only a month in any town after their first
coming**" (Green, *Town Life in the Fifteenth Century* II, PG 51637, note 205). Three clauses:
compulsory lodging with a citizen · wholesale only · a one-month clock.

**Step 3 — the compulsory venue.** Foreigners' goods must go to the designated hall and
nowhere else, on pain of forfeiture, and they pay a higher storage rate than freemen
(Leadenhall, 1503; lane 2 §2.3).

**Step 4 — a bed.** Options, in ascending order of respectability, all attested by Jusserand
(*English Wayfaring Life in the Middle Ages*, PG 58530):

- **The monastery guest-house** — "Only people of high rank were admitted into the monastery
  proper. The mass of travellers, pilgrims and others, were housed and fed in **the guest-house,
  a building made on purpose to receive passers-by; it usually stood by itself, and was even,
  sometimes, erected outside the precincts of the monastery**," e.g. Battle Abbey, where "the
  guest-house is still to be seen outside the large entrance gate." Its plan: "a hall with doors
  opening on each side into sleeping rooms. **People slept also in the hall**," and inventories
  (Dover's Maison-Dieu) show "beds were set up in the hall and remained, it seems, permanently
  there."
- **A great house's hospitality** — the guest at the high table, "his followers were at the
  lower tables disposed along the side walls"; after supper the traveller might take a guest
  room, or "sometimes into that of the master himself, **the *solar*** (room on the first
  storey), and spent the night there with him." Meanwhile in the hall "the lower tables were
  taken out, for in general these were not standing, but movable; **mattresses were placed on
  the ground over the litter of rushes** which day and night covered the pavement, and the
  people of the household, the suite of the traveller, the strangers of less importance,
  stretched themselves out there till morning."
- **The common inn**, which Jusserand places socially with precision: "It must have been chiefly
  to the very poor, or the very rich or powerful that the monastery served as a hostelry… **the
  common inns being at once too dear for the one and too miserable for the other. They were
  intended for the middle class: merchants, small landowners, itinerant packmen**." Sleeping
  arrangements: "A certain number of beds were placed in one room, and **a certain number of men
  in each bed, usually two, but sometimes three**," Eustache Deschamps complaining of Germany:
  "**No one lies apart, but two and two in a dark room, or oftener three and three, in the same
  bed as it chances.**"
- **The charitable lodging-house** — "the gild-merchant of Coventry, founded in 1340, maintained
  **'a common lodging-house of thirteen beds,'** to receive poor travellers who cross the country
  going on pilgrimage or from any other pious motive. This hostelry was managed by a governor,
  **aided by a woman who washed the feet of the guests and took care of them.** The annual
  expenditure on this foundation was **ten pounds sterling**." Thirteen beds, a governor, a
  foot-washing woman, £10 a year. A complete, costed institution.
- **The alehouse on the road** — "At the cross-roads, in the more frequented parts of the
  country, alehouses, **with a long projecting pole above the door and a bush at the end of it**,
  invited the traveller to have a rest and a drink" — Chaucer's "ale-stake."

**Step 5 — the arrival conversation.** The single best DM-lore artefact in the study is the
inn dialogue from a fourteenth-century French conversation manual for Englishmen, *La Manière
de Language*, which Jusserand quotes (PG 58530). The servant is sent ahead to engage the room
and hopes "**'that there are no fleas, nor bugs, nor other vermin.' 'No, sir, please God,'
replies the host, 'for I make bold that you shall be well and comfortably lodged here — save
that there is a great peck of rats and mice.'**"

And, from a later chapter, two travellers who have just shared a bed: "**'William, undress and
wash your legs, and then dry them with a cloth, and rub them well for love of the fleas, that
they may not leap on your legs, for there is a peck of them lying in the dust under the rushes.
… Hi! the fleas bite me so! and do me great harm, for I have scratched my shoulders till the
blood flows.'**"

Jusserand notes the traveller "assures himself before dismounting that he will find at the inn
'good supper, good lodging, and the rest'" — **the negotiation happens from horseback, before
committing.**

**Step 6 — being fleeced.** "Complaints as to excessive prices were not less frequent than
now. The innkeeper's extortions were supplemented by those of his assistants," Chaucer's parson
branding "thilke that holden hostelries" who "sustenen the theft of hir hostilers (ostlers)."
Edward III legislated twice — a statute "to constrain 'hostelers et herbergers' to sell food at
reasonable prices," and four years later against "the great and outrageous cost of victuals kept
up in all the realm by inn-keepers" (Jusserand, PG 58530). **Two royal statutes in four years is
the period's own admission that the problem was unfixable.**

**Step 7 — being robbed in your sleep.** Langland's confessed thief: "'Thus, ones I was
herberwed,' quod he · 'with an hep of chapmen, I roos whan thei were arest (having their rest) ·
and yrifled here males'" (their trunks) — and, asked whether that counted as restitution, "'I
wende (believed) ryflynge were restitucioun,' quod he" (Jusserand, PG 58530). **Chapmen sleeping
in a group; one of the group rises early and goes through the packs.**

## 4.3 The tavern as the rumour engine — in the authorities' own words

The evidence for the tavern's information role is, pleasingly, *the law's complaint about it*.
The royal prohibition on keeping open house after curfew states its reason: "**because such
offenders as aforesaid, going about by night, do commonly resort and have their meetings and
hold their evil talk in taverns more than elsewhere, and there do seek for shelter, lying in
wait and watching their time to do mischief**" (in Jusserand, PG 58530).

The Frankpledge jurors' brief supplies the suspect profile — "of such as continually haunt
taverns, and no man knoweth whence they come; **of such as sleep by day and watch by night, eat
well and drink well, and possess** [nothing]" (Jusserand, PG 58530). **"No man knoweth whence
they come" is the tavern's defining social property**, and the reason information travels there
and nowhere else.

What the tavern sounds like, from *London Lickpenny*'s Eastcheap: "**Pewter pots they clattered
on a heap; There was harp, pipe, and minstrelsy; 'Yea by Cock! Nay by Cock!' some began cry;
Some sung of Jenkin and Julian**" (Hindley, PG 37114). Clattering pewter, live music, shouted
oaths, and popular songs about named characters.

Who supplies the entertainment, and when: "**Musicians of the meanest order would entertain the
sitters at the table with their pipings, and then pass the hat**"; and, at the better sort of
inn, "come forward into the lord's presence the trumpeters and horn-blowers with their frestels
(pipes) and clarions, and begin to play and blow very loud, and then the lord with his squires
begin to move, to sway, to dance, to utter and sing fine carols **till midnight without
ceasing**" (Jusserand, PG 58530). Cross-reference lane 1: **minstrels are resident in town from
All Saints to Candlemas and travelling the rest of the year.**

The moral reputation is uniform. The Lollard William Thorpe in 1407 declared that pilgrims
frequent ill-famed hostelries, "**spending their goods upon vitious hostelars, which are oft
uncleane women of their bodies**"; and the continuator of Chaucer sets a scene at "the '**Cheker
of the Hope**' (hoop) in Canterbury," where the pardoner's advances to **Kit the tapster** "had
the edifying result of getting for him many more blows than caresses" (Jusserand, PG 58530). A
named inn and a named tapster who hits back.

**The wandering population that fills these rooms** is Jusserand's whole subject, and his own
summary list is the best NPC table in the corpus: "**minstrels, buffoons, quacks, messengers,
pedlars, pilgrims, wandering preachers, beggars, friars, vagabonds of all sorts, labourers
broken loose from the soil, pardoners, knights in search of adventure**," plus "scribes, tinkers,
cobblers, masons, showers of animals or bearwards."

**The pardoner** gets his own portrait, and it is the best "dangerous charming stranger" model
available: "after a busy day they must have been cheerful companions at the inn. **The thought
of the multitude of sins they had pardoned, of excommunications they had removed, of penalties
they had remitted — themselves mere vagabonds threatened with the jail or gallows — the
knowledge of their impunity**… must have made their hearts swell with uncontrollable
merriment." Their sales patter is quoted from a period text — the St Anthony's collector
promising that "the blessed St. Anthony may keep watch over your beeves and asses and swine and
sheep" in return for corn and oats — and Jusserand's verdict is a keeper: "**They released their
customers from all possible vows, and remitted any penance, for money; they were a living
encouragement to sin, making it so easy to atone for. The more prohibitions, obstacles, or
penances were imposed, the more their affairs prospered.**"

**The messenger** is the fastest thing on the road and legally privileged: "he had **the right
of way; woe to whoever thought to stop him**; there were immense fines if the master were
powerful, still more if the man were the king's messenger. A messenger of the queen, who had
been imprisoned by the constable of Roxburgh Castle, did not hesitate to claim **£10,000
sterling for contempt of his sovereign, and £2,000 as indemnity for himself**" (Jusserand, PG
58530). And a benchmark for information speed: news of the papal election of 7 August 1316
reached Edward II at York **ten days later**, carried by "Laurence of Ireland, messenger of the
house of the Bardi" — i.e. **by a banking house's courier, not a royal one.**

## 4.4 Outsider and minority quarters

**The named foreign colonies** in Stow's London are trade-specific and street-specific:

- **Lombard Street**, "so called of the Longobards, and other merchants, strangers of divers
  nations **assembling there twice every day**" — a standing, twice-daily merchants' exchange in
  the open street, continuing "until the 22nd of December, in the year 1568" when it moved to
  the Royal Exchange.
- **Mincing Lane**: "In this lane of old time dwelt divers strangers born of **Genoa** and those
  parts; these were commonly called **galley men**, as men that came up in the galleys brought
  up wines and other merchandises, which they landed in Thames street, at a place called
  **Galley key**; they had **a certain coin of silver amongst themselves, which were halfpence
  of Genoa, and were called Galley halfpence**" (Stow, PG 42959). **A resident foreign community
  with its own trade, its own wharf, and its own circulating coin.**
- **Blanch Appleton**: basket-makers, wire-drawers "and other foreigners" permitted shops "**in
  this manor… and not elsewhere**" (lane 2 §2.2).
- **Flemings** hawking felt hats and reading-spectacles at the door of Westminster Hall (*London
  Lickpenny*).

**The Jewry** is documented in the corpus mainly through its destruction, which is itself the
finding. In 1215 the barons "brake into the Jews' houses, searched their coffers to fill their
own purses, **and after with great diligence repaired the walls and gates of the city with stone
taken from the Jews' broken houses**." And in 1586, when Ludgate was taken down for rebuilding,
"there was found couched within the wall thereof a stone taken from one of the Jews' houses,
wherein was graven in Hebrew characters these words following: *Hæc est statio Rabbi Mosis, filii
insignis Rabbi Isaac* … this is the station or ward of Rabbi Moyses, the son of the honourable
Rabbi Isaac, **and had been fixed upon the front of one of the Jews' houses, as a note or sign
that such a one dwelt there**" (Stow, PG 42959).

**Read that twice.** A doorplate naming the householder, torn from a wrecked house, built face-in
to a city gate, and found there 371 years later when the gate came down. That is a
meaning-bearing decal, a pogrom, an archaeology, and a hook, in one object. Stow records the
1241 Norwich hangings on a blood-libel charge and the 20,000-mark levy on London's Jews under
threat of perpetual imprisonment in the same passages.

**The mature, formalised version** of the segregated quarter is the **Venetian Ghetto**, and its
mechanics are exact (Wikipedia, *Venetian Ghetto*): the Senate decree of **29 March 1516**
required all Jews in Venice to move into the Ghetto Nuovo, an area named for the **foundries**
that had worked there; residents were to "pay a rent which will be higher by one third" than
standard; **two bridges** connected it to the city and "opened only during daylight"; the gates
opened in the morning "**at the ringing of the *marangona*, the largest bell in St. Mark's
Campanile**," and locked in the evening; "**permanent, round-the-clock surveillance of the gates
occurred at the Jewish residents' expense**"; and "areas of Ghetto Nuovo that were open to the
canal were to be sealed off with walls, while outward facing quays were to be bricked over."
Jews were "legally forbidden from owning real estate" but held usage rights by *jus gazaka*, a
permanent lease.

**The generalisable mechanics:** a quarter with (1) a bell that governs its gates, (2) a rent
premium, (3) guards paid for by the guarded, (4) a waterfront deliberately bricked up, and
(5) tenure without ownership. Every one of those is a rollable urban-district property, and
none of them requires naming a real people.

## 4.5 Women's work

Thin in this corpus, but what is here is specific:

- **Brewing is women's work and is systematically fined.** "Almost every housewife of the leading
  families brewed ale and sold it to her neighbours, and invariably charged more than the fixed
  price… **these ladies were regularly presented and amerced every year for the same offence,
  paid their amercements and went away**" (Hudson, in Coulton, PG 37277). Brewing as a
  household side-trade of the *leading* families, not the poor.
- **Water-carrying is where ruined households land** — the displaced cordwainers' "wives and
  children… were turned into water-carriers and labourers" (Green II, PG 51637; lane 2 §2.6).
- **Hospitality care work**: the Coventry lodging-house's "woman who washed the feet of the
  guests and took care of them."
- **Plague searchers were required to be women**, sworn, "of honest reputation, and of the best
  sort as can be got in this kind," barred from any other employment during the visitation, and
  paid alongside chirurgeons who received "twelve-pence a body searched" (Orders of the Lord
  Mayor, 1665, as reproduced in Defoe, PG 376 — see lane 5). **A sworn, exclusive, female civic
  office with a body-count fee attached to its male colleagues.**
- **Almshouses founded specifically for women** — "fair alms houses for fourteen sole women"
  (Stow, PG 42959).
- **Tapsters** (Kit at the Cheker of the Hope) and inn women generally, treated in the sources as
  simultaneously essential and defamed (§4.3).
- **Church seating expressed the order**: at Liverpool, "according to ancient custom," the city
  officers **and their wives** had special seats in St Nicholas, "and after them the householders;
  **apprentices and servants shall sit or stand in the alleys**" (Green I, PG 50392, note 287).
  **The parish church is a seating chart of the town's hierarchy, wives included, servants
  standing in the aisles.** Totnes records survive as literal seat assignments: "Alice wife of
  Walter Cochela sits above the seats of Walter rustic."

## 4.6 Sanctuary — the social escape hatch, with a geometry

Sanctuary matters here because it is the one place in a town where **the social order can be
formally suspended for an individual**, and it has a *measured* structure:

> "one mile or more round it, the space being divided into several circles, usually marked by
> stone crosses, and **it was more and more sinful to remove fugitives violently from the
> sanctuary the nearer they were to the inner circle.** If they were dragged from the altar or
> the **fridstool**, no money atonement was accepted from the abductor" — Prior Richard of
> Hexham, writing 1154×1167: "**If any one, moved by a spirit of madness, ventured with
> diabolical boldness to seize one in the stone chair near the altar which the English call
> *fridstol*, that is a chair of quiet or peace… no compensation will be determined for such a
> glaring sacrilege, no amount of money will serve as an atonement, for it is what the English
> call *botolos* (bootless)**."
> (Jusserand, PG 58530.)

**A graded protection field, marked on the ground by stone crosses, with an innermost seat where
violation is literally beyond compensation.** That is a mechanic with a diagram already in it.

The corruption of the system is recorded in the same breath: charters of pardon "were thus given
to the innocent for money, and **to 'common felons and murderers' also, which had two results:
the number of brigands increased by reason of their impunity, and men dared not bring the most
formidable criminals to justice for fear of seeing them return pardoned and ready to wreak a
terrible revenge**." Great men bought pardons for their retainers because "in league with their
retainers, they wanted to defend them from justice as they themselves were defended by them in
the street or on the road" (Jusserand, PG 58530). **A pardon economy, with retainers as the
currency.**

## 4.7 THE FIVE-TO-TEN MOST GAME-RELEVANT FINDINGS

1. **ARRIVAL IS A STACK OF ADMISSIONS, and every step is documented.** Gate + earmarked toll →
   stranger's disabilities (lodge with a citizen, wholesale only, one month) → compulsory venue
   for goods → a bed at one of five distinct tiers → a haggle conducted from horseback → the
   near-certainty of being overcharged or robbed. That whole stack is a ready-made arrival hook
   generator, and every tier changes who the PC meets.
2. **Five distinct lodging tiers with distinct social meanings:** monastery guest-house (very
   poor + very great) · great-house hall floor (retinue on mattresses over the rushes) ·
   common inn (the middle: merchants, packmen, two or three to a bed) · charitable
   lodging-house (Coventry: thirteen beds, a governor, a foot-washing woman, £10 a year) ·
   roadside alehouse under a pole-and-bush.
3. **The inn dialogue, verbatim.** "No fleas, nor bugs, nor other vermin?" — "No, sir, please
   God… **save that there is a great peck of rats and mice.**" This is the single most quotable
   artefact in the study and it needs no adaptation at all.
4. **The tavern's information role is defined by the law that hates it:** "evil talk in taverns
   more than elsewhere," and the suspect profile "**no man knoweth whence they come… sleep by
   day and watch by night, eat well and drink well, and possess nothing.**"
5. **The poor quarter is *outside the wall and inside somebody's franchise*, and it is called
   "pestered with small tenements and homely cottages."** Its population can exceed a whole
   city's. Its trade is servicing whoever passes (sailors' victuallers). It grows along the road
   where the gallows used to be.
6. **Charity housing takes a different form from the shanty:** scattered small Godshuisen, each
   with its own chapel; English almshouses counted in individuals ("fourteen sole women"). Two
   different poverty morphologies in the same town.
7. **THE DOORPLATE IN THE GATE.** A Hebrew nameplate from a wrecked house, built into Ludgate,
   found 371 years later. A single prop that carries a pogrom, a rebuilding, and a rediscovery.
8. **The Ghetto's five mechanics are transferable without naming a real people:** a bell that
   governs the quarter's gates · a rent premium · guards paid for by the guarded · a waterfront
   deliberately bricked up · tenure without ownership.
9. **Sanctuary is a graded field marked by stone crosses, with a *fridstool* at the centre where
   violation is beyond any payment** — and it sits next to a functioning market for pardons.
10. **The parish church is a seating chart** of the whole social order — officers and their
    wives, then householders, "apprentices and servants shall sit or stand in the alleys."

## 4.8 DECLARED GAPS

- **Women's work is badly under-evidenced here**, and I want that stated plainly rather than
  padded. What I have is brewing, water-carrying, foot-washing, plague-searching, tapstering and
  almshouse residency — a handful of roles, mostly recovered as by-products of other topics.
  Eileen Power's *Medieval People* (PG 13144) was downloaded and **not mined** for its Ménagier
  de Paris chapter, which is the obvious remedy and would supply an entire urban household's
  female management in detail. That is the biggest single unforced gap in the study.
- **Servants** appear only in the church-seating rule and in passing. No indenture, no wage
  scale for urban domestic service, no household size.
- **No urban household inventory or rent figure was recovered**, so "who lives at the centre and
  who against the wall" is qualitative, not quantitative.
- **Jewish urban life is evidenced almost entirely through violence against it.** That is a real
  distortion of the historical record produced by my source selection (Stow is a chronicler of
  London's fabric, not of its Jewish community, and the community was expelled from England in
  1290). The Venetian Ghetto material partly compensates but is a century-plus later and a
  different polity. **Do not build a Genesis minority-quarter on this evidence alone.**
- **The brief's "hospitality codes" are only half-answered.** I have the mechanics of lodging
  well, and the *obligations* of a host badly: the duty to report guests, the host's liability
  for a guest's conduct, the guest-gift, and the formal welcome are all missing.
- **Nothing on urban kinship or neighbourhood association** below the guild — no fraternity
  membership lists, no godparent networks, no street-level mutual aid except the almshouse.
- **Beggars are named but not classified.** The 1665 Orders call them "rogues and wandering
  beggars that swarm in every place about the city"; Jusserand lists beggars among the roamers;
  but the period's own taxonomy of beggary (the counterfeit crank, the Abraham-man, licensed vs
  unlicensed) was not gathered.
