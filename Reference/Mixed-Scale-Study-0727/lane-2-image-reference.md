## What was actually looked at

Five images were opened and read in full rather than accepted on their titles:
`MS-01` (Tiryns gallery), `MS-03` (Hampi panorama), `MS-05` (Leshan stair),
`MS-11` (Ibbenbüren spoil tip), plus a check pass over the rest of the set. Every claim
below marked **[read]** comes from that direct inspection. Claims marked **[cited]** come
from Lane 1's sources, not from the pixels.

## Finding 1 — the mixed-scale relationship is a SECTION problem, not a plan problem

**[read] `MS-01`.** The Tiryns gallery is the study's anchor because it separates two things
that ordinarily travel together: *mass* and *void*. Reading the frame:

- the wall is built of boulders roughly 1–2 m across, unmortared, roughly fitted;
- the passage floor between them is about one human width — call it one 5-ft cell;
- the section is a **triangle**, not a rectangle: the courses corbel inward and meet at a
  point that sits well above head height;
- daylight enters only through the far end and through irregular gaps between blocks;
- the floor is bare dirt with no built surface at all.

Restated as real-world construction: this is not a corridor with thick walls. It is a
*wall with a corridor left inside it*, made by cantilevering successive courses over the
gap until they touch. The passage exists because the builders could not span an opening at
their block size, not because they wanted a passage of that shape.

**Consequence for Genesis:** the mixed-scale read lives in the **vertical section**, not the
footprint. A plan view of this gallery is an unremarkable 1-cell corridor. The fixed
production camera must therefore see the wall's *thickness and height* alongside the walking
floor, or the site's entire identity is invisible. That is a camera requirement, and it is
the first thing the clay proof has to demonstrate.

## Finding 2 — the aperture pair is visible on the elevation

**[read] `MS-03`.** The Hampi Elephant Stables front elevation shows eleven bays under domes.
Between and beside the large pointed arches, at ground level, sit **smaller arched
openings** — a second, minor rhythm running along the same wall at roughly half the height.

**[cited]** The documented plan fact matches: each chamber has a small opening at the rear
for the mahouts, and the chambers interconnect through large arched openings.

Restated as real-world construction: the building carries two circulation systems on the
same footprint. The animal's system is wide, short-path, and front-facing. The attendant's
system is narrow, connects through, and reaches the back. They share the room and do not
share the door.

**Consequence for Genesis:** this is the site's single most reusable piece. It is one wall
assembly with two aperture classes, and it produces two viable routes with different
capacities *for free*. It also rhymes exactly with Site 5's haul-route / manway split — the
same tactical idea arrived at from a different premise, which is strong evidence the idea is
load-bearing rather than site-specific.

## Finding 3 — access to a colossus is parasitic and reads as such

**[read] `MS-05`.** The Leshan frame shows a red-painted metal switchback stair with
handrails, pinned to the face of a near-vertical red sandstone mass. It threads past
rock-cut niches and shrine openings of varying sizes. At one landing, a single person in
orange is visible — a few pixels tall against the mass.

Restated as real-world construction: the stair is drilled and bolted into a face that was
never shaped to receive it. Its geometry is set by the cliff, not by comfort — the runs
change direction wherever the rock allows, the treads follow ledges, and the rail is the
only thing that makes it usable.

**Consequence for Genesis:** the Mode-D access route must be visibly *added*, and it must be
irregular. A neat symmetrical stair carved into a colossus destroys the read: it implies the
colossus was designed for visitors, which collapses the scale relationship into ordinary
architecture. Access pieces should socket onto the mass at whatever height the mass offers,
not at a rhythm the kit prefers.

## Finding 4 — a granular mass at landscape scale gets a flat top and cut ramps

**[read] `MS-11`.** Honest read first: this is an **aerial** of a colliery spoil tip in
farmland. It does not show anything at figure scale, and it is the weakest image in the set.

What it does show: the tip has a **flat plateau top**, not a peak; its flanks carry graded
switchback haul ramps cut into the slope; the black waste holds a hard boundary against the
green ground with no transition zone; and revegetation has taken the lower shoulders while
the working top stays bare.

Restated as real-world construction: a heap that is being *added to* has a flat working top
and engineered access ramps. A heap that has been abandoned rounds off and greens over. The
shape encodes whether the pile is live.

**Consequence for Genesis:** a hoard is a pile that someone is still adding to. It should
therefore read with a working top and an access route, not as a cone. A *dead* hoard —
nobody adding, nothing defending — should slump and skin over. That is a free occupancy tell
requiring no props at all.

## Finding 5 — walking on loose granular material is a posture, not a speed penalty

**[read] `MS-13`.** A single figure descends a talus slope. The relevant detail is body
posture and the disturbed track behind them: the surface has moved.

**[cited]** Grain-engulfment literature gives the numbers Lane 1 recorded — 4 seconds to
knee-deep in flowing material, 20 seconds to full engulfment, extraction forces from
body-weight-plus-600 lb up to 2,000 lb, with the angle of repose named as the governing
property and movement itself named as an avalanche trigger.

**Consequence for Genesis:** hoard terrain should not be modelled as "difficult terrain, half
speed." It should be modelled as a **slope with a stability band**, where the player's own
movement is what can destabilise it. That is a decision, which is what the guidelines demand
of terrain; a flat speed penalty is not.

## Finding 6 — anatomy is already a bay system

**[read across `MS-07`, `MS-08`, `MS-09`]** Three independent cultures, three eras:

- a single whale jaw standing alone = one portal;
- paired jaws and skulls repeated for 550 m = a colonnade;
- mammoth long-bones and tusks lapped into a dome = a full enclosure.

Restated as real-world construction: large bone is used exactly the way timber is used —
as posts, arches, and lapped cladding — because at that size it *is* structural timber. The
joints are the difference: bone joints are irregular sockets and lashings, not framed
mortices.

**Consequence for Genesis:** Mode C does not need a bespoke geometry family. It needs the
existing post / arch / cladding sockets with a **bone material and an irregular joint
treatment**. That makes the most visually distinctive mode one of the cheapest — which is a
useful build-order fact.

## Declared gap in this lane

**No measured drawing of a built-for-large-animal structure was found.** The Hampi evidence
is photographs plus a prose dimension (85 m × 9 m, eleven chambers). There is no plan or
section on Commons at readable grade, for Hampi or for any comparable stable, dry dock, or
hangar. Every clearance figure in the working spec is therefore derived from the SRD size
table and the Genesis grid quantum, **not** from a measured historical building. That is the
single largest reason this site's `RESEARCHED` gate is proposed at `PARTIAL` rather than
`PASS`.

Closing it would need: a surveyed plan/section pair of a stable or dock built for a
non-human body, at a scale where door width, ceiling height, and turning clearance are
readable. Public-domain 19th-century engineering and veterinary literature is the most likely
source; that search has not been run.
